## CHAPTER VI

Afterwards, when he remembered that moment, it all appeared to Raskolnikov like this.

The noise heard behind the door suddenly grew quickly louder, and the door opened a little.

“What is it?” Porfiry Petrovich shouted in vexation. “I gave orders...”

For a moment there was no answer, but it was evident that there were several people behind the door, and that someone seemed to be being pushed back.

“But what is going on there?” Porfiry Petrovich repeated in alarm.

“They’ve brought the prisoner, Nikolai,” someone’s voice was heard.

“Not now! Away! Wait!.. Why has he got in here! What disorder!” shouted Porfiry, rushing to the door.

“But he...” the same voice began again, and suddenly broke off.

For two seconds, no more, a real struggle went on; then suddenly someone seemed to push someone else away with force, and right after that a very pale man stepped straight into Porfiry Petrovich’s study.

The man’s appearance at first sight was very strange. He was looking straight ahead of him, but as if seeing no one. Resolve flashed in his eyes, but at the same time a deathly pallor covered his face, as if he were being led to execution. His lips, gone quite white, were trembling slightly.

He was still very young, dressed like a common man, of middle height, lean, his hair cut round in the peasant way, with thin, as it were dry, features. The man he had unexpectedly pushed away rushed into the room after him first and managed to seize him by the shoulder: it was the guard; but Nikolai jerked his arm and broke free of him once more.

Several curious people crowded in the doorway. Some of them were trying to get in. All that has been described happened almost in one instant.

“Away, it’s too early! Wait until you are called!.. Why was he brought in before time?” muttered Porfiry Petrovich in extreme vexation, as if thrown off his stride. But Nikolai suddenly went down on his knees.

“What are you doing?” Porfiry shouted in amazement.

“I am guilty! It is my sin! I am the murderer!” Nikolai said suddenly, as if somewhat out of breath, but in a fairly loud voice.

The silence lasted about ten seconds, as if everyone had been struck dumb; even the guard recoiled and no longer came up to Nikolai, but retreated mechanically to the door and stood there motionless.

“What is this?” cried Porfiry Petrovich, coming out of his momentary stupor.

“I... am the murderer...” Nikolai repeated, after a tiny pause.

“How... you... How... Whom did you kill?”

Porfiry Petrovich was obviously at a loss.

Nikolai paused a tiny bit again.

“Alyona Ivanovna and her sister, Lizaveta Ivanovna, I... killed... with an axe. A darkness came over me...” he added suddenly, and was silent again. He was still on his knees.

Porfiry Petrovich stood for a few moments as if thinking it over, but suddenly started up again and waved his arms at the uninvited witnesses. They vanished at once, and the door was shut. Then he looked at Raskolnikov, who was standing in the corner and staring wildly at Nikolai, and started towards him, but suddenly stopped, looked at him, at once moved his eyes to Nikolai, then back to Raskolnikov, then again to Nikolai, and suddenly, as if carried away, fell on Nikolai again.

“Why are you running ahead of me with your darkness?” he shouted at him almost angrily. “I haven’t asked you yet whether a darkness came over you or not... speak: did you kill them?”

“I am the murderer... I am making my statement...” said Nikolai.

“E-eh! What did you kill them with?”

“With an axe. I had it ready.”

“Eh, he’s in a hurry! Alone?”

Nikolai did not understand the question.

“Did you kill them alone?”

“Alone. And Mitka is innocent and had no part in any of it.”

“Don’t be in a hurry with Mitka! E-eh!”

“Then how, well, how did you run down the stairs that time? The caretakers met the two of you, didn’t they?”

“That was to throw them off... then... I ran with Mitka,” Nikolai answered, as if in a hurry and as if he had prepared it in advance.

“Well, so it is!” Porfiry cried angrily. “He is saying words that aren’t his own!” he muttered as if to himself, and suddenly caught sight of Raskolnikov again.

He had evidently been so carried away with Nikolai that for a moment he had even forgotten about Raskolnikov. Now he suddenly recollected himself, and was even embarrassed...

“Rodion Romanovich, my dear fellow! Forgive me, sir,” he rushed to him, “this won’t do, sir; please, sir... there is nothing for you here... I myself... you see what surprises!.. please, sir!..”

And taking him by the arm, he pointed him to the door.

“You didn’t expect this, it seems?” said Raskolnikov, who of course did not yet understand anything clearly, but had already managed to take heart greatly.

“And you didn’t expect it either, my dear fellow. Look how your little hand is trembling! he-he!”

“And you are trembling too, Porfiry Petrovich.”

“And I am trembling, sir; I didn’t expect it, sir!..”

They were already standing in the doorway. Porfiry was waiting impatiently for Raskolnikov to pass.

“And so you won’t show me the little surprise after all?” Raskolnikov said suddenly.

“He says that, and his own little teeth are still chattering in his mouth, he-he! You are an ironical man! Well, sir, until we meet again, sir.”

“For my part, I’ll say goodbye!”

“As God wills, sir, as God wills!” muttered Porfiry, with a smile that was somehow twisted.

As he passed through the office, Raskolnikov noticed that many people looked at him intently. In the hall, in the crowd, he managed to make out both caretakers from that house, whom he had called that night to go to the police officer. They were standing there waiting for something. But just as he came out onto the stairs, he suddenly heard Porfiry Petrovich’s voice behind him again. Turning round, he saw that he was catching up with him, all out of breath.

“One little word, Rodion Romanovich; as for all the rest of this, as God wills, but all the same I shall have to ask you about one or two things in due form, sir... so we shall see each other again, yes, sir.”

And Porfiry stopped in front of him with a smile.

“Yes, sir,” he added once more.

One might have supposed that he wanted to say something more, but somehow it would not come out.

“And you must forgive me, Porfiry Petrovich, about earlier... I lost my temper,” Raskolnikov began, now completely cheered, to the point of an irresistible wish to show off.

“Not at all, sir, not at all...” Porfiry took up almost joyfully. “I myself, sir... I have a venomous character, I confess, I confess! But we shall see each other, sir. If God wills, we shall see a great deal of each other, sir!..”

“And come to know each other finally?” Raskolnikov took up.

“And come to know each other finally,” Porfiry Petrovich agreed, and narrowing his eyes he looked at him very seriously. “Off to a name-day party now, sir?”

“To a funeral, sir.”

“Yes, of course, to a funeral! Take care of your health, your health, sir...”

“And I really don’t know what to wish you, for my part!” Raskolnikov took up; he had already begun to go down the stairs, but suddenly turned back to Porfiry again. “I would wish you great success, but you see what a comical post yours is!”

“Why comical, sir?” Porfiry Petrovich at once pricked up his ears; he too had turned to go.

“Why, of course: that poor Mikolka, how you must have tormented and tortured him, psychologically, in your own way, until he confessed; day and night, I suppose, you were proving to him: ‘You are the murderer, you are the murderer...’; and now that he has confessed, you will begin pulling him to pieces again, bone by bone: ‘You’re lying,’ you’ll say, ‘you are not the murderer! You couldn’t have been! These are not your own words!’ Well, after that, how can your post not be comical?”

“He-he-he! So you noticed that I said just now to Nikolai that he was ‘saying words that aren’t his own’?”

“How could I not notice?”

“He-he! Witty, witty, sir. You notice everything! A real playful mind, sir! And you strike the most comical string... he-he! It was Gogol, among writers, they say, who had that trait in the highest degree?”

“Yes, Gogol.”

“Yes, sir, Gogol, sir... until our most pleasant next meeting, sir.”

“Until our most pleasant next meeting...”

Raskolnikov went straight home. He was so thrown and confused that, having come home and thrown himself on the sofa, he sat for a quarter of an hour only resting and trying to collect his thoughts at least somewhat. He did not even attempt to reason about Nikolai: he felt that he was stunned; that in Nikolai’s confession there was something inexplicable, astonishing, which he could not possibly understand now. But Nikolai’s confession was a real fact. The consequences of this fact became clear to him at once: the lie could not fail to be discovered, and then they would set about him again. But at least until then he was free, and he must certainly do something for himself, because the danger was inevitable.

But, then, to what degree? The situation was beginning to clear. Recalling roughly, as a whole, his whole scene with Porfiry earlier, he could not help shuddering with horror once more. Of course he did not yet know all Porfiry’s purposes, could not grasp all his calculations of earlier. But part of the game had been revealed, and surely no one could understand better than he how terrible for him this “move” in Porfiry’s game had been. A little more, and he might have given himself away completely, in fact. Knowing the morbidity of his character, and having grasped and seen through it correctly at first sight, Porfiry had acted, though too decisively, almost surely. There was no denying that Raskolnikov had already compromised himself too much earlier, but it had still not come to facts; it was all still only relative. But was he, however, was he understanding all this correctly now? Wasn’t he mistaken? What result exactly had Porfiry been driving at today? Had he really had something prepared today? And what exactly? Had he really been waiting for something or not? How exactly would they have parted today, if the unexpected catastrophe through Nikolai had not come about?

Porfiry had shown almost his whole game; of course he had taken a risk, but he had shown it, and (so it all seemed to Raskolnikov) if Porfiry really had had anything more, he would have shown that too. What was this “surprise”? A mockery? Did it mean something or not? Could there be hidden under it even anything like a fact, a positive accusation? Yesterday’s man? Where had he vanished to? Where had he been today? For if Porfiry had anything positive, it was surely connected with yesterday’s man...

He sat on the sofa with his head hanging down, his elbows on his knees and his face in his hands. A nervous trembling still went on through his whole body. At last he stood up, took his cap, thought, and went towards the door.

He somehow had a feeling that, at least for today, he could almost certainly consider himself safe. Suddenly he felt almost joy in his heart: he wanted to go quickly to Katerina Ivanovna’s. He was too late for the funeral, of course, but he would be in time for the memorial meal, and there, now, he would see Sonya.

He stopped, thought, and a sickly smile forced itself onto his lips.

“Today! Today!” he repeated to himself. “Yes, this very day! It must be so...”

Just as he was about to open the door, it suddenly began to open by itself. He trembled and sprang back. The door opened slowly and quietly, and suddenly a figure appeared: yesterday’s man from under the ground.

The man stopped on the threshold, looked silently at Raskolnikov and took one step into the room. He was exactly as yesterday, the same figure, dressed the same way, but a great change had taken place in his face and in his look: he looked somehow sorrowful now, and after standing a little, he sighed deeply. All he needed was to put his palm to his cheek and tilt his head to one side to look completely like a peasant woman.

“What do you want?” asked Raskolnikov, numb with dread.

The man was silent, and suddenly bowed to him deeply, almost to the ground. At least he touched the ground with a finger of his right hand.

“What are you doing?” cried Raskolnikov.

“I am guilty,” the man said quietly.

“Of what?”

“Of wicked thoughts.”

They both looked at each other.

“I felt aggrieved. When you were pleased to come that time, in drink perhaps, and called the caretakers to the police and asked about the blood, I felt aggrieved that they let it go for nothing and took you for a drunk. And I felt so aggrieved that I lost my sleep. And having remembered the address, we came here yesterday and asked...”

“Who came?” Raskolnikov interrupted, instantly beginning to remember.

“I did, that is; I wronged you.”

“So you are from that house?”

“Why, I was standing right there with them at the gate that time, or have you forgotten? We have our trade there, from long ago. We are furriers, tradespeople, we take work home... and most of all I felt aggrieved...”

And suddenly Raskolnikov clearly remembered the whole scene in the gateway two days before; he realised that besides the caretakers several other people had been standing there then, women too. He remembered one voice suggesting that he be taken straight to the police. He could not remember the speaker’s face and did not recognise it even now, but he remembered that he had even answered him something then, had turned towards him...

So this, then, was how all yesterday’s horror had resolved itself. Most terrible of all was to think that he really had nearly perished, nearly destroyed himself, because of such a trifling circumstance. So, apart from the renting of the flat and the talk about the blood, this man could tell nothing. So Porfiry too had nothing, nothing but this delirium, no facts except psychology, which cuts both ways, nothing positive. So, if no more facts appeared (and they must not appear any more, they must not, they must not!), then... then what could they do to him? How could they finally convict him, even if they arrested him? And so Porfiry had learned about the flat only now, only just now, and had not known until then.

“Was it you who told Porfiry today... that I had been there?” he cried, struck by a sudden idea.

“What Porfiry?”

“The investigating magistrate.”

“I told him. The caretakers didn’t go then, so I went.”

“Today?”

“I was there just a minute before you. And I heard everything, everything, how he tormented you.”

“Where? What? When?”

“Why, right there, behind his partition; I sat there the whole time.”

“What? So you were the surprise? But how could that have happened? Good heavens!”

“Seeing,” the tradesman began, “that the caretakers didn’t want to go on my word, because, they said, it was already late, and perhaps he would be angry that they hadn’t come at the time, I felt aggrieved, and lost my sleep, and began to find things out. And having found out yesterday, I went today. The first time I came, he wasn’t there. I came an hour later, they wouldn’t admit me; the third time I came, they let me in. I began to report everything to him, how it was, and he began leaping about the room and beating his breast with his fist: ‘What are you doing to me, you robbers?’ he says. ‘If I had known of such a thing, I would have had him fetched under guard!’ Then he ran out, called someone and began talking with him in the corner, and then came back to me, and began asking and scolding. And he reproached me a lot; and I reported everything to him, and said that you didn’t dare answer me anything to my words yesterday and that you didn’t recognise me. And then he began running about again, and kept beating his breast, and was angry, and ran about, and when you were announced, ‘Well,’ he says, ‘get behind the partition, sit there for now, don’t move, whatever you hear,’ and he brought me a chair there himself and locked me in; ‘perhaps,’ he says, ‘I shall ask you.’ And when they brought Nikolai, then, after you, he let me out: ‘I shall send for you again,’ he says, ‘and question you again...’”

“And did he question Nikolai in front of you?”

“As soon as he let you out, he let me out at once too, and began questioning Nikolai.”

The tradesman stopped and suddenly bowed again, touching the floor with his finger.

“Forgive me for the slander and for my wickedness.”

“God will forgive,” answered Raskolnikov, and as soon as he had said it, the tradesman bowed to him, but not to the ground this time, only from the waist, slowly turned and went out of the room. “Everything cuts both ways, now everything cuts both ways,” Raskolnikov repeated, and went out of the room more cheerful than ever.

“Now we shall fight on,” he said with a malicious smile as he went down the stairs. The malice, though, was directed at himself: he remembered his “faint-heartedness” with contempt and shame.
