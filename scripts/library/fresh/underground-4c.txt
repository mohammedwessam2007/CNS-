## VIII

I did not soon agree to admit this truth, however. Waking the next morning after a few hours of deep, leaden sleep and at once recalling the whole of the day before, I was even amazed at my sentimentality with Liza, at all those “horrors and pities of yesterday”. “What an old woman’s fit of nerves to come over me, pah!” I decided. “And why on earth did I push my address on her? What if she comes? Oh well, let her come, perhaps; it doesn’t matter...” But obviously that was not the main and most important business now: I had to hurry and, at all costs, save my reputation in the eyes of Zverkov and Simonov as quickly as possible. That was the main business. And I even forgot about Liza altogether that morning in all my fuss.

First of all I had to repay yesterday’s debt to Simonov at once. I resolved on a desperate measure: to borrow a whole fifteen roubles from Anton Antonovich. As if on purpose, he was in the best of moods that morning and gave it to me at once, at the first request. I was so delighted at this that, signing the receipt, I told him carelessly, with a sort of dashing air, that yesterday “we had a bit of a spree with some friends at the Hôtel de Paris; we were seeing off a comrade, a childhood friend, one might even say; and, you know, he’s a great one for a spree, spoiled; of course, from a good family, a considerable fortune, a brilliant career, witty, charming, carries on affairs with certain ladies, you understand; we drank an extra ‘half dozen’ and...” And it went off fine; I said all this very easily, casually and complacently.

When I got home I immediately wrote to Simonov.

To this day I admire, remembering it, the truly gentlemanly, good-natured, frank tone of my letter. Deftly and nobly, and above all without a superfluous word, I blamed myself for everything. I excused myself, “if I may still be permitted to excuse myself”, on the grounds that, being completely unused to wine, I had got drunk on the first glass, which (I claimed) I had drunk before they came, while waiting for them at the Hôtel de Paris from five to six. I begged pardon chiefly of Simonov; and I asked him to pass on my explanations to all the others, especially Zverkov, whom, “as I remember as if in a dream”, I seem to have insulted. I added that I would have called on them all myself, but my head ached, and above all I was ashamed. I was particularly pleased with the “certain lightness”, almost carelessness (perfectly decent, though), that suddenly showed itself in my pen and, better than all possible reasons, made them understand at once that I took a rather independent view of “all that nastiness yesterday”; that I was not at all, not in the least, knocked flat, as you gentlemen probably think, but on the contrary looked at it as a calmly self-respecting gentleman should. What’s past is past, as they say; a fine fellow need not be ashamed of it.

“Why, there’s even a sort of marquis’s playfulness in it!” I admired, rereading the note. “And it all comes from being a cultured and educated man! Others in my place wouldn’t know how to get out of it, but I have wriggled out and am back on top, and all because I am ‘a cultured and educated man of our time’. And indeed, perhaps it all really did come from the wine yesterday. Hm... well, no, not from the wine. I didn’t drink any vodka at all between five and six, when I was waiting for them. I lied to Simonov; I lied shamelessly; and I’m not ashamed even now...

“But to hell with it! The main thing is that I’ve got out of it.”

I put six roubles in the letter, sealed it, and persuaded Apollon to take it to Simonov. When Apollon learned there was money in the letter, he became more respectful and agreed to go. Towards evening I went out for a walk. My head was still aching and spinning from the day before. But the more the evening came on and the thicker the dusk grew, the more my impressions changed and became confused, and my thoughts after them. Something inside me, deep in my heart and conscience, would not die, did not want to die, and showed itself in a burning anguish. I wandered mostly through the most crowded commercial streets, the Meshchanskayas, Sadovaya, by the Yusupov Garden. I always particularly liked walking along these streets at dusk, precisely when the crowd of all sorts of passers-by grows thicker there, tradesmen and workmen with faces anxious to the point of anger, going home from their day’s earnings. It was precisely this kopeck-counting bustle, this impudent prosiness, that I liked. This time all this jostling in the streets irritated me still more. I could not get control of myself, could not find the thread. Something kept rising and rising in my soul, painfully, and would not be quieted. I came home completely upset. It was as if some crime lay on my soul.

I was constantly tormented by the thought that Liza would come. What was strange to me was that of all yesterday’s memories, the memory of her tormented me in some special way, somehow quite apart from the rest. Everything else I had already managed to forget by evening; I had waved it away and was still completely satisfied with my letter to Simonov. But here I was somehow no longer satisfied. It was as if Liza alone were tormenting me. “What if she comes?” I thought ceaselessly. “Well, no matter, let her come. Hm. It’s bad enough, for one thing, that she’ll see how I live, for example. Yesterday I appeared before her as such a... hero... and now, hm! It is bad, though, that I have let myself go so much. The flat is simply squalid. And I made up my mind to go to dinner yesterday in clothes like that! And my oilcloth sofa with the stuffing sticking out! And my dressing gown that won’t close round me! What rags... And she will see all this; and she will see Apollon. That brute is sure to insult her. He’ll pick on her so as to be rude to me. And I, of course, as usual, will lose my nerve, start mincing in front of her, wrapping the skirts of my dressing gown round me, smiling, lying. Ugh, how vile! And that isn’t even the main vileness! There is something more important here, nastier, meaner! Yes, meaner! And again, again to put on that dishonest, lying mask!..”

At this thought I simply flared up:

“Why dishonest? What do you mean, dishonest? I spoke sincerely yesterday. I remember there was real feeling in me too. I wanted precisely to awaken noble feelings in her... if she cried, that is good; it will have a beneficial effect...”

But all the same I could not calm down.

All that evening, even after I had come home, even after nine o’clock, when by my reckoning Liza could not possibly come, I still kept imagining her, and, above all, I kept remembering her in one and the same position. One moment out of all of yesterday came back to me with particular vividness: when I lit up the room with the match and saw her pale, twisted face with its tormented look. And what a pitiful, what an unnatural, what a twisted smile she had at that moment! But I did not know then that fifteen years later I would still picture Liza with precisely that pitiful, twisted, useless smile she had at that moment.

The next day I was again ready to consider it all nonsense, overwrought nerves, and above all exaggeration. I was always aware of this weak spot of mine and was sometimes very afraid of it: “I exaggerate everything; that is where I limp,” I repeated to myself every hour. But all the same, “all the same, Liza will perhaps come”: that was the refrain with which all my reasoning ended in those days. I was so anxious that I sometimes flew into a rage. “She’ll come! She’s sure to come!” I exclaimed, running about the room. “If not today, then tomorrow; she’ll find me! That is the accursed romanticism of all these pure hearts! Oh, the vileness, the stupidity, the narrowness of these ‘filthy sentimental souls’! How can she not understand, how can she possibly not understand?..” But here I would stop myself, and even in great confusion.

“And how few, how few words were needed,” I thought in passing, “how little idyll was needed (and an affected, bookish, made-up idyll at that) to turn a whole human soul my way at once. That’s virginity for you! That’s freshness of soil for you!”

Sometimes the thought came to me of going to her myself, to “tell her everything” and beg her not to come to me. But at this thought such fury rose in me that I think I would have crushed that “damned” Liza if she had suddenly turned up beside me; I would have insulted her, spat at her, driven her out, struck her!

One day passed, however, a second, a third: she did not come, and I began to calm down. I felt especially encouraged and cheerful after nine o’clock; I even began sometimes to dream, and rather sweetly: “I, for example, save Liza, precisely by her coming to see me and my talking to her... I develop her, educate her. At last I notice that she loves me, loves me passionately. I pretend not to understand (I don’t know why I pretend, by the way; for effect, probably). At last, all confused, beautiful, trembling and sobbing, she throws herself at my feet and says that I am her saviour and that she loves me more than anything in the world. I am amazed, but... ‘Liza,’ I say, ‘can you really think I had not noticed your love? I saw everything, I guessed, but I did not dare to lay claim to your heart first, because I had influence over you and was afraid that, out of gratitude, you would deliberately force yourself to respond to my love, would call up in yourself a feeling that perhaps was not there; and I did not want that, because that would be... despotism... It would be indelicate’ (well, in short, here I would get carried away into some European, George-Sandish, inexpressibly noble refinement...). ‘But now, now you are mine; you are my creation; you are pure, beautiful; you are my beautiful wife.’ Then we begin to live happily ever after, go abroad, and so on and so on.” In short, I felt mean myself, and I would end by sticking my tongue out at myself.

“And they won’t let her out, the ‘hussy’!” I thought. “I don’t think they let them out walking very much, especially in the evening.” (For some reason I was certain that she must come in the evening, and at seven o’clock precisely.) “But then, she said she was not yet entirely in bondage there, that she was on a special footing; so, hm! Damn it, she’ll come; she’s sure to come!”

It was a good thing that Apollon distracted me at that time with his rudeness. He drove me past the last of my patience! He was my plague, a scourge sent upon me by Providence. We had been bickering constantly for several years on end, and I hated him. My God, how I hated him! I don’t think I have ever hated anyone in my life as I hated him, especially at certain moments. He was an elderly, dignified man who did a little tailoring on the side. But for some unknown reason he despised me, even beyond all measure, and looked down on me unbearably. However, he looked down on everyone. You had only to look at that flaxen, smoothly combed head, at the quiff he fluffed up on his forehead and greased with vegetable oil, at that solid mouth, always pursed into a little V, and you already felt before you a being who had never doubted himself. He was a pedant of the highest degree, the most enormous pedant of any I have ever met on earth; and with a vanity befitting perhaps only Alexander the Great. He was in love with every one of his buttons, every one of his fingernails: absolutely in love; he looked it! He treated me quite despotically, spoke to me extremely little, and if he happened to glance at me, he looked with a firm, majestically self-assured and constantly mocking look that sometimes drove me into a frenzy. He performed his duties with an air as if he were doing me the greatest favour. However, he did almost absolutely nothing for me, and did not even consider himself obliged to do anything. There could be no doubt that he considered me the greatest fool in the whole world, and if he “kept me on”, it was solely because he could get his wages from me every month. He agreed to “do nothing” for me for seven roubles a month. Many sins will be forgiven me on his account. At times it came to such hatred that his walk alone would almost throw me into convulsions. But what I found most loathsome of all was his lisp. His tongue was a little longer than it should be, or something of the kind, and so he constantly lisped and slobbered, and I believe he was terribly proud of it, imagining that it gave him an extraordinary amount of dignity. He spoke softly, measuredly, with his hands behind his back and his eyes on the ground. He infuriated me especially when he began reading the Psalter behind his partition. I fought many battles over that reading. But he was terribly fond of reading in the evenings, in a soft, even, sing-song voice, just as if over the dead. It is curious that that is how he ended up: he now hires himself out to read the Psalter over the dead, and also exterminates rats and makes boot polish. But in those days I could not get rid of him; it was as if he were chemically combined with my existence. Besides, he would not have agreed to leave me for anything. I could not live in furnished rooms: my flat was my private residence, my shell, my case, in which I hid from all mankind; and Apollon, the devil knows why, seemed to me to belong to this flat, and for seven whole years I could not drive him out.

To hold back his wages, for example, even for two or three days, was impossible. He would have made such a fuss that I would not have known where to hide. But in those days I was so angry with everyone that I decided, for some reason and with some purpose, to punish Apollon and not pay him his wages for another two weeks. I had long been meaning to do this, for two years or so, solely to prove to him that he must not dare to put on such airs with me, and that if I wanted to, I could always withhold his wages. I resolved not to tell him about it, and even to keep silent on purpose, so as to conquer his pride and force him to be the first to speak of his wages. Then I would take all seven roubles out of the drawer, show him that I had them and had put them aside on purpose, but that I “don’t want to, don’t want to, simply don’t want to pay him his wages; don’t want to, because that is what I want”; because such is “my will as master”; because he is disrespectful, because he is a lout; but that if he asked respectfully, I might relent and give it to him; otherwise he could wait another two weeks, three weeks, a whole month...

But however angry I was, he won all the same. I did not hold out even four days. He began as he always began in such cases, for such cases had happened before and been tried (and, I may note, I knew all this beforehand; I knew his vile tactics by heart): namely, he would begin by fixing an extremely stern look on me and not taking it off me for several minutes at a time, especially when he let me in or saw me out of the house. If, for example, I held out and pretended not to notice these looks, he would proceed, still in silence, to further tortures. Suddenly, for no reason at all, he would come quietly and smoothly into my room when I was walking about or reading, stop at the door, put one hand behind his back, set one foot forward, and fix his look on me, no longer merely stern but utterly contemptuous. If I suddenly asked him what he wanted, he would not answer, but go on staring at me for a few more seconds; then, pressing his lips together in a special way, with a significant air, he would slowly turn on the spot and slowly go back to his room. Two hours later he would suddenly come out again and appear before me in the same way. Sometimes in my fury I no longer asked him what he wanted, but simply raised my head sharply and imperiously myself and began to stare back at him. So we would stare at each other for about two minutes; at last he would turn, slowly and importantly, and go away again for two hours.

If I still did not come to my senses even then, and went on rebelling, he would suddenly begin to sigh as he looked at me, to sigh long and deeply, as if measuring with that one sigh the whole depth of my moral fall; and of course it ended at last with his winning completely: I raged and shouted, but was still forced to do what the matter was about.

This time, as soon as the usual manoeuvres of “stern looks” began, I at once lost my temper and fell on him in a fury. I was too irritated as it was.

“Stop!” I shouted in a frenzy, as he was slowly and silently turning, with one hand behind his back, to go to his room. “Stop! Come back, come back, I tell you!” And I must have bellowed so unnaturally that he turned and even began to examine me with some surprise. But he went on saying not a word, and that was what infuriated me.

“How dare you come into my room without asking and look at me like that? Answer!”

But after looking at me calmly for half a minute, he began to turn again.

“Stop!” I roared, running up to him. “Don’t move! So. Now answer: what did you come in to look at?”

“If you have anything to order me now, it is my business to carry it out,” he answered, again after a pause, softly and measuredly lisping, raising his eyebrows and calmly tilting his head from one shoulder to the other; and all this with a terrifying calm.

“That’s not what I’m asking you, not that, you executioner!” I shouted, shaking with rage. “I’ll tell you myself, executioner, why you come in here: you see that I’m not paying you your wages, you won’t bow down yourself, out of pride, to ask for them; and so you come in with your stupid looks to punish me, to torment me, and you don’t sus-s-spect, executioner, how stupid it is, stupid, stupid, stupid, stupid!”

He began to turn silently again, but I seized hold of him.

“Listen,” I shouted at him. “Here is the money, you see; here it is!” (I took it out of the table drawer.) “All seven roubles; but you won’t get it, you won’t ge-e-et it until you come respectfully, with your head bowed, and beg my pardon. Do you hear!”

“That cannot be!” he answered with a kind of unnatural self-assurance.

“It will be!” I shouted. “I give you my word of honour, it will be!”

“And there’s nothing I need to beg your pardon for,” he went on, as if not noticing my shouts at all, “because you called me an ‘executioner’, for which I can always lodge a complaint against you for insult at the police station.”

“Go! Complain!” I roared. “Go now, this minute, this second! But you are an executioner all the same! An executioner! An executioner!” But he only looked at me, then turned and, no longer listening to my shouts calling him back, went smoothly to his room without looking round.

“If it weren’t for Liza, none of this would have happened!” I decided to myself. Then, after standing for a minute, I went myself, importantly and solemnly, but with my heart beating slowly and hard, behind his screen.

“Apollon!” I said quietly and deliberately, but out of breath. “Go at once, without the least delay, and fetch the district police officer!”

He had meanwhile sat down at his table, put on his spectacles and taken up some sewing. But hearing my order, he suddenly snorted with laughter.

“Go now, this minute! Go, or you can’t imagine what will happen!”

“You really aren’t in your right mind,” he remarked, without even raising his head, lisping just as slowly and going on threading his needle. “And whoever heard of a man going to the authorities against himself? And as for frightening me, you’re only straining yourself for nothing, because nothing will happen.”

“Go!” I shrieked, seizing him by the shoulder. I felt that in a moment I would hit him.

But I did not even hear the door from the passage open quietly and slowly at that moment, and a figure come in, stop, and begin to look at us in bewilderment. I looked, nearly died of shame, and rushed into my room. There, seizing my hair with both hands, I leaned my head against the wall and froze in that position.

About two minutes later I heard Apollon’s slow steps.

“There’s some woman asking for you,” he said, looking at me particularly sternly; then he stood aside and let in... Liza. He did not want to go away, and looked at us mockingly.

“Go away! Go away!” I commanded him, losing my head. At that moment my clock strained itself, wheezed, and struck seven.
