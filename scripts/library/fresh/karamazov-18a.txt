## Book Seven, Chapter IV · Cana of Galilee

It was already very late by monastery hours when Alyosha came back to the hermitage; the gatekeeper let him in by a special way. It had already struck nine, the hour of general rest and quiet after a day so troubling for everyone. Alyosha timidly opened the door and went into the elder’s cell, where his coffin now stood. Apart from Father Paisy, who was reading the Gospel over the coffin alone, and the young novice Porfiry, who, worn out by last night’s conversation and today’s commotion, was asleep on the floor in the other room with his sound young sleep, there was no one in the cell. Father Paisy, though he heard Alyosha come in, did not even look his way. Alyosha turned to the right of the door, into the corner, knelt down and began to pray. His soul was overflowing, but somehow vaguely, and no one sensation stood out too strongly; on the contrary, one pushed out another in a kind of quiet, even rotation. But there was sweetness in his heart, and, strangely, Alyosha was not surprised at it. Again he saw before him this coffin, this dead man so precious to him, covered all round; but there was no weeping, aching, tormenting pity in his soul as there had been that morning. Coming in just now, he had fallen down before the coffin as before a holy thing; but joy, joy was shining in his mind and in his heart. One window of the cell was open; the air was fresh and coolish. “So the odour has grown stronger, if they decided to open the window,” thought Alyosha. But even this thought of the odour of corruption, which only a little while ago had seemed to him so terrible and inglorious, did not stir in him now his earlier anguish and his earlier indignation. He began quietly to pray, but soon felt himself that he was praying almost mechanically. Fragments of thought flashed through his soul, flared up like little stars and went out again at once, giving way to others; but something whole, firm and soothing reigned in his soul, and he was aware of it himself. At times he would begin a prayer fervently; he so wanted to give thanks and to love… But having begun the prayer, he would suddenly pass to something else, fall to thinking, and forget both the prayer and what had interrupted it. He began to listen to what Father Paisy was reading, but, being very tired, little by little he began to doze…

“And the third day there was a marriage in Cana of Galilee,” Father Paisy was reading, “and the mother of Jesus was there: and both Jesus was called, and his disciples, to the marriage.”

“A marriage? What is that… a marriage…” rushed like a whirlwind through Alyosha’s mind. “She has happiness too… she has gone to a feast… No, she didn’t take a knife, she didn’t take a knife… It was only a ‘pitiful’ word… Well… pitiful words must be forgiven, without fail. Pitiful words comfort the soul… without them grief would be too heavy for people. Rakitin went off into a side street. As long as Rakitin thinks about his grievances he will always go off into a side street… But the road… the road is wide, straight, bright, crystal, and the sun is at the end of it… Eh?… What are they reading?”

“…And when they wanted wine, the mother of Jesus saith unto him, They have no wine…” Alyosha heard.

“Ah yes, I missed that, and I didn’t want to miss it; I love this passage: it is Cana of Galilee, the first miracle… Ah, that miracle, ah, that dear miracle! It was not grief but men’s joy that Christ visited when he worked his first miracle; he helped men’s joy… ‘He who loves men loves their joy too…’ The dead man used to repeat that every minute; it was one of his chief thoughts… One can’t live without joy, says Mitya… Yes, Mitya… Everything that is true and beautiful is always full of all-forgiveness; that again was what he said…”

“…Jesus saith unto her, Woman, what have I to do with thee? mine hour is not yet come. His mother saith unto the servants, Whatsoever he saith unto you, do it.”

“Do it… Joy, the joy of some poor, very poor people… Of course they were poor, if there wasn’t even enough wine for a wedding… The historians write that round the lake of Gennesaret and in all those parts the poorest population imaginable was settled then… And another great heart, of another great being who was there too, his mother, knew that he had come down then not only for his great and terrible deed, but that his heart was open to the simple, artless merrymaking of some dark, dark and unsophisticated creatures who had kindly invited him to their poor wedding. ‘Mine hour is not yet come,’ he says with a quiet smile (he surely smiled at her gently)… Indeed, was it to multiply wine at poor weddings that he came down to earth? And yet he went and did it at her request… Ah, he is reading again.”

“…Jesus saith unto them, Fill the waterpots with water. And they filled them up to the brim. And he saith unto them, Draw out now, and bear unto the governor of the feast. And they bare it. When the ruler of the feast had tasted the water that was made wine, and knew not whence it was: (but the servants which drew the water knew;) the governor of the feast called the bridegroom, and saith unto him, Every man at the beginning doth set forth good wine; and when men have well drunk, then that which is worse: but thou hast kept the good wine until now.”

“But what is this, what is this? Why is the room growing wider… Ah yes… of course, it is the marriage, the wedding… yes, of course. Here are the guests, here are the young couple sitting, and the merry crowd, and… where is the wise governor of the feast? But who is this? Who? The room has grown wider again… Who is that getting up there from the great table? What… Is he here too? But he is in the coffin… But he is here too… he has got up, he has seen me, he is coming this way… Lord!…”

Yes, he came up to him, to him, the dry little old man with fine wrinkles on his face, joyful and laughing quietly. There was no coffin now, and he was in the same clothes in which he had sat with them yesterday when the visitors had gathered round him. His face was all uncovered; his eyes were shining. How is this? So he too is at the feast, he too has been called to the marriage in Cana of Galilee…

“I too, my dear, I too am called; called and bidden,” a quiet voice sounds above him. “Why have you hidden yourself here, so that you can’t be seen?… Come and join us too.”

His voice, the voice of Elder Zosima… And how could it not be he, if he is calling? The elder raised Alyosha with his hand; Alyosha rose from his knees.

“We are making merry,” the dry little old man goes on. “We are drinking new wine, the wine of a new, great joy. Do you see how many guests there are? Here are the bridegroom and the bride, here is the wise governor of the feast, tasting the new wine. Why do you wonder at me? I gave an onion, and so I am here. And many here gave only an onion, only one little onion… What are our deeds? And you, my quiet one, you too, my gentle boy, you too were able today to give an onion to a hungry woman. Begin, my dear, begin, my gentle one, your work!… And do you see our Sun, do you see him?”

“I am afraid… I dare not look…” whispered Alyosha.

“Do not be afraid of him. He is terrible in his greatness before us, awful in his height, but infinitely merciful; out of love he has become like us and makes merry with us; he turns water into wine so that the guests’ joy may not be cut short; he is waiting for new guests, he is calling new ones unceasingly, and now for ever and ever. See, they are bringing the new wine; do you see, they are bringing the vessels…”

Something was burning in Alyosha’s heart; something suddenly filled it till it hurt; tears of rapture were breaking from his soul… He stretched out his arms, cried out and woke…

Again the coffin, the open window, and the quiet, solemn, distinct reading of the Gospel. But Alyosha was no longer listening to what was being read. Strange: he had fallen asleep on his knees, and now he was standing on his feet; and suddenly, as if torn from his place, with three firm, quick steps he went right up to the coffin. He even brushed Father Paisy with his shoulder and did not notice it. Father Paisy raised his eyes to him from the book for a moment, but at once looked away again, understanding that something strange had happened to the young man. For half a minute Alyosha looked at the coffin, at the dead man lying there covered, motionless, stretched out in the coffin, with the icon on his breast and the cowl with the eight-pointed cross on his head. Only just now he had heard his voice, and that voice was still ringing in his ears. He was still listening; he was still expecting sounds… But suddenly, turning sharply, he went out of the cell.

He did not stop even on the steps, but went quickly down. His soul, full of rapture, thirsted for freedom, space, breadth. Above him the dome of heaven, full of quiet shining stars, was spread out wide and boundless. From the zenith to the horizon the still faint Milky Way ran double. The fresh night, still to the point of motionlessness, lay over the earth. The white towers and golden domes of the cathedral gleamed against the sapphire sky. The splendid autumn flowers in the beds round the house had fallen asleep till morning. The silence of the earth seemed to merge with the silence of the heavens; the mystery of the earth touched the mystery of the stars… Alyosha stood and looked, and suddenly, as if cut down, threw himself on the earth.

He did not know why he embraced it; he did not try to understand why he so irresistibly longed to kiss it, to kiss all of it; but he kissed it, weeping, sobbing and watering it with his tears, and vowed in ecstasy to love it, to love it for ever and ever. “Water the earth with the tears of your joy and love those tears of yours…” rang in his soul. What was he weeping over? Oh, in his rapture he was weeping even over those stars that shone on him from the abyss, and “he was not ashamed of this ecstasy”. It was as if threads from all those countless worlds of God had come together at once in his soul, and it was trembling all over, “in contact with other worlds”. He wanted to forgive everyone and for everything, and to beg forgiveness, oh! not for himself, but for all, for everything and for everyone; “and others are begging for me,” rang again in his soul. But with every moment he felt plainly, almost tangibly, how something firm and unshakeable, like that vault of heaven, was entering his soul. A sort of idea, as it were, was taking possession of his mind, and now for his whole life and for ever and ever. He had fallen on the earth a weak youth, and he rose up a fighter, steadfast for the rest of his life; and he knew and felt it suddenly, in that same moment of his rapture. And never, never in all his life afterwards could Alyosha forget that moment. “Someone visited my soul in that hour,” he would say afterwards, with firm faith in his words…

Three days later he left the monastery, which was in keeping too with the word of his late elder, who had bidden him “to sojourn in the world”.
