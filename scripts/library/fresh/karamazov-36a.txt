## Book Twelve, Chapter XIV · The Peasants Stood Their Ground

So Fetyukovich ended, and the rapture of his listeners that broke out this time was irresistible as a storm. It was unthinkable now to restrain it: the women wept; many of the men wept too; even two dignitaries shed tears. The presiding judge gave way and even delayed ringing his bell: “To attack such enthusiasm would have been to attack something sacred,” as our ladies cried afterwards. The orator himself was sincerely moved. And at just such a moment our Ippolit Kirillovich rose once more “to exchange rejoinders”. He was looked at with hatred: “What? What is this? Does he dare to object still?” the ladies began to babble. But even if the ladies of the whole world had babbled, with the prosecutor’s wife herself, Ippolit Kirillovich’s spouse, at their head, it would have been impossible to restrain him at that moment. He was pale; he was shaking with agitation; his first words, his first sentences, were even unintelligible; he was gasping, articulating badly, losing the thread. He soon recovered, however. But of this second speech of his I will quote only a few sentences.

“…We are reproached with having made up novels. But what has the defence given us but novel upon novel? Only the verses were missing. Fyodor Pavlovich, waiting for his mistress, tears open the envelope and throws it on the floor. We are even told what he said on this remarkable occasion. Is that not a poem? And where is the proof that he took out the money; who heard what he said? The feeble-minded idiot Smerdyakov, transformed into some Byronic hero avenging himself on society for his illegitimacy: is that not a poem in the Byronic taste? And the son who broke into his father’s house and killed him, and at the same time did not kill him: that is not even a novel or a poem any more; it is a sphinx setting riddles which it certainly cannot solve itself. If he killed him, he killed him; but how can it be that, having killed him, he did not kill him? Who can understand that? Then we are told that our tribune is a tribune of truth and sound ideas, and from this tribune of ‘sound ideas’ there rings out, with an oath, the axiom that to call the murder of a father parricide is only a prejudice! But if parricide is a prejudice, and if every child is to question his father, ‘Father, why must I love you?’, what will become of us, what will become of the foundations of society, where will the family go? Parricide, you see, is only the ‘bogey’ of a Moscow merchant’s wife. The most precious, the most sacred precepts of the purpose and future of the Russian court are presented perversely and frivolously, merely to gain an end, to gain the acquittal of what cannot be justified. ‘Oh, crush him with mercy,’ cries the defence; and that is all the criminal wants, and tomorrow everyone will see how crushed he is! And is not the defence too modest, in asking only for the acquittal of the accused? Why not demand the founding of a scholarship in the parricide’s name, to immortalise his exploit for posterity and the younger generation? The Gospel and religion are corrected: that, they say, is all mysticism, but ours is the only true Christianity, already tested by the analysis of reason and sound ideas. And so a false likeness of Christ is set up before us! ‘With what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again,’ cries the defence, and in the same breath concludes that Christ commanded us to measure with the measure that is measured to us, and this from the tribune of truth and sound ideas! We glance into the Gospel only on the eve of our speeches, so as to show off our acquaintance with what is, after all, a rather original composition, which may come in handy and serve for a certain effect, as need requires, all as need requires! But Christ bids us precisely not to do so, to beware of doing so, because the wicked world does so; whereas we must forgive and turn the other cheek, and not measure out with the same measure with which our offenders measure to us. That is what our God taught us, and not that to forbid children to kill their fathers is a prejudice. And we will not correct from the chair of truth and sound ideas the Gospel of our God, whom the defence deigns to call only ‘the crucified lover of mankind’, in opposition to all Orthodox Russia, which cries out to him: ‘For thou art our God!…’”

At this point the presiding judge intervened and checked the speaker, who had got carried away, asking him not to exaggerate, to keep within proper bounds, and so on, and so on, as presiding judges usually say on such occasions. And the hall was restless too. The public stirred, even exclaimed in indignation. Fetyukovich did not even reply; he only mounted the platform to say, with his hand on his heart and in an injured voice, a few words full of dignity. He only touched again, lightly and mockingly, on “novels” and “psychology”, and in one place slipped in apropos: “Jupiter, you are angry; therefore you are wrong,” which provoked a numerous approving titter among the public, for Ippolit Kirillovich was not at all like Jupiter. Then, to the charge that he was supposedly allowing the younger generation to kill their fathers, Fetyukovich remarked with profound dignity that he would not even reply. As for “the false likeness of Christ”, and his not having deigned to call Christ God but only “the crucified lover of mankind”, which “is contrary to Orthodoxy and could not have been said from the tribune of truth and sound ideas”, Fetyukovich hinted at “insinuation”, and at the fact that in coming here he had at least counted on this tribune being secure from accusations “dangerous to my person as a citizen and a loyal subject…” But at these words the presiding judge checked him too, and Fetyukovich, bowing, concluded his reply, accompanied by a general approving murmur in the hall. And Ippolit Kirillovich, in the opinion of our ladies, was “crushed for ever”.

Then the accused himself was allowed to speak. Mitya stood up, but said little. He was terribly exhausted both in body and in spirit. The look of independence and strength with which he had appeared in the hall in the morning had almost disappeared. It was as if he had lived through something that day for his whole life, which had taught him and made him understand something very important that he had not understood before. His voice had grown weak; he no longer shouted as he had earlier. In his words there could be heard something new, humbled, defeated and bowed.

“What shall I say, gentlemen of the jury! My judgement has come; I feel the right hand of God upon me. The end of a dissolute man! But as I confess to God, I say to you too: ‘Of my father’s blood, no, I am not guilty!’ For the last time I repeat: ‘It was not I who killed him.’ I was dissolute, but I loved what is good. Every moment I strove to reform, and I lived like a wild beast. Thanks to the prosecutor: he told me much about myself that I did not know; but it is not true that I killed my father; the prosecutor is mistaken! Thanks to the defence too: I wept listening to him; but it is not true that I killed my father, and there was no need even to suppose it! And don’t believe the doctors: I am in my perfect mind; only my soul is heavy. If you spare me, if you let me go, I will pray for you. I will become better; I give my word, I give it before God. And if you condemn me, I will break the sword over my head myself, and having broken it, I will kiss the pieces! But spare me; do not take my God from me; I know myself: I shall murmur! My soul is heavy, gentlemen… spare me!”

He almost fell back into his seat; his voice broke; he could hardly get out the last phrase. Then the court proceeded to put the questions and began asking the parties for their conclusions. But I do not describe the details. At last the jury rose to withdraw for their deliberations. The presiding judge was very tired, and so he gave them a very weak parting word: “Be impartial; do not be influenced by the eloquent words of the defence; but still, weigh everything; remember that a great responsibility lies upon you,” and so on, and so on. The jury withdrew, and the session was adjourned. One could get up, walk about, exchange accumulated impressions, have a snack at the buffet. It was very late, already about one in the morning, but no one went home. Everyone was so tense and wrought up that there was no question of rest. Everyone was waiting with a sinking heart; though, it is true, not everyone’s heart was sinking. The ladies were only in hysterical impatience, but their hearts were calm: “An acquittal is inevitable.” They were all preparing for the effective moment of general enthusiasm. I confess that in the men’s half of the hall too there were a great many convinced of an inevitable acquittal. Some were glad, others frowned, and some simply hung their heads: they did not want an acquittal! Fetyukovich himself was firmly convinced of success. He was surrounded, received congratulations, was fawned upon.

“There are,” he said in one group, as was reported afterwards, “there are these invisible threads binding the defence to the jury. They are tied and felt already during the speech. I felt them; they exist. Our case is won; rest assured.”

“And what will our peasants say now, I wonder?” said a frowning, stout, pockmarked gentleman, a landowner from near the town, coming up to one group of gentlemen who were talking.

“But there aren’t only peasants. There are four officials.”

“Yes, the officials,” said a member of the district council, coming up.

“And do you know Nazaryev, Prokhor Ivanovich, that merchant with the medal, the juryman?”

“What of him?”

“A man of brains.”

“But he never says anything.”

“He doesn’t, and so much the better. It’s not for a Petersburg man to teach him; he could teach the whole of Petersburg himself. Twelve children, think of it!”

“But really, can they possibly not acquit him?” one of our young officials was crying in another group.

“They’ll acquit him for certain,” said a decided voice.

“It would be shameful, disgraceful not to acquit him!” the official exclaimed. “Suppose he did kill him; but there are fathers and fathers! And then, he was in such a frenzy… He really may only have swung the pestle, and the old man fell down. The only bad thing is that they dragged the lackey in. That’s simply a ridiculous episode. In the defence’s place I would have said straight out: he killed him, but he is not guilty, and the devil take you!”

“That is just what he did, only he didn’t say ‘the devil take you’.”

“No, Mikhail Semyonych, he almost did say it,” a third little voice put in.

“But, gentlemen, they acquitted an actress here in Lent who cut the throat of her lover’s lawful wife.”

“But she didn’t finish cutting it.”

“No matter, no matter, she began cutting it!”

“And what about the children, what he said about them? Magnificent!”

“Magnificent.”

“Well, and about mysticism, about mysticism, eh?”

“Oh, stop about mysticism,” someone else cried. “Think of Ippolit instead, of his fate from this day on! His prosecutor’s wife will scratch his eyes out tomorrow over Mitenka.”

“Is she here?”

“Here? If she were here, she’d have scratched them out here. She is sitting at home with toothache. He-he-he!”

“He-he-he!”

In a third group:

“And yet perhaps Mitenka will be acquitted.”

“Like as not, tomorrow he’ll smash up the whole Metropolis tavern and be drunk for ten days.”

“Oh, the devil!”

“The devil, yes, the devil; the devil was bound to be in it; where else should he be, if not here?”

“Gentlemen, eloquence, granted. But one can’t go breaking fathers’ heads with steelyards either. Otherwise where shall we end?”

“And the chariot, the chariot, remember?”

“Yes, he made a chariot out of a cart.”

“And tomorrow he’ll make a cart out of a chariot, ‘as need requires, all as need requires’.”

“Clever people they’ve become. Is there any justice in Russia, gentlemen, or is there none at all?”

But the bell rang. The jury had deliberated exactly an hour, neither more nor less. A profound silence reigned as soon as the public had sat down again. I remember how the jury came into the hall. At last! I will not give the questions point by point; I have forgotten them anyway. I remember only the answer to the first and chief question of the presiding judge, that is, “Did he kill with premeditation for the purpose of robbery?” (I do not remember the wording). Everything froze. The foreman of the jury, that very official who was the youngest of them all, pronounced loudly and clearly, in the deathly silence of the hall:

“Yes, guilty!”

And then on every point it went the same: guilty and guilty, and that without the slightest recommendation to mercy! No one had expected this; almost everyone had been sure at least of a recommendation to mercy. The deathly silence of the hall was not broken; literally everyone seemed turned to stone, both those who had longed for a conviction and those who had longed for an acquittal. But that was only for the first minutes. Then a terrible chaos arose. Among the male public many turned out to be very pleased. Some even rubbed their hands, not hiding their joy. Those who were displeased seemed crushed; they shrugged their shoulders and whispered, but as if they still had not taken it in. But, my God, what became of our ladies! I thought they would start a riot. At first they seemed not to believe their ears. And suddenly, through the whole hall, exclamations were heard: “But what is this? What is the meaning of this?” They leapt up from their seats. They must have thought that it could all be changed and done over again at once. At that instant Mitya suddenly stood up and, stretching out his hands before him, cried with a heart-rending wail:

“I swear by God and by his Last Judgement, I am not guilty of my father’s blood! Katya, I forgive you! Brothers, friends, spare the other one!”

He did not finish, but burst into sobs that filled the hall, loud and terrible, in a voice that was not his own, a new, unexpected voice that had suddenly come to him God knows from where. In the gallery above, in the farthest corner, there was a piercing woman’s scream: it was Grushenka. She had begged someone earlier and had been let into the hall again before the speeches began. Mitya was led away. The passing of sentence was put off till the next day. The whole hall rose in a commotion, but I did not wait or listen any longer. I remember only a few exclamations, on the steps, as people went out.

“He’ll get a whiff of the mines for twenty years.”

“No less.”

“Yes, sir, our peasants stood their ground.”

“And finished off our Mitenka!”

End of the Fourth and Last Part

## Epilogue · Chapter I · Plans to Save Mitya

On the fifth day after Mitya’s trial, very early in the morning, before nine o’clock, Alyosha came to Katerina Ivanovna to settle finally a certain matter of importance to them both, and having besides a message for her. She sat and talked with him in the very room in which she had once received Grushenka; and next to it, in another room, Ivan Fyodorovich lay in a fever, unconscious. Immediately after the scene in court, Katerina Ivanovna had had the sick and unconscious Ivan Fyodorovich carried to her house, disregarding all the future and inevitable talk of society and its condemnation. One of the two relatives who lived with her had left for Moscow immediately after the scene in court; the other had stayed. But even if both had left, Katerina Ivanovna would not have changed her decision and would have stayed to nurse the sick man and sit with him day and night. He was being treated by Varvinsky and Herzenstube; the Moscow doctor had gone back to Moscow, refusing to give his opinion on the possible outcome of the illness. The doctors who stayed, though they encouraged Katerina Ivanovna and Alyosha, evidently could not yet give firm hope. Alyosha visited his sick brother twice a day. But this time he had a special, most troublesome matter, and he foresaw how hard it would be for him to begin on it; and meanwhile he was in a great hurry: he had another urgent matter that same morning in another place, and he had to hurry. They had been talking for a quarter of an hour already. Katerina Ivanovna was pale, very tired, and at the same time in an extreme, morbid excitement: she sensed why, among other things, Alyosha had come to her now.

“Don’t worry about his decision,” she said to Alyosha with firm insistence. “One way or another, he will come to this way out all the same: he must escape! That unhappy man, that hero of honour and conscience (not that one, not Dmitri Fyodorovich, but the one lying behind that door, who sacrificed himself for his brother),” Katya added with flashing eyes, “told me the whole plan of the escape long ago. You know, he has already made contacts… I have told you something already… You see, it will probably happen at the third halting place from here, when the party of exiles is marched to Siberia. Oh, that is still a long way off. Ivan Fyodorovich has already been to see the head of the third halting place. Only it is not known who will be in charge of the party, and that cannot be known so far ahead. Tomorrow perhaps I will show you the whole plan in detail, which Ivan Fyodorovich left with me on the eve of the trial, in case anything happened… It was that very time when, you remember, you found us quarrelling that evening: he was already going downstairs, and I, seeing you, made him come back; do you remember? Do you know what we were quarrelling about then?”

“No, I don’t,” said Alyosha.

“Of course he hid it from you then: it was precisely about this plan of escape. He had revealed all the main points to me three days before; that was when we began to quarrel, and we quarrelled all those three days. We quarrelled because when he told me that in the event of a conviction Dmitri Fyodorovich would escape abroad together with that creature, I suddenly grew angry; I won’t tell you why; I don’t know why myself… Oh, of course I was angry then about the creature, about that creature, and precisely because she too would be escaping abroad with Dmitri!” Katerina Ivanovna suddenly exclaimed, her lips trembling with anger. “As soon as Ivan Fyodorovich saw then that I was so angry about that creature, he thought in a flash that I was jealous of Dmitri over her and that, therefore, I still went on loving Dmitri. And so our first quarrel came about. I would not give explanations; I could not ask forgiveness; it was hard for me that such a man could suspect me of my former love for that… And that, when I myself, long before, had told him straight out that I didn’t love Dmitri but loved only him! It was only from anger at that creature that I grew angry with him! Three days later, that very evening when you came in, he brought me a sealed envelope, which I was to open at once if anything happened to him. Oh, he foresaw his illness! He told me that the envelope contained the details of the escape, and that if he died or fell dangerously ill, I alone was to save Mitya. He left money with me too, almost ten thousand: those very ten thousand that the prosecutor, having learned from someone that he had sent them to be changed, mentioned in his speech. I was terribly struck all at once that Ivan Fyodorovich, still jealous of me and still convinced that I loved Mitya, had not, however, given up the idea of saving his brother, and was entrusting this business of saving him to me, to me myself! Oh, that was a sacrifice! No, you will not understand such self-sacrifice in all its fullness, Alexei Fyodorovich! I wanted to fall at his feet in reverence; but when I suddenly thought that he would take it only for my joy that Mitya was being saved (and he would certainly have thought so!), I was so irritated by the mere possibility of so unjust a thought on his part that I grew irritated again and, instead of kissing his feet, made another scene! Oh, I am unhappy! That is my character, a terrible, unhappy character! Oh, you will see yet: I shall do it, I shall bring things to the point where he too leaves me for another woman, with whom life is easier, like Dmitri; but then… no, then I shall not bear it; I shall kill myself! And when you came in then, and I called you and told him to come back, then, as he came in with you, I was so seized with anger at the hateful, contemptuous look he suddenly gave me that, you remember, I suddenly cried out to you that it was he, he alone who had convinced me that his brother Dmitri was the murderer! I slandered him on purpose, to wound him once more; he never, never assured me that his brother was the murderer; on the contrary, it was I, I myself who assured him of it! Oh, my fury is the cause of everything, everything! It was I, I who prepared that cursed scene in court! He wanted to prove to me that he was noble, and that even if I loved his brother, he would still not ruin him out of revenge and jealousy. And so he came out in court… I am the cause of everything; I alone am to blame!”

Katya had never made such confessions to Alyosha before, and he felt that she was now precisely at that degree of unbearable suffering when the proudest heart painfully crushes its pride and falls, defeated by grief. Oh, Alyosha knew another terrible cause of her present torment too, however she had hidden it from him all these days since Mitya’s conviction; but for some reason it would have been too painful for him if she had brought herself so low as to speak to him of that cause herself, now, at once. She was suffering for her “betrayal” at the trial, and Alyosha sensed that her conscience was drawing her to confess, precisely before him, before Alyosha, with tears, with shrieks, with hysterics, beating herself on the floor. But he feared that moment and wanted to spare the sufferer. All the harder became the message he had come with. He began to speak of Mitya again.

“Never mind, never mind; don’t be afraid for him!” Katya began again, stubbornly and sharply. “All that is only for the moment with him; I know him; I know that heart only too well. Be sure that he will agree to escape. And above all, it isn’t now; there will still be time for him to decide. By then Ivan Fyodorovich will have recovered and will manage everything himself, so that I shan’t have to do anything. Don’t worry, he will agree to escape. Why, he has agreed already: how can he leave his creature? And they won’t let her go to penal servitude, so how can he not escape? Above all, he is afraid of you; he is afraid you won’t approve of the escape from the moral side; but you must generously allow it to him, if your sanction is so indispensable here,” Katya added venomously. She was silent for a moment and smiled.

“He talks there,” she began again, “about some hymns, about a cross he must bear, about some duty; I remember, Ivan Fyodorovich told me a great deal about it then; and if you knew how he spoke!” Katya suddenly exclaimed with irrepressible feeling. “If you knew how he loved that unhappy man at the moment when he was telling me about him, and how he hated him, perhaps, at the same moment! And I, oh, I listened to his story and his tears then with a haughty smile! Oh, the creature! It is I who am the creature, I! It was I who gave him the fever! And that other one, the convicted one, is he ready for suffering?” Katya finished irritably. “Is such a man to suffer? Men like him never suffer!”

A sort of hatred and loathing contempt now sounded in these words. And yet it was she who had betrayed him. “Well, perhaps it is because she feels so guilty before him that she hates him at moments,” Alyosha thought to himself. He wanted it to be only “at moments”. In Katya’s last words he heard a challenge, but he did not take it up.

“That is why I asked you to come today: so that you would promise me to persuade him yourself. Or do you also think it would be dishonourable to escape, not valiant, or however it is… not Christian, perhaps?” Katya added with a still greater challenge.

“No, nothing. I will tell him everything…” Alyosha muttered. “He asks you to come to him today,” he suddenly blurted out, looking her firmly in the eyes. She started all over and drew back from him a little on the sofa.

“Me… is that possible?” she murmured, turning pale.

“It is possible, and it must be!” Alyosha began insistently, all animated. “He needs you very much, precisely now. I would not have begun on this and tormented you prematurely if it were not necessary. He is ill; he is like a madman; he keeps asking for you. He does not ask you to come to be reconciled; only that you should come and show yourself in the doorway. Much has happened to him since that day. He understands how incalculably guilty he is before you. He does not want your forgiveness: ‘I cannot be forgiven,’ he says himself; only that you should show yourself in the doorway…”

“You suddenly…” Katya murmured. “All these days I had a presentiment that you would come with this… I knew he would call for me!… It is impossible!”

“Let it be impossible, but do it. Remember, for the first time he is struck by how he insulted you, for the first time in his life; he never grasped it before so fully! He says: if she refuses to come, then ‘I shall be unhappy all my life now’. Do you hear: a convict for twenty years is still meaning to be happy; isn’t that pitiful? Think: you will be visiting a man ruined innocently,” broke from Alyosha with a challenge. “His hands are clean; there is no blood on them! For the sake of his countless future suffering, visit him now! Come; see him off into the darkness… stand in the doorway, that is all… For you must, you must do this!” Alyosha concluded, stressing the word “must” with incredible force.

“I must, but… I cannot,” Katya almost moaned. “He will look at me… I cannot.”

“Your eyes must meet. How will you live all your life if you don’t bring yourself to it now?”

“Better to suffer all my life.”

“You must come; you must come,” Alyosha stressed again inexorably.

“But why today, why now?… I can’t leave the sick man…”

“You can for a minute; it is only a minute. If you don’t come, he will be in a fever by night. I would not tell you an untruth; have pity!”

“Have pity on me,” Katya reproached him bitterly, and began to cry.

“Then you will come!” Alyosha said firmly, seeing her tears. “I will go and tell him that you are coming at once.”

“No, on no account tell him!” Katya cried out in fright. “I will come, but don’t tell him beforehand, because I will come, but perhaps I won’t go in… I don’t know yet…”

Her voice broke. She was breathing with difficulty. Alyosha got up to go.

“And what if I meet someone?” she suddenly said quietly, turning pale all over again.

“That is why it must be now, so that you meet no one there. There will be no one; I tell you truly. We shall be waiting,” he concluded insistently, and went out of the room.
