## Book Six: The Russian Monk · Chapter I · Elder Zosima and His Visitors

When Alyosha, with anxiety and pain in his heart, went into the elder’s cell, he stopped almost in amazement. Instead of a dying man, perhaps already unconscious, as he had feared to find him, he suddenly saw him sitting in his armchair, his face worn with weakness but bright and cheerful, surrounded by visitors and holding a quiet, radiant conversation with them. He had, however, got up from his bed no more than a quarter of an hour before Alyosha came. The visitors had gathered in his cell earlier and waited for him to wake, on Father Paisy’s firm assurance that “the teacher will certainly rise, to talk once more with those dear to his heart, as he himself declared and promised this morning.” Father Paisy believed firmly in this promise, as in every word of the dying elder, so firmly that if he had seen him completely unconscious and even no longer breathing, but had his promise that he would rise once more and take leave of him, he would perhaps not have believed even death itself, but would have gone on expecting the dying man to come to and fulfil what he had promised. That morning, as he lay down to sleep, Elder Zosima had told him positively: “I shall not die before I have once more drunk my fill of talk with you, beloved of my heart, looked on your dear faces and poured out my soul to you once more.” Those who had gathered for what was probably the elder’s last conversation were his most devoted friends of many years. There were four of them: the hieromonks Father Iosif and Father Paisy; the hieromonk Father Mikhail, head of the hermitage, a man not yet very old, far from so learned, of humble origin, but firm in spirit, of unshakeable and simple faith, stern in appearance but deeply tender of heart, though he evidently hid his tenderness to the point of a kind of shame. The fourth visitor was a quite old, simple little monk of the poorest peasant origin, Brother Anfim, hardly able to read and write, silent and quiet, who rarely spoke to anyone, the humblest among the most humble, with the look of a man forever frightened by something great and terrible beyond the reach of his mind. Elder Zosima loved this trembling man very much and all his life treated him with extraordinary respect, though perhaps he had spoken fewer words to him than to anyone in his whole life, despite having once spent many years travelling with him, the two of them alone, all over holy Russia. That was very long ago, some forty years before, when Elder Zosima first began his monastic labours in a poor, little-known monastery in Kostroma, and soon after went with Father Anfim on his wanderings to collect donations for their poor little Kostroma monastery. Everyone, host and visitors, was in the elder’s second room, where his bed stood, a room that, as was said before, was very cramped, so that all four (besides Porfiry the novice, who remained standing) could barely fit round the elder’s armchair on chairs brought in from the first room. It was already getting dark; the room was lit by the icon lamps and wax candles before the icons. Seeing Alyosha, who was embarrassed on coming in and stopped in the doorway, the elder smiled at him joyfully and held out his hand.

“Greetings, my quiet one, greetings, my dear; here you are. I knew you would come.”

Alyosha went up to him, bowed down before him to the ground and wept. Something was bursting from his heart; his soul trembled; he wanted to sob.

“What is this? Wait before you mourn me,” the elder smiled, laying his right hand on his head. “You see, I am sitting and talking; perhaps I shall live another twenty years, as that kind, dear woman from Vyshegorye wished me yesterday, with little Lizaveta in her arms. Remember, Lord, the mother and the little girl Lizaveta!” (He crossed himself.) “Porfiry, did you take her gift where I told you?”

He was remembering the sixty kopecks given yesterday by the cheerful pilgrim woman to be passed on “to one poorer than I am”. Such offerings are made as penances, laid on oneself voluntarily for some reason, and always from money earned by one’s own labour. The elder had sent Porfiry the evening before to a townswoman of ours who had recently been burnt out, a widow with children who had gone begging after the fire. Porfiry hastened to report that it was done and that he had given it, as he had been told, “from an unknown benefactress”.

“Stand up, my dear,” the elder went on to Alyosha, “let me look at you. Have you been to your family? Have you seen your brother?”

It seemed strange to Alyosha that he asked so firmly and precisely about only one of the brothers. But which? So it was perhaps for that brother’s sake that he had sent him away both yesterday and today.

“I have seen one of my brothers,” Alyosha answered.

“I mean the one from yesterday, the eldest, to whom I bowed down to the ground.”

“Him I only saw yesterday; today I could not find him anywhere,” said Alyosha.

“Make haste and find him; go again tomorrow, and make haste; leave everything and make haste. Perhaps you will still be in time to prevent something terrible. Yesterday I bowed down to his great future suffering.”

He suddenly fell silent and seemed to be thinking. The words were strange. Father Iosif, who had witnessed the elder’s bow to the ground the day before, exchanged a glance with Father Paisy. Alyosha could not bear it.

“Father and teacher,” he said in extreme agitation, “your words are too obscure… What suffering awaits him?”

“Do not be curious. Something terrible appeared to me yesterday… as if his whole destiny were expressed in his look yesterday. There was a look he gave… so that my heart was instantly horrified at what this man is preparing for himself. Once or twice in my life I have seen the same expression on certain faces… as if it pictured the whole destiny of those people, and their destiny, alas, came true. I sent you to him, Alexei, because I thought your brotherly face would help him. But all is from the Lord, and all our destinies. ‘Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit.’ Remember that. And you, Alexei, I have blessed many times in my thoughts in the course of my life for your face; know that,” the elder said with a quiet smile. “This is what I think of you: you will go forth from these walls, and in the world you will live as a monk. You will have many opponents, but even your enemies will love you. Life will bring you many misfortunes, but through them you will be happy, and you will bless life and make others bless it, which matters most of all. Well, that is what you are like. My fathers and teachers,” he turned to his visitors with a tender smile, “never until this day have I said, even to him, why the face of this youth was so dear to my soul. Only now will I say it: his face was to me as a reminder and a prophecy. At the dawn of my days, when I was still a little child, I had an elder brother who died as a youth, before my eyes, only seventeen years old. And afterwards, passing through my life, I became convinced little by little that this brother of mine had been in my destiny as a sign and a calling from above; for had he not appeared in my life, had he not been at all, never perhaps, so I think, would I have taken the monastic habit and entered on this precious path. That first appearance was in my childhood, and now, at the end of my road, a repetition of it, as it were, has appeared before my eyes. It is wonderful, fathers and teachers, that, not being so like him in face, only a little, Alexei seemed to me so like him in spirit that many times I took him as if he were that youth, my brother, come to me mysteriously at the end of my road for a kind of remembrance and insight, so that I even wondered at myself and at so strange a dream of mine. Do you hear this, Porfiry?” he turned to the novice who waited on him. “Many times I have seen on your face as it were a grief that I love Alexei more than you. Now you know why it was so; but I love you too, know that, and many times I have grieved that you were grieved. And to you, dear visitors, I want to tell about this youth, my brother, for there has been no appearance in my life more precious, more prophetic and moving than this. My heart is moved, and I behold my whole life at this moment as if I were living it all again…”

Here I must remark that this last conversation of the elder with the visitors who came to him on the last day of his life has been preserved partly in writing. Alexei Fyodorovich Karamazov wrote it down from memory some time after the elder’s death. But whether it was entirely the conversation of that evening, or whether in his notes he added to it from earlier conversations with his teacher, I cannot decide. Besides, the elder’s whole speech in these notes runs on as if without a break, as though he were setting out his life in the form of a story addressed to his friends, whereas without doubt, from what was told afterwards, it happened in reality somewhat differently, for the conversation that evening was general, and though the visitors interrupted their host little, they did speak for themselves, joining in the conversation, perhaps even telling and relating something of their own; besides, there could be no such continuity in the account, for the elder sometimes gasped for breath, lost his voice, and even lay down on his bed to rest, though he did not fall asleep, and the visitors did not leave their places. Once or twice the conversation was interrupted by reading from the Gospel; Father Paisy read. It is remarkable too that none of them, however, supposed that he would die that very night, all the more since on this last evening of his life, after his deep sleep during the day, he seemed suddenly to have found a new strength in himself, which sustained him through the whole of this long conversation with his friends. It was like a last tenderness that sustained in him an incredible animation, but only for a short time, for his life broke off suddenly… But of that later. For now I want to say that I have preferred, without setting out all the details of the conversation, to confine myself to the elder’s story as Alexei Fyodorovich Karamazov’s manuscript gives it. It will be shorter and not so tiring, though of course, I repeat, Alyosha took much from earlier conversations too and put it together.
