## VII

How it came about in the third month of Ivan Ilyich’s illness cannot be said, because it happened step by step, imperceptibly; but what came about was that his wife, his daughter, his son, the servants, his acquaintances, the doctors and, above all, he himself, knew that the whole interest he held for other people lay only in how soon he would at last vacate his place, free the living from the constraint his presence caused them, and be freed himself from his sufferings.

He slept less and less; they gave him opium and began injecting morphine. But this did not relieve him. The dull anguish he felt in his half-drugged state relieved him at first only as something new, but then it became as tormenting as the open pain, or more so.

Special dishes were prepared for him on the doctors’ instructions; but all these dishes became more and more tasteless to him, more and more disgusting.

Special arrangements were also made for his bowel movements, and every time it was a torment. A torment from the uncleanness, the indecency and the smell, from the awareness that another person had to take part in it.

But it was in this most unpleasant business that Ivan Ilyich found his consolation. It was always Gerasim, the young servant, who came to carry the things out for him.

Gerasim was a clean, fresh young peasant, grown sturdy on town food. Always cheerful and bright. At first the sight of this man, always cleanly dressed in the Russian style, doing this repulsive work, embarrassed Ivan Ilyich.

Once, getting up from the commode and unable to pull up his trousers, he collapsed into a soft armchair and looked with horror at his bare, powerless thighs, with the muscles sharply outlined on them.

In came Gerasim, in his thick boots, spreading around him the pleasant smell of the tar on his boots and the freshness of the winter air, with his light, strong step, in a clean hempen apron and a clean cotton shirt, its sleeves rolled up on his bare, strong young arms; and, not looking at Ivan Ilyich, evidently holding back, so as not to hurt the sick man, the joy of life that shone in his face, he went over to the commode.

“Gerasim,” said Ivan Ilyich weakly.

Gerasim started, evidently afraid that he had done something wrong, and with a quick movement turned towards the sick man his fresh, kind, simple young face, which was just beginning to grow a beard.

“What would you like, sir?”

“This must be unpleasant for you, I think. Forgive me. I can’t help it.”

“Oh, please, sir.” And Gerasim’s eyes flashed and he showed his young white teeth. “Why shouldn’t I take a little trouble? You’re ill.”

And with his deft, strong hands he did his usual work and went out with a light step. And five minutes later, stepping just as lightly, he came back.

Ivan Ilyich was still sitting in the armchair.

“Gerasim,” he said, when Gerasim had put down the clean, washed pan, “please help me; come here.” Gerasim came over. “Lift me up. It’s hard for me alone, and I’ve sent Dmitry away.”

Gerasim came over; with his strong arms, as lightly as he walked, he put them round him, deftly and gently lifted and held him, pulled up his trousers with his other hand, and was going to sit him down. But Ivan Ilyich asked him to take him to the sofa. Gerasim, without effort and as if without pressing, led him, almost carrying him, to the sofa and sat him down.

“Thank you. How deftly and well you... do everything.”

Gerasim smiled again and was about to go. But Ivan Ilyich felt so good with him that he did not want to let him go.

“Here’s something: please move that chair over for me. No, that one, under my feet. It’s easier for me when my feet are higher.”

Gerasim brought the chair, set it down without a knock, lowering it evenly to the floor in one movement, and lifted Ivan Ilyich’s feet onto the chair; it seemed to Ivan Ilyich that he felt better while Gerasim was lifting his feet high.

“I feel better when my feet are higher,” said Ivan Ilyich. “Put that cushion under them.”

Gerasim did so. Again he lifted the feet and laid them down. Again Ivan Ilyich felt better while Gerasim held his feet. When he lowered them, he felt worse.

“Gerasim,” he said to him, “are you busy now?”

“Not at all, sir,” said Gerasim, who had learned from town people how to speak to gentlefolk.

“What else do you have to do?”

“What should I have to do? I’ve done everything, except chop wood for tomorrow.”

“Then could you hold my feet up like this?”

“Why not? I can.” Gerasim lifted the feet higher, and it seemed to Ivan Ilyich that in this position he felt no pain at all.

“But what about the wood?”

“Please don’t worry, sir. There’ll be time.”

Ivan Ilyich told Gerasim to sit down and hold his feet, and talked to him. And, strange to say, it seemed to him that he felt better while Gerasim held his feet.

From then on Ivan Ilyich sometimes called Gerasim and had him hold his feet on his shoulders, and he liked to talk to him. Gerasim did this easily, willingly, simply, and with a kindness that moved Ivan Ilyich. Health, strength and vigour of life in all other people offended Ivan Ilyich; only Gerasim’s strength and vigour of life did not distress but soothed him.

Ivan Ilyich’s chief torment was the lie: the lie, for some reason accepted by everyone, that he was only ill and not dying, and that he only had to keep calm and follow his treatment, and then something very good would come of it. But he knew that whatever they did, nothing would come of it but still more agonising suffering, and death. And this lie tormented him; it tormented him that they would not admit what everyone knew and he knew, but wanted to lie to him about his terrible situation, and wanted him, and forced him, to take part in this lie himself. The lie, this lie committed over him on the eve of his death, a lie that had to bring down this terrible, solemn act of his death to the level of all their visits, curtains, sturgeon for dinner... was terribly tormenting for Ivan Ilyich. And, strangely, many times, when they were playing their tricks over him, he was within a hair’s breadth of shouting at them: “Stop lying; you know and I know that I am dying, so at least stop lying.” But he never had the courage to do it. The terrible, awful act of his dying, he saw, had been brought down by everyone around him to the level of a chance unpleasantness, partly an indecency (rather as one treats a man who comes into a drawing room and gives off a bad smell); brought down by that very “decency” he had served all his life. He saw that no one would pity him, because no one even wanted to understand his situation. Only Gerasim understood this situation and pitied him. And so Ivan Ilyich felt good only with Gerasim. He felt good when Gerasim, sometimes all night long, held his feet and would not go to bed, saying: “Please don’t worry, Ivan Ilyich, I’ll get my sleep later”; or when, suddenly speaking to him as an equal, he added: “If you weren’t ill, it would be different; but as it is, why shouldn’t I help you?” Gerasim alone did not lie; everything showed that he alone understood what was happening and did not think it necessary to hide it, and simply pitied his wasted, weak master. Once he even said it straight out, when Ivan Ilyich was sending him away:

“We shall all die. So why shouldn’t I take a little trouble?” he said, meaning by this that he did not find his work a burden precisely because he was doing it for a dying man, and hoped that someone would do the same work for him when his time came.

Besides this lie, or because of it, what tormented Ivan Ilyich most of all was that no one pitied him as he wanted to be pitied. At certain moments, after long suffering, what Ivan Ilyich wanted most of all, however ashamed he would have been to admit it, was for someone to pity him as one pities a sick child. He wanted to be petted, kissed, wept over, as children are petted and comforted. He knew that he was an important member of the court, that he had a greying beard, and that therefore this was impossible; but he wanted it all the same. And in his relations with Gerasim there was something close to this, and so his relations with Gerasim comforted him. Ivan Ilyich wants to weep, wants to be petted and wept over; and then his colleague, the judge Shebek, comes in, and instead of weeping and being petted, Ivan Ilyich puts on a serious, stern, profound face and, by inertia, gives his opinion on the significance of a ruling of the Court of Appeal, and stubbornly insists on it. This lie around him and within him poisoned the last days of Ivan Ilyich’s life more than anything else.

## VIII

It was morning. It was morning only because Gerasim had gone and Pyotr the footman had come, put out the candles, opened one curtain and begun quietly tidying up. Whether it was morning or evening, Friday or Sunday, made no difference; it was all one and the same: the gnawing, agonising pain that never let up for a moment; the awareness of life hopelessly passing away but never quite gone; the same terrible, hateful death coming nearer, which alone was real; and the same lie. What were days, weeks and hours of the day here?

“Would you like some tea, sir?”

(“He needs order; the gentry must drink tea in the morning,” he thought, and said only:)

“No.”

“Would you like to move to the sofa, sir?”

(“He needs to tidy the room, and I’m in the way; I am the mess, the disorder,” he thought, and said only:)

“No, leave me alone.”

The footman fussed about a little more. Ivan Ilyich stretched out his hand. Pyotr came over obligingly.

“What would you like, sir?”

“My watch.”

Pyotr took the watch, which was lying close at hand, and gave it to him.

“Half past eight. Are they up?”

“No, sir. Vasily Ivanovich” (that was the son) “has gone to school, and Praskovya Fyodorovna gave orders to wake her if you asked for her. Shall I?”

“No, don’t.” (“Shouldn’t I try some tea?” he thought.) “Yes, tea... bring some.”

Pyotr went to the door. Ivan Ilyich felt afraid to be left alone. “How can I keep him here? Yes, the medicine.” “Pyotr, give me my medicine.” (“Why not? Perhaps the medicine will still help.”) He took the spoon and drank. “No, it won’t help. It’s all nonsense, a fraud,” he decided, as soon as he felt the familiar sickly, hopeless taste. “No, I can’t believe in it any more. But the pain, why the pain? If only it would stop for a minute.” And he groaned. Pyotr came back. “No, go. Bring the tea.”

Pyotr went out. Left alone, Ivan Ilyich groaned, not so much from the pain, terrible as it was, as from anguish. “The same thing over and over, all these endless days and nights. If only it would come sooner. What sooner? Death, darkness. No, no. Anything is better than death!”

When Pyotr came in with the tea on a tray, Ivan Ilyich looked at him for a long time in bewilderment, not understanding who or what he was. Pyotr was embarrassed by this look. And when Pyotr was embarrassed, Ivan Ilyich came to himself.

“Yes,” he said, “the tea... good, put it down. Only help me wash, and a clean shirt.”

And Ivan Ilyich began to wash. Resting between times, he washed his hands and face, cleaned his teeth, began to comb his hair and looked in the mirror. He felt afraid; what was especially frightening was the way his hair lay flat against his pale forehead.

While they were changing his shirt, he knew that he would be still more frightened if he looked at his body, and he did not look at himself. But at last it was all over. He put on his dressing gown, wrapped himself in a rug, and sat in the armchair to drink his tea. For a minute he felt refreshed, but as soon as he began to drink the tea, there was the same taste again, the same pain. He forced himself to finish it and lay down, stretching out his legs. He lay down and dismissed Pyotr.

Always the same. Now a drop of hope gleams, now a sea of despair rages, and always pain, always pain, always anguish, and always one and the same. Alone it is terribly dreary; he wants to call someone, but he knows in advance that with others it is worse still. “If only there were morphine again, to forget. I’ll tell the doctor to think of something else. It’s impossible, impossible like this.”

An hour, two hours pass like this. But now there is a ring at the door. Perhaps it is the doctor. Yes, it is the doctor, fresh, brisk, plump, cheerful, with that expression which says: “You’ve been frightened of something there, but we’ll arrange everything for you in a moment.” The doctor knows that this expression is out of place here, but he put it on once and for all and cannot take it off, like a man who has put on a tailcoat in the morning to go round paying visits. The doctor rubs his hands briskly and reassuringly.

“I’m cold. A healthy frost. Let me warm up,” he says, with an expression as if one only has to wait a little until he has warmed up, and then he will put everything right.

“Well, how are we?”

Ivan Ilyich feels that the doctor wants to say, “How are things?” but that he too feels he cannot talk like that, and he says, “How did you spend the night?”

Ivan Ilyich looks at the doctor with an expression that asks: “Will you never be ashamed of lying?”

But the doctor does not want to understand the question.

And Ivan Ilyich says:

“Just as terrible. The pain doesn’t go, doesn’t give way. If only there were something!”

“Yes, you patients are always like that. Well, now I think I’ve warmed up; even the most particular Praskovya Fyodorovna couldn’t object to my temperature. Well, good morning.” And the doctor shakes his hand.

And, throwing off all his former playfulness, the doctor begins with a serious face to examine the patient, his pulse, his temperature, and then the tapping and the listening begin.

Ivan Ilyich knows firmly and beyond doubt that all this is nonsense and empty deception; but when the doctor, kneeling, stretches over him, putting his ear now higher, now lower, and performs various gymnastic movements over him with a most significant face, Ivan Ilyich gives in to it, just as he used to give in to the speeches of lawyers, when he already knew very well that they were all lying, and why they were lying.

The doctor, kneeling on the sofa, was still tapping at something when Praskovya Fyodorovna’s silk dress rustled in the doorway and she was heard reproaching Pyotr for not telling her the doctor had arrived.

She comes in, kisses her husband, and at once begins to prove that she has been up for a long time, and that it was only through a misunderstanding that she was not there when the doctor arrived.

Ivan Ilyich looks at her, examines her from head to foot, and holds against her the whiteness, the plumpness and the cleanness of her hands and neck, the gloss of her hair and the sparkle of her eyes, full of life. He hates her with all the strength of his soul, and her touch makes him suffer from a surge of hatred for her.

Her attitude to him and his illness is the same as ever. Just as the doctor had worked out an attitude to his patients that he could no longer take off, so she had worked out one attitude to him: that he was not doing something he ought to do, and was himself to blame, and she lovingly reproached him for it; and she could no longer take off this attitude.

“You see, he doesn’t do as he’s told! He doesn’t take his medicine on time. And above all, he lies in a position that’s certainly bad for him: with his legs up.”

She told how he made Gerasim hold his feet.

The doctor smiled with contemptuous kindness, as if to say: “What can one do? These patients sometimes think up such foolish things; but one can forgive them.”

When the examination was over, the doctor looked at his watch, and then Praskovya Fyodorovna announced to Ivan Ilyich that, whatever he might wish, she had invited the famous doctor for today, and that he and Mikhail Danilovich (that was the ordinary doctor’s name) would examine him together and consult.

“Now please don’t object. I’m doing this for myself,” she said ironically, letting him feel that she was doing everything for him, and only saying this so as to leave him no right to refuse her. He was silent and frowned. He felt that the lie surrounding him had become so tangled that it was hard to make anything out.

Everything she did for him, she did only for herself; and she told him she was doing for herself what she really was doing for herself, as something so incredible that he was bound to understand it the other way round.

And indeed, at half past eleven the famous doctor arrived. Again there was listening, and significant conversations in his presence and in the next room about the kidney and the appendix, and questions and answers with such a significant air that once again, instead of the real question of life and death, which now alone stood before him, there came forward the question of the kidney and the appendix, which were doing something not as they should, and which Mikhail Danilovich and the celebrity were about to attack and force to mend their ways.

The famous doctor took his leave with a serious but not a hopeless air. And to the timid question that Ivan Ilyich put to him, with eyes raised to him and shining with fear and hope, whether there was any possibility of recovery, he answered that he could not vouch for it, but that there was a possibility. The look of hope with which Ivan Ilyich saw the doctor off was so pitiful that, seeing it, Praskovya Fyodorovna even burst into tears as she went out of the study to hand the famous doctor his fee.

The rise in his spirits produced by the doctor’s encouragement did not last long. Again the same room, the same pictures, curtains, wallpaper, medicine bottles, and the same aching, suffering body of his own. And Ivan Ilyich began to groan; they gave him an injection, and he lost consciousness.

When he came to, it was getting dark; they brought him his dinner. He forced himself to eat some broth; and again the same thing, and again night coming on.

After dinner, at seven o’clock, Praskovya Fyodorovna came into his room, dressed as if for an evening party, with her full bosom pushed up and traces of powder on her face. That morning she had reminded him of their outing to the theatre. Sarah Bernhardt was visiting the town, and they had a box, which he had insisted they take. Now he had forgotten about it, and her dress offended him. But he hid his offence when he remembered that he himself had insisted that they get a box and go, because it was an educational aesthetic pleasure for the children.

Praskovya Fyodorovna came in pleased with herself, but as if guilty. She sat down and asked about his health, he saw, only for the sake of asking and not in order to find out, knowing there was nothing to find out, and began to say what she needed to say: that she would not have gone for anything, but the box had been taken, and Hélène and their daughter and Petrishchev (the examining magistrate, their daughter’s fiancé) were going, and it was impossible to let them go alone. And that she would so much rather have sat with him. Only he must do as the doctor ordered while she was away.

“Oh, and Fyodor Petrovich” (the fiancé) “wanted to come in. May he? And Liza.”

“Let them come in.”

In came his daughter, dressed up, her young body bared, that body which made him suffer so. And she displayed it. Strong, healthy, evidently in love and indignant at illness, suffering and death, which stood in the way of her happiness.

In came Fyodor Petrovich too, in a tailcoat, his hair curled *à la Capoul*, with a long, sinewy neck tightly encased in a white collar, an enormous white shirt front, strong thighs tightly encased in narrow black trousers, one white glove drawn on his hand, and an opera hat.

Behind him, almost unnoticed, crept in the schoolboy, in his brand-new uniform, poor thing, wearing gloves, with terrible dark rings under his eyes, the meaning of which Ivan Ilyich knew.

He had always pitied his son. And the boy’s frightened and sympathetic look was terrible to him. Apart from Gerasim, it seemed to Ivan Ilyich that only Vasya understood and pitied him.

They all sat down and asked again about his health. There was a silence. Liza asked her mother about the opera glasses. There was a squabble between mother and daughter about who had put them where. It was unpleasant.

Fyodor Petrovich asked Ivan Ilyich whether he had seen Sarah Bernhardt. At first Ivan Ilyich did not understand what he was being asked, and then he said:

“No. Have you seen her already?”

“Yes, in *Adrienne Lecouvreur*.”

Praskovya Fyodorovna said she was especially good in such and such. Her daughter disagreed. A conversation began about the elegance and realism of her acting: the very conversation that is always the same.

In the middle of the conversation Fyodor Petrovich glanced at Ivan Ilyich and fell silent. The others glanced at him and fell silent. Ivan Ilyich was staring in front of him with shining eyes, evidently indignant with them. This had to be put right, but there was no way of putting it right. The silence had to be broken somehow. No one dared to break it, and everyone grew afraid that suddenly the decent lie would somehow be broken, and what really was would become clear to all. Liza was the first to dare. She broke the silence. She wanted to hide what everyone was feeling, but she gave it away.

“Well, if we’re going, it’s time,” she said, glancing at her watch, a present from her father, and with a barely noticeable smile, significant of something known only to the two of them, she glanced at the young man and stood up, her dress rustling. They all stood up, said goodbye, and left.

When they had gone, it seemed to Ivan Ilyich that he felt easier: the lie was gone; it had left with them. But the pain remained. The same pain, the same fear, made it so that nothing was harder, nothing easier. Everything was worse.

Again minute after minute went by, hour after hour, always the same, and still no end, and the inevitable end more and more terrible.

“Yes, send Gerasim,” he answered Pyotr’s question.
