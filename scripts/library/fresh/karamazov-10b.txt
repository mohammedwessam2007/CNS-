## Chapter II · Smerdyakov with a Guitar

And indeed he had no time. A thought had flashed into his mind while he was still saying goodbye to Lise. The thought was this: how to catch brother Dmitry now in the most cunning way, since Dmitry was obviously hiding from him? It was no longer early; it was getting on for three in the afternoon. With his whole being Alyosha longed to go to the monastery to his “great” dying man, but the need to see brother Dmitry outweighed everything: with every hour the conviction grew in Alyosha’s mind that a terrible catastrophe was inevitable and about to happen. What exactly the catastrophe consisted in, and what he wanted to say to his brother at this moment, he perhaps could not have defined himself. “Let my benefactor die without me, but at least I shall not reproach myself all my life that perhaps I could have saved something and did not save it, passed by, hurried to my own home. In doing so I shall be doing as his great word bids...”

His plan was to catch brother Dmitry unawares, namely: to climb over that wattle fence as yesterday, go into the garden and sit down in that summer house. “And if he is not there,” thought Alyosha, “then, without telling either Foma or the landladies, I’ll hide and wait in the summer house, even till evening. If he is still keeping watch for Grushenka’s coming, then very possibly he will come to the summer house...” Alyosha, however, did not reason too much about the details of the plan, but he decided to carry it out, even if he did not get to the monastery today...

Everything went without a hitch: he climbed over the fence almost at the same place as yesterday and secretly made his way to the summer house. He did not want to be noticed: both the landlady and Foma (if he was there) might take his brother’s side and obey his orders, and so either not let Alyosha into the garden or warn his brother in time that he was being looked for and asked after. There was no one in the summer house. Alyosha sat down in his place of yesterday and began to wait. He looked round the summer house; for some reason it seemed to him much more dilapidated than yesterday; it seemed so shabby to him this time. The day, however, was just as clear as yesterday. On the green table there was the imprint of a ring, probably from yesterday’s glass of brandy that had been spilled. Empty thoughts of no use to the business, as always during tedious waiting, crept into his head: for instance, why, coming in here now, had he sat down exactly on the very same place where he had sat yesterday, and why not on another? At last he grew very sad, sad from anxious uncertainty. But he had not sat a quarter of an hour when suddenly, somewhere very near, a chord on a guitar was heard. Someone was sitting, or had only just sat down, some twenty paces from him, no further, somewhere in the bushes. A memory suddenly flashed through Alyosha’s mind that yesterday, leaving his brother in the summer house, he had seen, or seemed to glimpse, on his left by the fence, a low old green garden bench among the bushes. So it must be on that that the visitors had now sat down. Who? A man’s voice suddenly began singing a couplet in a sugary falsetto, accompanying himself on the guitar:

> By a force invincible
> I am bound to my sweetheart.
> Lord, have mer-er-cy
> on her and me!
> On her and me!
> On her and me!

The voice stopped. A lackey’s tenor, and the twirl of the song a lackey’s. Another voice, a woman’s this time, suddenly said caressingly and as if timidly, but with great affectation, however:

“Why don’t you come to see us for so long, Pavel Fyodorovich, why do you keep despising us?”

“Not at all, ma’am,” answered the man’s voice, politely but above all with insistent and firm dignity. Evidently the man was the dominant one, and the woman was flirting. “The man, I think, is Smerdyakov,” thought Alyosha, “at least by his voice, and the lady is probably the daughter of the landlady of this little house, who came from Moscow, wears a dress with a train and goes to Marfa Ignatievna for soup...”

“I terribly love any kind of verse, if it is smooth,” the woman’s voice went on. “Why don’t you go on?”

The voice sang again:

> A royal crown,
> if only my sweetheart were well.
> Lord, have mer-er-cy
> on her and me!
> On her and me!
> On her and me!

“Last time it came out even better,” remarked the woman’s voice. “You sang about the crown: ‘if only my little darling were well’. It came out more tenderly that way; you must have forgotten today.”

“Verses are rubbish, ma’am,” Smerdyakov cut her short.

“Oh no, I love a little verse very much.”

“As for verse, ma’am, it is essential rubbish, ma’am. Consider for yourself: who in the world talks in rhyme? And if we all began to talk in rhyme, even by order of the authorities, how much would we say, ma’am? Verses are no business, Marya Kondratievna.”

“How clever you are about everything; how did you come to be so in everything?” the woman’s voice coaxed more and more.

“I could have done more than that, ma’am, I could have known more than that, ma’am, if it were not for my lot from my very childhood. I would have shot dead in a duel the man who told me I was a scoundrel because I was born of Stinking Lizaveta without a father; and they threw it in my face in Moscow too; it crept over there from here, thanks to Grigory Vasilievich. Grigory Vasilievich reproaches me that I rebel against my birth: ‘You,’ he says, ‘opened her womb.’ Womb or no womb, I would have let myself be killed while still in the womb, so as not to come into the world at all, ma’am. They said in the market, and your mama too took it into her head to tell me, in her great indelicacy, that she went about with her hair matted on her head and was only four feet and a lit-tel tall. Why say lit-tel, when one can simply say ‘little’, as all people say it? She wanted to say it tearfully, but that is a peasant’s tear, so to speak, ma’am, the most peasant feelings. Can a Russian peasant have feeling as against an educated man? In his lack of education he cannot have any feeling. From my very childhood, whenever I used to hear ‘lit-tel’, I could have thrown myself at the wall. I hate the whole of Russia, Marya Kondratievna.”

“If you were a little military cadet or a young little hussar, you wouldn’t talk like that; you would draw your sabre and go to defend the whole of Russia.”

“Not only do I not wish to be a military hussar, Marya Kondratievna, but on the contrary I wish for the abolition of all soldiers, ma’am.”

“And when the enemy comes, who will defend us?”

“There is no need at all, ma’am. In the year twelve there was a great invasion of Russia by the Emperor Napoleon, the first French one, the father of the present one, and it would have been a good thing if those same French had conquered us then: an intelligent nation would have conquered a very stupid one, ma’am, and joined it to itself. There would even have been quite different arrangements, ma’am.”

“But are they really so much better over there than ours? I wouldn’t exchange one of our young dandies for three of the youngest Englishmen,” Marya Kondratievna said tenderly, probably accompanying her words at that moment with the most languishing little eyes.

“That is as one fancies, ma’am.”

“And you yourself are just like a foreigner, just like the most noble foreigner; I tell you that through my shame.”

“If you wish to know, as regards depravity those over there and ours are all alike. All rogues, ma’am, but with this difference, that over there they go about in polished boots, while our scoundrel stinks in his poverty and finds nothing bad in it. The Russian people need flogging, ma’am, as Fyodor Pavlovich rightly said yesterday, though he is a madman, with all his children, ma’am.”

“You said yourself that you respect Ivan Fyodorovich so much.”

“And he referred to me as a stinking lackey. He thinks I might rebel; there he is mistaken, ma’am. If I had a certain sum in my pocket, I would have been gone from here long ago. Dmitry Fyodorovich is worse than any lackey in conduct, and in mind, and in his poverty, ma’am, and he can’t do anything at all, and yet, on the contrary, he is respected by everyone. I, let us suppose, am only a soup-maker, but with luck I can open a café-restaurant in Moscow on Petrovka. Because I cook specially, and not one of them in Moscow, except the foreigners, can serve anything specially. Dmitry Fyodorovich is a beggar in torn trousers, ma’am, but let him challenge the very first count’s son to a duel, and the other will go out with him, ma’am; and in what way is he better than me, ma’am? Because he is incomparably stupider than me. How much money he has whistled away without any use, ma’am.”

“I think it must be very nice at a duel,” Marya Kondratievna remarked suddenly.

“In what way, ma’am?”

“It is so terrible and brave, especially when young little officers with pistols in their hands blaze away at each other over some girl. Simply a picture. Ah, if only girls were allowed to watch, I would want terribly to see it.”

“It is good when he is aiming himself, but when they are aiming right at his own snout, then it is the stupidest feeling, ma’am. You would run from the spot, Marya Kondratievna.”

“Would you really run?”

But Smerdyakov did not condescend to answer. After a minute’s silence the chord sounded again, and the falsetto poured out the last couplet:

> However hard I try
> I shall go far away,
> to enjoy li-i-ife
> and live in the capital!
> I shall not grieve.
> I shall not grieve at all,
> I don’t even intend to grieve at all!

Here something unexpected happened: Alyosha suddenly sneezed; on the bench they instantly fell quiet. Alyosha got up and went towards them. It was indeed Smerdyakov, dressed up, pomaded and very nearly curled, in polished boots. The guitar lay on the bench. And the lady was Marya Kondratievna, the landlady’s daughter; she wore a light blue dress with a train four feet long; the girl was still young and would not have been bad-looking, but she had a very round face and dreadful freckles.

“Will brother Dmitry be back soon?” said Alyosha as calmly as possible.

Smerdyakov slowly rose from the bench; Marya Kondratievna rose too.

“How should I be informed about Dmitry Fyodorovich; it would be another matter if I were employed as his watchman?” Smerdyakov answered quietly, distinctly and disdainfully.

“But I was simply asking whether you knew,” Alyosha explained.

“I know nothing about his whereabouts, and I don’t wish to know, sir.”

“But my brother told me precisely that it is you who let him know everything that goes on in the house, and that you promised to let him know when Agrafena Alexandrovna comes.”

Smerdyakov slowly and imperturbably raised his eyes to him.

“And how were you pleased to get in this time, since the gate here has been bolted for an hour?” he asked, looking intently at Alyosha.

“I came from the lane over the fence straight into the summer house. You will forgive me for it, I hope,” he turned to Marya Kondratievna. “I needed to catch my brother quickly.”

“Ah, how could we be offended with you,” Marya Kondratievna drawled, flattered by Alyosha’s apology, “since Dmitry Fyodorovich too often comes to the summer house this way; we don’t even know, and there he is already sitting in the summer house.”

“I am looking for him very much now; I would very much like to see him, or to learn from you where he is now. Believe me, it is on business very important for him himself.”

“He doesn’t tell us,” Marya Kondratievna babbled.

“Though I come here as an acquaintance,” Smerdyakov began again, “he has oppressed me inhumanly even here with his incessant questioning about the master: what, he says, and how are things with him, who comes and who leaves, and can’t I tell him something else? Twice he even threatened me with death.”

“With death? How?” Alyosha was surprised.

“And is that anything to him, sir, with his character, which you yourself were pleased to observe yesterday, sir? If, he says, I let Agrafena Alexandrovna in and she spends the night here, you will be the first not to live. I am very much afraid of him, sir, and if I weren’t even more afraid, I ought to report him to the town authorities. God knows what he may even do, sir.”

“The other day he told him: ‘I’ll pound you in a mortar,’” added Marya Kondratievna.

“Well, if it’s a mortar, that is perhaps only talk...” Alyosha remarked. “If I could meet him now, I could say something to him about this too...”

“Here is the only thing I can tell you,” Smerdyakov said, as if he had suddenly thought of it. “I come here as an old neighbourly acquaintance, and why shouldn’t I come, sir? On the other hand, Ivan Fyodorovich sent me at dawn today to Dmitry Fyodorovich’s lodging in Lake Street, without a letter, sir, so that Dmitry Fyodorovich should come without fail, by word of mouth, to the tavern here in the square, to dine together, sir. I went, sir, but I did not find Dmitry Fyodorovich at his lodging, sir, and it was already eight o’clock. ‘He was here,’ they say, ‘but he’s gone out’: those were the very words his landlords told me. It is as if there is some conspiracy between them, sir, a mutual one, sir. And now, perhaps, at this very moment, he is sitting in that tavern with his brother Ivan Fyodorovich, since Ivan Fyodorovich did not come home to dinner, and Fyodor Pavlovich dined alone an hour ago and has now gone to lie down. I beg you most earnestly, however, not to tell him anything about me and what I have told you, sir, for he will kill me for nothing, sir.”

“Brother Ivan invited Dmitry to the tavern today?” Alyosha asked again quickly.

“Just so, sir.”

“To the Capital City tavern, in the square?”

“That very one, sir.”

“That is very possible!” Alyosha exclaimed in great agitation. “Thank you, Smerdyakov; that is important news; I’ll go there at once.”

“Don’t give me away, sir,” Smerdyakov said after him.

“Oh no, I’ll turn up at the tavern as if by chance; don’t worry.”

“But where are you going? I’ll open the gate for you,” Marya Kondratievna began to call.

“No, it’s nearer this way; I’ll go over the fence again.”

The news shook Alyosha terribly. He set off for the tavern. It was improper for him to go into a tavern in his dress, but to inquire on the stairs and call them out, that was possible. But just as he came up to the tavern, a window suddenly opened and brother Ivan himself shouted down to him from the window:

“Alyosha, can you come in here to me now or not? You’d do me a terrible favour.”

“I certainly can, only I don’t know how, in my clothes.”

“But I am in a private room; go up to the porch; I’ll run down to meet you...”

A minute later Alyosha was sitting beside his brother. Ivan was alone and having dinner.
