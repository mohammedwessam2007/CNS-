## CHAPTER V

When, the next morning, at eleven o’clock precisely, Raskolnikov entered the building of the — district, the department of the investigating magistrate, and asked to be announced to Porfiry Petrovich, he was even surprised at how long they kept him waiting: at least ten minutes passed before he was called. And by his reckoning, it seemed, they ought to have pounced on him at once. Meanwhile he stood in the waiting room, and people walked past him, back and forth, who apparently had nothing whatever to do with him. In the next room, which looked like an office, several clerks sat writing, and it was obvious that not one of them had the slightest idea who or what Raskolnikov was. With an uneasy and suspicious look he watched everything around him, on the lookout for some guard near him, some mysterious eye appointed to watch him so that he should not get away. But there was nothing of the kind: he saw only office faces, petty and preoccupied, then some other people, and no one had any need of him at all: he could go off to the four corners of the earth that very moment. The thought grew firmer and firmer in him that if that enigmatic man of yesterday, that phantom who had come up out of the ground, really knew everything and had seen everything, would they let him, Raskolnikov, stand here like this and wait calmly? And would they have waited for him here till eleven o’clock, until it suited him to come of his own accord? It followed that either the man had not yet reported anything, or... or simply he too knew nothing and had seen nothing with his own eyes (and how could he have seen?), and so all that had happened to him, Raskolnikov, yesterday, was again a phantom, magnified by his irritated and sick imagination. This guess had begun to take hold of him even yesterday, during his most violent alarm and despair. Thinking all this over now and preparing for a new battle, he suddenly felt that he was trembling, and indignation even boiled up in him at the thought that he was trembling with fear before the hateful Porfiry Petrovich. The most terrible thing of all for him was to meet that man again: he hated him beyond measure, infinitely, and was even afraid that his hatred might somehow give him away. And so strong was his indignation that it stopped the trembling at once; he prepared to go in with a cold and insolent air, and gave himself his word to keep silent as much as possible, to watch and listen, and, at least this time, at any cost, to conquer his morbidly irritated nature. At that very moment he was called in to Porfiry Petrovich.

It turned out that at that moment Porfiry Petrovich was alone in his study. His study was a room neither large nor small; in it stood a large writing table in front of a sofa covered with oilcloth, a bureau, a cupboard in the corner and a few chairs, all government furniture of polished yellow wood. In the corner, in the back wall, or rather in the partition, there was a locked door: beyond it, behind the partition, there must therefore be some further rooms. When Raskolnikov came in, Porfiry Petrovich at once closed the door by which he had entered, and they were left alone. He met his visitor with what seemed the merriest and most welcoming air, and only some minutes later did Raskolnikov notice in him, by certain signs, something like confusion, as if he had suddenly been thrown off his stride or caught at something very private and secret.

“Ah, my most esteemed friend! Here you are... in our parts...” Porfiry began, holding out both hands to him. “Well, sit down, my dear fellow! Or perhaps you don’t like being called most esteemed and... my dear fellow, just like that, tout court, without ceremony? Please don’t take it for familiarity... Here, sir, on the little sofa.”

Raskolnikov sat down, not taking his eyes off him.

“In our parts”, the apology for familiarity, the little French phrase “tout court”, and so on and so on: all these were characteristic signs. “He held out both hands to me, though, and didn’t give me either; he drew them back in time,” flashed through him suspiciously. Both were watching each other, but as soon as their eyes met, both, with the speed of lightning, looked away from each other.

“I have brought you this paper... about the watch... here it is, sir. Is it written properly, or must I write it out again?”

“What? The paper? Yes, yes... don’t worry, it’s quite right, sir,” said Porfiry Petrovich, as if hurrying somewhere, and only after saying it did he take the paper and look it over. “Yes, quite right, sir. Nothing more is needed,” he confirmed in the same patter, and put the paper on the table. Then, a minute later, already talking of something else, he took it from the table again and moved it to his bureau.

“You said yesterday, I think, that you would like to question me... formally... about my acquaintance with that... murdered woman?” Raskolnikov began again. “Now why did I put in ‘I think’?” flashed through him like lightning. “Now why am I so worried about having put in that ‘I think’?” another thought flashed through him at once, like lightning.

And he suddenly felt that his suspiciousness, from mere contact with Porfiry, from only two words, only two looks, had already grown in an instant to monstrous proportions... and that this was terribly dangerous: his nerves were getting irritated, his agitation was growing. “Trouble! Trouble!.. I’ll give myself away again.”

“Yes, yes, yes! Don’t worry! There’s plenty of time, plenty of time, sir,” muttered Porfiry Petrovich, walking up and down near the table, but somehow without any purpose, as if rushing now to the window, now to the bureau, now back to the table, now avoiding Raskolnikov’s suspicious look, now suddenly stopping still and looking straight at him point-blank. His small, fat, round little figure seemed extraordinarily strange as he did this, like a little ball rolling in different directions and at once bouncing off all the walls and corners.

“We’ll have time, sir, we’ll have time!.. And do you smoke? Have you any? Here, sir, a little cigarette, sir...” he went on, offering his visitor a cigarette. “You know, I am receiving you here, but my lodging is right here, behind the partition... a government lodging, sir, and I am in a private one for the time being. Some little repairs had to be done here. Now it is almost ready... a government lodging, you know, is a splendid thing, eh? What do you think?”

“Yes, a splendid thing,” answered Raskolnikov, looking at him almost mockingly.

“A splendid thing, a splendid thing...” Porfiry Petrovich repeated, as if suddenly thinking about something quite different. “Yes! a splendid thing!” he almost cried out at the end, suddenly raising his eyes to Raskolnikov and stopping two steps away from him. This repeated, silly assertion that a government lodging was a splendid thing was, in its vulgarity, too much at odds with the serious, thinking and enigmatic look which he now fixed on his visitor.

But this boiled up Raskolnikov’s anger still more, and he could no longer keep himself from a mocking and rather incautious challenge.

“And do you know what,” he asked suddenly, looking at him almost insolently and as if taking pleasure in his insolence, “there exists, I believe, a legal rule, a legal device, for all possible investigators: first to begin from far off, with trifles, or even with something serious, but quite irrelevant, so as to encourage, so to speak, or rather to distract the person being questioned, to lull his caution, and then suddenly, in the most unexpected way, to stun him on the crown of the head with some most fatal and dangerous question; isn’t that so? This, I believe, is still mentioned religiously in all the rules and manuals?”

“Just so, just so... what, do you think I was using the government lodging to, er... eh?” And having said this, Porfiry Petrovich narrowed his eyes and winked; something merry and sly ran over his face, the little wrinkles on his forehead smoothed out, his little eyes narrowed, his features stretched, and he suddenly broke into a nervous, prolonged laugh, heaving and shaking all over and looking straight into Raskolnikov’s eyes. Raskolnikov began to laugh himself, forcing himself somewhat; but when Porfiry, seeing that he too was laughing, went off into such a fit of laughter that he almost turned purple, Raskolnikov’s disgust suddenly overcame all caution: he stopped laughing, frowned, and looked long and hatefully at Porfiry, not taking his eyes off him, all through his long laughter, which seemed to go on as if on purpose. The incaution, however, was obvious on both sides: it turned out that Porfiry Petrovich was as if laughing in the face of his visitor, who received this laughter with hatred, and was very little embarrassed by the circumstance. This last was very significant for Raskolnikov: he understood that Porfiry Petrovich had surely not been embarrassed at all earlier either, but that on the contrary he himself, Raskolnikov, had perhaps fallen into a trap; that there was obviously something here that he did not know, some purpose; that perhaps everything was already prepared and would be revealed at any moment, this very minute, and come crashing down...

He went straight to the point at once, got up from his seat and took his cap.

“Porfiry Petrovich,” he began resolutely, but with rather strong irritability, “yesterday you expressed the wish that I should come for some kind of questioning.” (He laid special stress on the word questioning.) “I have come, and if you need anything, then ask; if not, allow me to withdraw. I have no time, I have business... I must be at the funeral of that same official run over by horses, of whom you... also know...” he added, at once angry at having added this, and therefore at once more irritated still. “I am sick of all this, sir, do you hear, and have been for a long time... it was partly why I was ill... in a word,” he almost shouted, feeling that the phrase about illness was still more out of place, “in a word: be so good as either to question me or to let me go, at once... and if you are going to question me, then only in due form, sir! Otherwise I shall not permit it; and so, for the time being, goodbye, since the two of us have nothing to do together now.”

“Lord! What are you saying! What is there to question you about?” Porfiry Petrovich suddenly began clucking, at once changing both his tone and his look and instantly ceasing to laugh. “Please don’t worry,” he fussed, now rushing in all directions again, now suddenly trying to make Raskolnikov sit down. “There’s plenty of time, plenty of time, sir, and all this is mere trifles, sir! I, on the contrary, am so glad that you have come to us at last... I am receiving you as a guest. And forgive me for that cursed laughter, my dear Rodion Romanovich. Rodion Romanovich? That is your patronymic, isn’t it?.. I’m a nervous man, sir; you made me laugh very much with the wit of your remark; sometimes, really, I start shaking like india rubber, and for half an hour at a time... I’m given to laughing, sir. With my build I am even afraid of a stroke. But sit down, what is the matter?.. Please, my dear fellow, or else I shall think you are angry...”

Raskolnikov was silent, listening and observing, still frowning angrily. He did sit down, however, but without letting go of his cap.

“I will tell you one thing about myself, my dear Rodion Romanovich, by way of explaining my character, so to speak,” Porfiry Petrovich went on, bustling about the room and, as before, seeming to avoid meeting his visitor’s eyes. “I am, you know, a bachelor, a man not of the world and not known, and besides, a finished man, a stiffened man, sir, gone to seed and... and... and have you noticed, Rodion Romanovich, that among us, that is, here in Russia, sir, and most of all in our Petersburg circles, if two intelligent men who are not yet very well acquainted, but, so to speak, respect each other mutually, as you and I do now, sir, come together, they can’t find a subject for conversation for a whole half hour; they stiffen before each other, sit there and are mutually embarrassed. Everyone has a subject of conversation; the ladies, for instance... people of the world, for instance, of the higher tone, always have a subject of conversation, c’est de rigueur, it is the rule; but people of the middling sort, like us, are all bashful and untalkative... the thinking ones, that is. Why is that, my dear fellow? Is it that we have no public interests, sir, or are we so very honest that we don’t wish to deceive each other, I don’t know, sir. Eh? What do you think? But put down your little cap, sir, as if you were about to leave this minute; really, it’s awkward to look at... I, on the contrary, am so glad, sir...”

Raskolnikov put down his cap, continuing to be silent and listening seriously, frowning, to Porfiry’s empty and confused chatter. “What, does he really want to distract my attention with his stupid chatter?”

“I won’t offer you coffee, sir, this isn’t the place; but why not sit for five minutes with a friend, for diversion,” Porfiry poured out without stopping, “and you know, sir, all these official duties... but don’t be offended, my dear fellow, that I keep walking up and down like this, back and forth; forgive me, my dear fellow, I am so afraid of offending you, but exercise is simply indispensable to me, sir. I sit all the time, and I’m so glad to walk for five minutes... haemorrhoids, sir... I keep meaning to treat myself with gymnastics; there, they say, state councillors, actual state councillors and even privy councillors are happy to skip with a rope, sir; that’s how it is, science, in our age, sir... yes, sir... And as for these duties here, the interrogations and all this formality... you yourself, my dear fellow, were pleased just now to mention interrogations, sir... so, you know, really, my dear Rodion Romanovich, these interrogations sometimes throw the interrogator off more than the interrogated... You were pleased, my dear fellow, to remark on that just now with perfect justice and wit.” (Raskolnikov had remarked nothing of the kind.) “One gets muddled, sir! Really, one gets muddled! And it’s always one and the same thing, always one and the same, like a drum! There’s the reform coming, and at least we’ll be renamed, he-he-he! And as for our legal methods, as you so wittily put it, I agree with you entirely, sir. Well, tell me, who among all the accused, even the most homespun peasant, doesn’t know that, for instance, they will first start lulling him with irrelevant questions (in your happy expression), and then suddenly stun him on the very crown, with the butt of the axe, sir, he-he-he! on the very crown, in your happy comparison, he-he! So you really thought that I wanted to use the lodging to... he-he! You are an ironical man. Well, I won’t! Ah, yes, by the way, one little word calls up another, one thought calls up another: you were also pleased to mention form earlier, regarding, you know, the little interrogation, sir... But what of form! Form, you know, in many cases, is nonsense, sir. Sometimes it is more profitable just to have a friendly talk. Form will never run away, allow me to reassure you on that, sir; and what is form, essentially, I ask you? An investigator cannot be hampered by form at every step. The investigator’s business, you know, is, so to speak, a free art, in its way, sir, or something of the kind... he-he-he!..”

Porfiry Petrovich paused for breath for a moment. He poured out his words tirelessly, now senselessly empty phrases, now suddenly slipping in some enigmatic little words, and at once getting lost again in nonsense. He was almost running about the room by now, moving his fat little legs faster and faster, looking at the ground all the time, his right hand thrust behind his back, his left hand constantly waving and making various gestures, each time amazingly unsuited to his words. Raskolnikov suddenly noticed that as he ran about the room he had twice seemed to stop by the door, for an instant, as if listening... “Is he expecting something?”

“And you are really perfectly right, sir,” Porfiry took up again, looking merrily and with extraordinary simple-heartedness at Raskolnikov (which made him start and instantly prepare himself), “really right, sir, to have been pleased to laugh with such wit at our legal forms, he-he! These profoundly psychological methods of ours (some of them, of course) are extremely ridiculous, sir, and perhaps useless too, sir, if one is very constrained by form, sir. Yes, sir... I am back to form again: well, suppose I recognised, or rather suspected, someone or other, this one, that one, as a criminal, sir, in some little case entrusted to me... You are preparing to be a lawyer, aren’t you, Rodion Romanovich?”

“Yes, I was...”

“Well, then here is a little example for the future, so to speak; that is, don’t think that I would dare to teach you: why, you publish such articles about crimes! No, sir, but just as a fact, I will venture to present a little example. So suppose I considered this one, that one, the other, to be a criminal; why, I ask, should I disturb him before the time, even if I had evidence against him, sir? One I am obliged, for instance, to arrest as soon as possible, but another is not of that character, really, sir; so why not let him walk about the town a bit, he-he, sir! No, I see you don’t quite understand, so I’ll picture it more clearly for you, sir: if I put him away too early, for instance, why, I might give him, so to speak, moral support, he-he! You are laughing?” (Raskolnikov had no thought of laughing: he sat with his lips compressed, not taking his inflamed gaze from Porfiry Petrovich’s eyes.) “And yet that is how it is, sir, especially with some subjects, because people are very various, sir, and over everything there is only practice, sir. You are pleased to say now: evidence; well, let us suppose there is evidence, sir, but evidence, my dear fellow, cuts both ways, for the most part, sir, and I am an investigator, and therefore a weak man, I confess: I would like to present the case, so to speak, with mathematical clarity; I would like to get hold of such a piece of evidence that it would be like two times two is four! Like a direct and indisputable proof! But if I lock him up at the wrong time, even if I were sure that it was he, why, I may deprive myself of the means of exposing him further, and why? Because I will give him, so to speak, a definite position, define him psychologically, so to speak, and set him at ease, and so he will withdraw from me into his shell: he will understand at last that he is a prisoner. They say that in Sevastopol, right after Alma, the clever people were terribly afraid that the enemy would attack in open force at any moment and take Sevastopol at once; but when they saw that the enemy preferred a regular siege and was opening the first parallel, my, they say, how glad and relieved the clever people were, sir: it meant the business would drag on for at least two months, because when would a regular siege take it! You’re laughing again, you don’t believe me again? Well, of course, you are right too. You are right, sir, right, sir! These are all particular cases, I agree with you; the case I presented is indeed a particular one, sir! But here is what one must observe, my kindest Rodion Romanovich: the general case, sir, that very one for which all the legal forms and rules are fitted and from which they are calculated and written down in books, does not exist at all, sir, for the very reason that every case, every one, a crime, for instance, as soon as it happens in reality, at once turns into a completely particular case, sir; and sometimes into such a one: just like nothing that has gone before, sir. Most comical cases of that kind sometimes occur, sir. Why, if I leave some gentleman quite alone: don’t take him and don’t disturb him, but let him know every hour and every minute, or at least suspect, that I know everything, every last detail, and am watching him day and night, guarding him without sleeping, and let him be consciously under eternal suspicion and fear before me, why, by God, he will get dizzy, really, sir, he will come of his own accord and perhaps even do something that will be like two times two, will have, so to speak, a mathematical look, and that is pleasant, sir. That can happen even with a clod-footed peasant, and all the more with one of us, a modern intelligent man, and developed in a certain direction at that! Because, my dear, it is a very important thing to understand in which direction a man is developed. And nerves, sir, nerves, sir, you have quite forgotten them, sir! Why, nowadays all this is sick, and thin, and irritated!.. And the bile, how much bile there is in them all! Why, I tell you, that is a kind of mine on occasion, sir! And what does it worry me that he walks about the town unbound! Let him, let him walk about for now, let him; I know anyway that he is my little victim and won’t run away from me anywhere! And where would he run to, he-he! Abroad, perhaps? A Pole will run abroad, but not he, especially since I am watching and have taken measures. Into the depths of the fatherland, perhaps? But peasants live there, real, homespun, Russian ones; why, a modern developed man would sooner choose prison than live with such foreigners as our peasants, he-he! But this is all nonsense and externals. What is ‘run away’! That is the formal side; that is not the main thing; it’s not only because there is nowhere to run that he won’t run away from me: he won’t run away from me psychologically, he-he! What a little expression! He won’t run away from me by a law of nature, even if there were somewhere to run. Have you seen a moth before a candle? Well, so he will keep, keep circling round me as round a candle; freedom will lose its charm for him, he will begin to brood, to get entangled, he will entangle himself all round as in a net, he will worry himself to death!.. More than that: he himself will prepare for me some mathematical little thing, like two times two, if only I give him a long enough interval... And he will keep, keep making circles round me, narrowing and narrowing the radius, and... slap! He will fly straight into my mouth, and I will swallow him, sir, and that is very pleasant indeed, sir, he-he-he! You don’t believe me?”

Raskolnikov did not answer; he sat pale and motionless, still gazing with the same intensity into Porfiry’s face.

“A fine lesson!” he thought, growing cold. “This is not even cat and mouse any more, as it was yesterday. And he is not just showing me his strength for nothing and... prompting me: he is far too clever for that! There is another purpose here; what is it? Eh, nonsense, brother, you are trying to frighten me and being cunning! You have no proofs, and yesterday’s man does not exist! You simply want to throw me off, want to irritate me before the time and slam me down in that state; only you’re lying, you’ll come to grief, you’ll come to grief! But why, why prompt me to such an extent?.. Are we counting on my sick nerves?.. No, brother, you’re lying, you’ll come to grief, though you have prepared something... Well, we shall see what you have prepared there.”

And he braced himself with all his strength, preparing for a terrible and unknown catastrophe. At moments he wanted to throw himself on Porfiry and strangle him on the spot. Even as he came in here he had feared this anger. He felt that his lips had dried up, his heart was pounding, froth had dried on his lips. But still he resolved to keep silent and not utter a word until the right time. He understood that this was the best tactic in his position, because not only would he not give himself away, but on the contrary he would irritate the enemy himself with his silence, and perhaps the other would give himself away to him. At least he hoped so.

“No, I see you don’t believe me, sir; you keep thinking I am playing innocent little jokes on you,” Porfiry took up, growing merrier and merrier and constantly giggling with pleasure, and beginning to circle the room again. “Of course you are right, sir; God himself has made my figure so that it arouses only comic thoughts in others; a buffoon, sir; but I will tell you this, and repeat it again, sir: you, my dear Rodion Romanovich, forgive me, an old man, are still a young man, sir, in your first youth, so to speak, and therefore you value the human mind above all, like all young people. The playful sharpness of the mind and the abstract arguments of reason tempt you, sir. And that is exactly like the old Austrian Hofkriegsrat, for instance, as far as I can judge of military events, that is: on paper they defeated Napoleon and took him prisoner, and how wittily they calculated and arranged everything there in their office, and then you look, and General Mack surrenders with his whole army, he-he-he! I see, I see, my dear Rodion Romanovich, you are laughing at me for picking little examples from military history, such a civilian as I am. But what is to be done, it’s a weakness; I love military matters, and how I love reading all these military dispatches... I have decidedly missed my career. I should have served in the army, sir, really, sir. I might not have become a Napoleon, but I would have been a major, sir, he-he-he! Well, sir, now, my dear, I will tell you the whole detailed truth about the particular case, that is: reality and nature, my dear sir, are an important thing, and oh, how they sometimes cut down the most far-sighted calculation! Eh, listen to an old man, I am speaking seriously, Rodion Romanovich.” (As he said this, Porfiry Petrovich, who was hardly thirty-five, really did seem suddenly to grow old all over: even his voice changed, and he seemed to shrink all over.) “Besides, I am a frank man, sir... Am I a frank man or not? What do you think? It seems to me that I am entirely: I tell you such things for nothing, and don’t even ask for a reward for it, he-he! Well, then, sir, I continue, sir: wit, in my opinion, is a magnificent thing, sir; it is, so to speak, the beauty of nature and the consolation of life, and what tricks, it seems, it can play, so that how, it seems, is some poor little investigator to guess sometimes, who is besides carried away by his own fancy, as always happens, because he is a man too, sir! But nature comes to the rescue of the poor little investigator, there’s the trouble! And youth carried away by its wit never thinks of that, youth ‘stepping over all obstacles’ (as you were pleased to put it in the wittiest and most cunning way). Let us suppose he will lie, I mean the man, sir, the particular case, sir, the incognito, sir, and lie excellently, in the most cunning manner; here, it seems, is triumph, and he can enjoy the fruits of his wit, but he goes... slap! and at the most interesting, the most scandalous point, he faints. Let us suppose it is illness; rooms are sometimes stuffy too; but all the same, sir! All the same he has given someone the idea! He lied incomparably, but he didn’t know how to reckon with nature. There is where the treachery lies, sir! Another time, carried away by the playfulness of his wit, he will begin to make a fool of the man who suspects him; he will turn pale as if on purpose, as if in play, but he turns pale too naturally, too much like the truth, and again he has given the idea! Even if he deceives him the first time, the other will think it over during the night, if he is no fool himself. Why, it happens like that at every step, sir! What’s more: he will begin running ahead himself, poking in where no one asks him, talking constantly about what he ought, on the contrary, to keep quiet about, slipping in various allegories, he-he! He will come himself and begin asking: why don’t you take me for so long? he-he-he! And that can happen with the wittiest man, with a psychologist and a man of letters, sir! Nature is a mirror, a mirror, sir, the most transparent one, sir! Look into it and admire, that’s what, sir! But why have you turned so pale, Rodion Romanovich? Isn’t it stuffy for you? Shall I open the little window?”

“Oh, don’t trouble, please,” cried Raskolnikov, and suddenly burst out laughing. “Please don’t trouble!”

Porfiry stopped in front of him, waited, and suddenly burst out laughing himself, following him. Raskolnikov got up from the sofa, suddenly breaking off his laughter, which was completely like a fit.

“Porfiry Petrovich!” he said loudly and distinctly, though he could hardly stand on his trembling legs. “I see clearly at last that you positively suspect me of the murder of that old woman and her sister Lizaveta. For my part I declare to you that I have long been sick of all this. If you find that you have the right to prosecute me lawfully, then prosecute me; to arrest me, then arrest me. But I will not allow you to laugh in my face and torment me.”

Suddenly his lips began to tremble, his eyes blazed with fury, and his voice, held back until then, rang out.

“I will not allow it, sir!” he shouted suddenly, banging his fist on the table with all his strength. “Do you hear that, Porfiry Petrovich? I will not allow it!”

“Ah, Lord, what is this again!” cried Porfiry Petrovich, apparently in utter fright. “My dear fellow! Rodion Romanovich! My dear! My good man! What is the matter with you?”

“I will not allow it!” Raskolnikov began to shout a second time.

“My dear fellow, quieter! They’ll hear, they’ll come! Well, what shall we tell them then, think!” Porfiry Petrovich whispered in horror, bringing his face right up to Raskolnikov’s face.

“I will not allow it, I will not allow it!” Raskolnikov repeated mechanically, but also suddenly in a complete whisper.

Porfiry turned quickly and ran to open the window.

“Let in some air, some fresh air! And you ought to drink a little water, my dear, it’s a fit, sir!” And he rushed to the door to order water, but right there in the corner, conveniently, there turned out to be a carafe of water.

“My dear fellow, drink,” he whispered, rushing to him with the carafe, “perhaps it will help...” Porfiry Petrovich’s fright and his very sympathy were so natural that Raskolnikov fell silent and began to examine him with wild curiosity. He did not take the water, however.

“Rodion Romanovich! my dear! why, you will drive yourself out of your mind like this, I assure you, e-eh! A-ah! Do drink! Drink just a little!”

He did make him take the glass of water in his hand. Raskolnikov mechanically raised it to his lips, but, recollecting himself, put it down on the table with disgust.

“Yes, sir, we had a little fit, sir! At this rate, my dear, you will bring your former illness back on yourself,” Porfiry Petrovich clucked with friendly sympathy, though still with a somewhat bewildered look. “Lord! How can you not take care of yourself like that? And there was Dmitry Prokofich, who came to see me yesterday; I agree, I agree, sir, I have a caustic, nasty character, but what they concluded from it!.. Lord! He came yesterday, after you, we had dinner, he talked and talked, and I could only throw up my hands; well, I thought... oh, Lord! Did he come from you? But sit down, my dear fellow, sit down, for Christ’s sake!”

“No, not from me! But I knew that he was going to you, and why he was going,” Raskolnikov answered sharply.

“You knew?”

“I knew. Well, what of it?”

“Why, this, my dear Rodion Romanovich, that I know still other exploits of yours; I am informed about everything, sir! Why, I know how you went to rent the flat, right at nightfall, when it was getting dark, and began ringing the bell, and asked about the blood, and confused the workmen and the caretakers. Why, I understand your state of soul, your state then... but all the same, at this rate you will simply drive yourself out of your mind, by God, sir! You’ll get dizzy! The indignation in you is boiling up very strongly, sir, a noble indignation, sir, at the injuries received, first from fate, and then from the police officers, and so you rush about here and there, so as to make everyone speak out quickly, so to speak, and end everything at once, because you are sick of this foolishness and all these suspicions. Isn’t that so? Have I guessed your state of mind, sir?.. Only at this rate you will make not only yourself dizzy, but Razumikhin too; he is far too kind a man for this, you know yourself. With you it is illness, and with him it is virtue, and the illness turns out catching for him... I will tell you, my dear fellow, when you have calmed down... but sit down, my dear fellow, for Christ’s sake! Please, rest; you look terrible; do sit down.”

Raskolnikov sat down; his trembling was passing, and he was breaking into a heat all over his body. In deep amazement he listened intently to Porfiry Petrovich, who was frightened and fussing over him like a friend. But he did not believe a single word of his, though he felt some strange inclination to believe. Porfiry’s unexpected words about the flat had completely astounded him. “How is it, then, he knows about the flat?” he suddenly thought, “and he is telling me about it himself!”

“Yes, sir, there was such a case, almost exactly, a psychological one, in our legal practice, sir, such a morbid case, sir,” Porfiry went on in a patter. “A man also accused himself falsely of a murder, sir, and how he accused himself: he produced a whole hallucination, presented facts, told circumstances, muddled and confused each and everyone, and why? He himself, quite unintentionally, partly, had been the cause of a murder, but only partly, and when he found out that he had given the murderers an occasion, he grew melancholy, his mind clouded, he began to imagine things, he went quite off his head, and he convinced himself that he was the murderer! But the Governing Senate at last examined the case, and the unhappy man was acquitted and put under care. Thanks to the Governing Senate! Eh, dear me, ai-ai-ai! But what is this, my dear fellow? At this rate one can catch a fever, when such urges to irritate one’s nerves appear, going to ring bells at night and asking about blood! Why, I have studied all this psychology in practice, sir. Sometimes, you know, a man is drawn to jump out of a window or off a bell tower, and the sensation is so tempting. And the bells too, sir... Illness, Rodion Romanovich, illness! You have begun to neglect your illness too much, sir. You should consult an experienced medical man; what good is that fat fellow of yours!.. You are delirious! All this happens to you simply in delirium!..”

For a moment everything spun round Raskolnikov.

“Can it be, can it be,” flashed through him, “that he is lying even now? Impossible, impossible!” he pushed the thought away from him, feeling beforehand to what a degree of fury and rage it could bring him, feeling that he could go out of his mind with fury.

“It was not in delirium, it was awake!” he cried, straining all the powers of his reason to penetrate Porfiry’s game. “Awake, awake! Do you hear?”

“Yes, I understand and hear, sir! You said yesterday too that it was not in delirium; you even insisted especially that it was not in delirium! I understand everything you can say, sir! E-eh!.. But listen, Rodion Romanovich, my benefactor, take just this circumstance. Why, if you really were, in actual fact, guilty, or somehow mixed up in this cursed business, well, would you, I ask you, insist yourself that you did all this not in delirium but, on the contrary, in full consciousness? And insist especially, with such peculiar stubbornness, insist; well, could it be, well, could it be, I ask you? Why, quite the contrary, in my opinion. Why, if you felt anything against yourself, you ought precisely to insist: that it was certainly in delirium! Isn’t that so? It is, isn’t it?”

Something sly could be heard in this question. Raskolnikov recoiled right against the back of the sofa from Porfiry, who was leaning over him, and examined him silently, point-blank, in bewilderment.

“Or take Mr Razumikhin, that is, whether he came to talk yesterday on his own or at your prompting? Why, you ought precisely to say that he came on his own and hide that it was at your prompting! And yet you don’t hide it! You actually insist that it was at your prompting!”

Raskolnikov had never insisted on that. A chill ran down his back.

“You keep lying,” he said slowly and weakly, his lips twisted into a sickly smile. “You want to show me again that you know my whole game, that you know all my answers in advance,” he said, himself almost feeling that he was no longer weighing his words as he should, “you want to frighten me... or you are simply laughing at me...”

He went on looking at him point-blank as he said this, and suddenly boundless anger flashed in his eyes again.

“You are lying all the time!” he cried. “You know perfectly well yourself that the best dodge for a criminal is, as far as possible, not to hide what can be left unhidden. I don’t believe you!”

“What a wriggler you are!” giggled Porfiry. “Why, there is no managing you, my dear fellow; some monomania has got hold of you. So you don’t believe me? And I tell you that you already do believe me, you already believe me by a quarter of a yard, and I will make you believe the whole yard, because I truly love you and sincerely wish you well.”

Raskolnikov’s lips trembled.

“Yes, sir, I do, sir, I tell you finally, sir,” he went on, taking Raskolnikov lightly, in a friendly way, by the arm a little above the elbow, “I tell you finally, sir: look after your illness. Besides, your family has come to you now; think of them. You ought to give them peace and tenderness, and you only frighten them...”

“What business is that of yours? How do you know about it? Why are you so interested? So you are watching me and want to show me that?”

“My dear fellow! Why, I learned everything from you, from you yourself! You don’t notice that, in your agitation, you tell everything in advance yourself, both to me and to others. From Mr Razumikhin, Dmitry Prokofich, I also learned many interesting details yesterday. No, sir, you interrupted me, but I will tell you that through your suspiciousness, with all your wit, you have even lost a sound view of things. Well, for example, on the same subject again, about the bells: why, such a precious thing, such a fact (a whole fact, sir!) I handed over to you just like that, bag and baggage, I, the investigator! And you see nothing in that? Why, if I suspected you even a little, is that how I should have acted? On the contrary, I ought first to have lulled your suspicions and not let on that I already knew of this fact; distracted you, like this, in the opposite direction, and then suddenly, as with an axe butt on the crown (in your own expression), stunned you: ‘And what, sir, were you pleased to be doing in the murdered woman’s flat at ten o’clock at night, or even nearly eleven? And why did you ring the bell? And why did you ask about the blood? And why did you confuse the caretakers and invite them to the police station, to the district lieutenant?’ That is how I ought to have acted if I had even a drop of suspicion of you. I ought to have taken a statement from you in full form, made a search, and perhaps even arrested you... So I have no suspicions of you, since I acted otherwise! But you have lost a sound view and don’t see anything, I repeat, sir!”

Raskolnikov started with his whole body, so that Porfiry Petrovich noticed it all too clearly.

“You are lying all the time!” he cried. “I don’t know your purposes, but you are lying all the time... Earlier you weren’t speaking in that sense, and I can’t be mistaken... You are lying!”

“I am lying?” Porfiry took up, apparently growing heated, but keeping the merriest and most mocking air and seeming not in the least worried about what opinion Mr Raskolnikov had of him. “I am lying?.. Well, and how did I treat you earlier (I, the investigator), prompting you myself and handing you all the means of defence, setting out all this psychology for you myself: ‘Illness, he says, delirium, he had been insulted; melancholy and the police officers’, and all the rest of it? Eh? he-he-he! Though, by the way, I will say in passing, all these psychological means of defence, these excuses and dodges, are extremely unsound, and cut both ways: ‘Illness, delirium, dreams, I imagined it, I don’t remember’; all that is so, sir, but why, my dear fellow, in illness and delirium, does one imagine precisely such dreams and not others? There could have been others, couldn’t there, sir? Isn’t that so? He-he-he-he!”

Raskolnikov looked at him proudly and with contempt.

“In a word,” he said insistently and loudly, standing up and pushing Porfiry slightly as he did so, “in a word, I want to know: do you acknowledge me finally free of suspicion or not? Speak, Porfiry Petrovich, speak positively and finally, and quickly, now!”

“What a business! Well, what a business you are,” cried Porfiry with a perfectly merry, sly and not in the least worried air. “And why do you need to know, why do you need to know so much, when they haven’t even begun to trouble you at all! Why, you are like a child: give me, give me fire to hold! And why are you so worried? Why do you keep thrusting yourself on us like this, for what reasons? Eh? he-he-he!”

“I repeat to you,” Raskolnikov cried in fury, “that I cannot bear it any longer...”

“Bear what, sir? The uncertainty?” Porfiry interrupted.

“Don’t sting me! I don’t want it!.. I tell you I don’t want it!.. I can’t and I don’t want to!.. Do you hear! Do you hear!” he shouted, banging his fist on the table again.

“Quieter, quieter! They’ll hear! I warn you seriously: take care of yourself. I am not joking, sir!” said Porfiry in a whisper, but this time there was no longer in his face the womanish good-natured and frightened expression of before; on the contrary, now he was simply giving orders, sternly, frowning, as if at one stroke breaking through all secrets and ambiguities. But that was only for an instant. Raskolnikov, taken aback, suddenly fell into a real frenzy; but strangely: again he obeyed the order to speak more quietly, though he was in the most violent paroxysm of fury.

“I will not let myself be tormented!” he began whispering suddenly as before, realising in an instant, with pain and hatred, that he could not help submitting to the order, and coming to still greater fury at the thought. “Arrest me, search me, but be so good as to act according to form, and not play with me, sir! Don’t you dare...”

“But don’t worry about form,” Porfiry interrupted, with his former sly smile and as if even admiring Raskolnikov with relish. “I have invited you now, my dear fellow, in a homely way, quite in a friendly way, like this!”

“I don’t want your friendship and I spit on it! Do you hear? And here: I take my cap and go. Well, what will you say now, if you mean to arrest me?”

He seized his cap and went to the door.

“But don’t you want to see the little surprise?” giggled Porfiry, again seizing him a little above the elbow and stopping him at the door. He was visibly growing merrier and more playful, which finally drove Raskolnikov beside himself.

“What little surprise? what is it?” he asked, suddenly stopping and looking at Porfiry in fright.

“A little surprise, sir, sitting here behind my door, he-he-he!” (He pointed with his finger at the locked door in the partition, which led to his government lodging.) “I have even locked it, so that it won’t run away.”

“What is it? where? what?..” Raskolnikov went up to the door and wanted to open it, but it was locked.

“It’s locked, sir; here is the key!”

And indeed he showed him the key, taking it out of his pocket.

“You are lying all the time!” Raskolnikov howled, no longer holding back. “You’re lying, you damned Punchinello!” and he flung himself at Porfiry, who was retreating towards the door, but was not in the least frightened.

“I understand everything, everything!” He sprang up to him. “You are lying and teasing me, so that I’ll give myself away...”

“But you can’t give yourself away any further, my dear Rodion Romanych. You have fallen into a frenzy. Don’t shout, or I shall call people, sir!”

“You’re lying, nothing will happen! Call people! You knew I was ill and wanted to irritate me, to the point of fury, so that I would give myself away; that is your purpose! No, produce your facts! I have understood everything! You have no facts, you have only trashy, worthless guesses, Zamyotov’s guesses!.. You knew my character, you wanted to drive me to a frenzy, and then suddenly stun me with priests and deputies... Are you waiting for them? eh? What are you waiting for? Where? Produce them!”

“What deputies, my dear fellow! The things a man will imagine! Why, it is impossible even to act according to form, as you say; you don’t know the business, my dear... But form will not run away, sir, you’ll see for yourself!..” muttered Porfiry, listening at the door.

Indeed, at that moment something like a noise was heard right at the door in the other room.

“Ah, they’re coming!” cried Raskolnikov. “You sent for them!.. You were waiting for them! You calculated... Well, bring them all here: deputies, witnesses, whatever you like... come on! I’m ready! ready!..”

But here a strange incident occurred, something so unexpected in the ordinary course of things that neither Raskolnikov nor Porfiry Petrovich could, of course, have counted on such an outcome.
