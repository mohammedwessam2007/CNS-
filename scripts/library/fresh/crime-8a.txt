## CHAPTER VII

In the middle of the street stood a carriage, smart and fit for a gentleman, drawn by a pair of spirited grey horses; there were no passengers, and the coachman himself had got down from the box and was standing beside it; the horses were being held by the bridles. A great crowd of people pressed around, with the police in front of them all. One of them had a lighted lantern in his hand, with which, bending down, he was lighting up something on the roadway, right by the wheels. Everyone was talking, shouting, gasping; the coachman seemed bewildered and kept repeating now and then:

“What a sin! Lord, what a sin!”

Raskolnikov pushed his way through as far as he could, and at last saw the object of all this bustle and curiosity. On the ground lay a man who had just been run down by the horses, unconscious by the look of it, very badly dressed, but in “gentleman’s” clothes, all covered in blood. Blood was flowing from his face, from his head; his face was all battered, scraped and mangled. It was clear that he had been run over in earnest.

“Good Lord!” the coachman lamented. “How could I have seen him in time! If I had been driving fast, or hadn’t shouted to him; but I was driving not in a hurry, steady. Everyone saw it: people may lie, but not me. A drunk man won’t light a candle to God, that’s well known!.. I see him, he’s crossing the street, staggering, nearly falling; I shouted once, and again, and a third time, and held the horses in; and he fell straight under their feet! Whether he did it on purpose, or he was very far gone... The horses are young, skittish; they pulled, and he cried out, and they pulled worse... and there’s the trouble.”

“That’s just how it was!” came a witness’s voice from the crowd.

“He shouted, it’s true, he shouted to him three times,” said another voice.

“Exactly three times, everybody heard!” cried a third.

The coachman, however, was not very downcast or frightened. It was evident that the carriage belonged to a rich and important owner who was waiting for its arrival somewhere; the police, of course, were very concerned about how to settle this last circumstance. The injured man had to be taken to the police station and the hospital. No one knew his name.

Meanwhile Raskolnikov had pushed his way through and bent down still closer. Suddenly the lantern lit up the unhappy man’s face brightly; he recognised him.

“I know him, I know him!” he shouted, squeezing right to the front. “He’s an official, retired, a titular councillor, Marmeladov! He lives here, near by, in Kozel’s house... A doctor, quickly! I’ll pay, look!” He pulled money out of his pocket and showed it to a policeman. He was in amazing agitation.

The police were glad to have found out who the injured man was. Raskolnikov gave his own name too, gave his address, and with all his might, as if it were his own father, urged them to carry the unconscious Marmeladov to his lodging as quickly as possible.

“Here, three houses along,” he fussed, “Kozel’s house, a German, a rich one... He was probably drunk just now, making his way home. I know him... He’s a drunkard... He has a family there, a wife, children, a daughter. It will take time to drag him to the hospital, and there’s sure to be a doctor in the house! I’ll pay, I’ll pay!.. At least he’ll have his own people to look after him, they’ll help him at once, or else he’ll die before he gets to the hospital...”

He even managed to slip something unnoticed into a hand; the matter, however, was clear and lawful, and in any case help was nearer here. The injured man was lifted and carried; helpers were found. Kozel’s house was about thirty paces away. Raskolnikov walked behind, carefully holding up the head and showing the way.

“This way, this way! On the stairs you must carry him head first; turn him round... that’s it! I’ll pay, I’ll thank you,” he muttered.

Katerina Ivanovna, as always as soon as she had a free minute, had at once begun walking up and down her little room, from the window to the stove and back, her arms tightly folded across her breast, talking to herself and coughing. Lately she had begun to talk more and more often with her eldest girl, ten-year-old Polenka, who, though there was still much she did not understand, had understood very well that her mother needed her, and so she always followed her with her big, clever eyes and tried with all her might to pretend that she understood everything. This time Polenka was undressing her little brother, who had been unwell all day, to put him to bed. While waiting for his shirt to be changed (it was to be washed that same night), the boy sat on a chair in silence, with a serious face, upright and motionless, his little legs stretched out in front of him, pressed tightly together, heels towards the company and toes turned out. He was listening to what Mama was saying to his sister, pouting his lips, his eyes wide open and not moving, exactly as all clever little boys are supposed to sit when they are being undressed to go to bed. A girl still smaller than he, in utter rags, stood by the screen waiting her turn. The door onto the stairs was open, to give some protection at least against the waves of tobacco smoke that burst in from the other rooms and kept making the poor consumptive cough long and painfully. Katerina Ivanovna seemed to have grown even thinner that week, and the red spots on her cheeks burned brighter than before.

“You wouldn’t believe, you can’t even imagine, Polenka,” she was saying as she walked about the room, “how gaily and grandly we lived in Papa’s house, and how this drunkard has ruined me and will ruin you all! Papa was a colonel in the civil service and very nearly a governor; he had only one more step to take, so that everyone came to see him and said: ‘We already regard you, Ivan Mikhailych, as our governor.’ When I... khe! when I... khe-khe-khe... oh, accursed life!” she cried, coughing up phlegm and clutching her chest. “When I... ah, when at the last ball... at the marshal of nobility’s... Princess Bezzemelnaya saw me (she who gave me her blessing afterwards, when I married your papa, Polya), she asked at once: ‘Isn’t that the charming girl who danced the shawl dance at the leaving ceremony?’... (That tear must be mended; you should take a needle and darn it at once, as I taught you, or else tomorrow... khe! tomorrow... khe-khe-khe!.. it will tear worse!” she cried, straining herself)... “Then Prince Shchegolskoy, a gentleman of the bedchamber, had just come from Petersburg... he danced the mazurka with me and wanted to come the very next day to propose; but I thanked him myself in flattering terms and said that my heart had long belonged to another. That other was your father, Polya; Papa was terribly angry... Is the water ready? Well, give me the little shirt; and the stockings?.. Lida,” she turned to her little daughter, “you must sleep like that tonight, without your shirt; somehow... and put your stockings out beside it... I’ll wash them at the same time... Why doesn’t that ragamuffin come home, the drunkard! He has worn his shirt like a dishcloth, torn it all to pieces... I’d do it all at once, so as not to be tormented two nights running! Lord! Khe-khe-khe-khe! Again! What is it?” she cried, looking at the crowd in the passage and at the people pushing their way into her room with some burden. “What is it? What are they carrying? Lord!”

“Where shall we put him?” asked the policeman, looking round, when they had already dragged the bloodstained, unconscious Marmeladov into the room.

“On the sofa! Put him right on the sofa, with his head here,” Raskolnikov pointed.

“Run over in the street! Drunk!” someone shouted from the passage.

Katerina Ivanovna stood there quite pale, breathing with difficulty. The children were frightened. Little Lidochka screamed, rushed to Polenka, clung to her and trembled all over.

When he had laid Marmeladov down, Raskolnikov rushed to Katerina Ivanovna:

“For God’s sake, calm yourself, don’t be frightened!” he said, rattling it off. “He was crossing the street, a carriage ran him down; don’t worry, he’ll come to, I had him carried here... I was here before, remember... He’ll come to, I’ll pay!”

“He’s done it now!” Katerina Ivanovna cried in despair, and rushed to her husband.

Raskolnikov soon noticed that this woman was not one of those who faint at once. In an instant a pillow was under the unhappy man’s head, which no one had yet thought of; Katerina Ivanovna began to undress him, to examine him; she bustled about and did not lose her head, forgetting about herself, biting her trembling lips and stifling the cries that were ready to burst from her breast.

Meanwhile Raskolnikov persuaded someone to run for a doctor. A doctor, it turned out, lived next door but one.

“I have sent for a doctor,” he kept telling Katerina Ivanovna. “Don’t worry, I’ll pay. Isn’t there any water?.. And give me a napkin, a towel, something, quickly; we don’t know yet how badly he is hurt... He is hurt, not killed, you may be sure... We’ll see what the doctor says!”

Katerina Ivanovna rushed to the window; there, on a broken-down chair in the corner, stood a big earthenware basin of water, ready for the night washing of the children’s and her husband’s linen. This night washing was done by Katerina Ivanovna herself, with her own hands, at least twice a week and sometimes more often, for they had come to such a point that there was almost no change of linen left at all, and each member of the family had only one of everything; and Katerina Ivanovna could not bear dirt, and would rather torment herself at night, beyond her strength, when everyone was asleep, so as to dry the wet linen by morning on a line stretched across the room and give them clean things, than see dirt in the house. She seized the basin to carry it as Raskolnikov asked, but almost fell with the weight. But he had already found a towel, wetted it with water, and begun to wash Marmeladov’s blood-covered face. Katerina Ivanovna stood right there, painfully drawing breath and holding her chest with her hands. She needed help herself. Raskolnikov began to understand that perhaps he had done badly in persuading them to carry the injured man here. The policeman too stood there in perplexity.

“Polya!” cried Katerina Ivanovna. “Run to Sonya, quickly. If you don’t find her at home, never mind, say that Father has been run over by horses and that she must come here at once... as soon as she gets back. Quickly, Polya! Here, cover yourself with the shawl!”

“Wun as fast as you can!” the boy suddenly shouted from his chair, and having said it, sank back into his former silent, upright sitting on the chair, with eyes wide open, heels forward and toes apart.

Meanwhile the room had filled so that there was no room for an apple to fall. The police left, except one, who stayed for the time being and tried to drive the people who had gathered from the stairs back onto the stairs. On the other hand, almost all Madame Lippewechsel’s lodgers poured out of the inner rooms; at first they only crowded in the doorway, but then they surged in a crowd into the room itself. Katerina Ivanovna flew into a frenzy.

“You might at least let him die in peace!” she shouted at the whole crowd. “What a show you have found! With cigarettes! Khe-khe-khe! Why not come in with your hats on!.. And there is one in his hat... Get out! At least have some respect for the dying!”

The coughing choked her, but the scolding did its work. Katerina Ivanovna was evidently even a little feared; the lodgers, one after another, squeezed back to the door with that strange inner sense of satisfaction which is always to be noticed, even in those nearest to us, when sudden misfortune strikes a neighbour, and from which not a single person, without exception, is free, in spite of even the most sincere feeling of pity and sympathy.

From behind the door, however, voices could be heard about the hospital, and that there was no point in disturbing people here for nothing.

“No point in dying, is there!” cried Katerina Ivanovna, and she was about to rush and open the door to burst upon them with a whole thunderstorm, but in the doorway she ran into Madame Lippewechsel herself, who had only just heard of the misfortune and had come running to put things in order. She was an extremely quarrelsome and muddle-headed German woman.

“Ach, mein Gott!” she clasped her hands. “Your husband drunk horse trample. To hospital vith him! I am ze landlady!”

“Amalia Ludwigovna! I beg you to remember what you are saying,” Katerina Ivanovna began haughtily (she always spoke to her landlady in a haughty tone, so that she should ‘remember her place’, and even now she could not deny herself this pleasure). “Amalia Ludwigovna...”

“I told you vonce and for all zat you never dare call me Amal Ludwigovna; I am Amal-Ivan!”

“You are not Amal-Ivan but Amalia Ludwigovna, and since I am not one of your vile flatterers, like Mr Lebezyatnikov, who is laughing now behind the door” (behind the door there was indeed laughter and a shout: “They’re at it!”), “I shall always call you Amalia Ludwigovna, though I absolutely cannot understand why you dislike that name. You can see for yourself what has happened to Semyon Zakharovich; he is dying. I beg you to shut this door at once and let no one in here. Let him at least die in peace! Otherwise, I assure you, your conduct will be known tomorrow to the Governor-General himself. The Prince knew me when I was a girl, and remembers Semyon Zakharovich very well, and did him many favours. Everyone knows that Semyon Zakharovich had many friends and patrons, whom he himself gave up out of noble pride, feeling his unhappy weakness; but now” (she pointed to Raskolnikov) “a generous young man is helping us, who has means and connections, and whom Semyon Zakharovich knew as a child, and you may be sure, Amalia Ludwigovna...”

All this was uttered in an extraordinary patter, faster and faster, but coughing suddenly broke off Katerina Ivanovna’s eloquence. At that moment the dying man came to himself and groaned, and she ran to him. The injured man opened his eyes and, still not recognising or understanding, began to stare at Raskolnikov, who was standing over him. He was breathing heavily, deeply and slowly; blood had oozed out at the corners of his lips; sweat stood out on his forehead. Not recognising Raskolnikov, he began to look around uneasily. Katerina Ivanovna looked at him with a sad but stern gaze, and tears flowed from her eyes.

“My God! His whole chest is crushed! The blood, the blood!” she said in despair. “We must take all his outer clothes off! Turn a little, Semyon Zakharovich, if you can,” she cried to him.

Marmeladov recognised her.

“A priest!” he said in a hoarse voice.

Katerina Ivanovna went to the window, leaned her forehead against the window frame, and exclaimed in despair:

“Oh, accursed life!”

“A priest!” the dying man said again after a minute’s silence.

“They’ve gone for one!” Katerina Ivanovna shouted at him; he obeyed the shout and fell silent. With a timid, anguished look he sought her with his eyes; she came back to him again and stood at the head of the sofa. He grew a little calmer, but not for long. Soon his eyes rested on little Lidochka (his favourite), who was trembling in the corner as if in a fit and looking at him with her astonished, childishly intent eyes.

“Ah... ah...” he pointed at her uneasily. He wanted to say something.

“What now?” cried Katerina Ivanovna.

“Barefoot! Barefoot!” he muttered, pointing with a crazed look at the little girl’s bare feet.

“Be quie-e-et!” Katerina Ivanovna cried irritably. “You know yourself why she’s barefoot!”

“Thank God, the doctor!” cried Raskolnikov, relieved.

The doctor came in, a neat little old man, a German, looking about him with a distrustful air; he went up to the injured man, took his pulse, carefully felt his head and, with Katerina Ivanovna’s help, unfastened the whole blood-soaked shirt and bared the man’s chest. The whole chest was mangled, crushed and torn; several ribs on the right side were broken. On the left side, right over the heart, was an ominous, large, yellowish-black mark, a cruel blow from a hoof. The doctor frowned. The policeman told him that the man had been caught in the wheel and dragged, turning round and round, about thirty paces along the roadway.

“It’s surprising that he came to at all,” the doctor whispered softly to Raskolnikov.

“What do you say?” he asked.

“He will die in a moment.”

“Is there really no hope?”

“Not the slightest! He is at his last breath... Besides, his head is very dangerously hurt... Hm. Perhaps I could bleed him... but... it will be useless. In five or ten minutes he will certainly die.”

“Then bleed him, better!”

“Very well... But I warn you, it will be completely useless.”

At that moment more steps were heard, the crowd in the passage parted, and on the threshold appeared a priest with the sacrament, a grey-haired little old man. A policeman had gone for him, still from the street. The doctor at once gave up his place to him and exchanged a significant look with him. Raskolnikov begged the doctor to wait a little at least. He shrugged his shoulders and stayed.

Everyone stepped back. The confession lasted a very short time. The dying man hardly understood anything well; he could only utter broken, indistinct sounds. Katerina Ivanovna took Lidochka, lifted the boy from his chair and, going into the corner by the stove, knelt down and made the children kneel in front of her. The little girl only trembled; but the boy, kneeling on his bare little knees, raised his little hand in measured movements, crossed himself fully and bowed to the ground, knocking his forehead on the floor, which apparently gave him special pleasure. Katerina Ivanovna bit her lips and held back her tears; she too was praying, now and then straightening the child’s little shirt, and she managed to throw over the girl’s too bare shoulders a scarf which she took from the chest of drawers without getting up from her knees and while still praying. Meanwhile the doors from the inner rooms began to be opened again by the curious. In the passage, the onlookers, lodgers from the whole staircase, crowded closer and closer, not, however, stepping over the threshold of the room. A single candle end lit the whole scene.

At that moment Polenka, who had run for her sister, pushed her way quickly through the crowd from the passage. She came in, hardly able to breathe from running fast, took off her shawl, looked for her mother, went up to her and said: “She’s coming! I met her in the street!” Her mother pulled her down onto her knees and put her beside her. From the crowd, soundlessly and timidly, a girl pushed her way forward, and her sudden appearance in that room, among poverty, rags, death and despair, was strange. She too was in rags; her finery was cheap, but decked out in the street fashion, according to the taste and rules that have grown up in their own special world, with a bright and shameful purpose standing out. Sonya stopped in the passage right by the threshold, but did not cross the threshold, and looked like someone lost, seeming not to be aware of anything, having forgotten both her silk dress, bought fourth-hand, coloured and improper here, with its very long and ridiculous train, and her immense crinoline, which blocked the whole door, and her light-coloured boots, and the parasol, useless at night, which she had brought with her, and the ridiculous round straw hat with a bright flame-coloured feather. From under this hat, worn tilted to one side like a boy’s, there peeped out a thin, pale, frightened little face with its mouth open and eyes fixed with horror. Sonya was small, about eighteen, thin, but a rather pretty blonde, with remarkable blue eyes. She stared at the bed, at the priest; she too was breathless from walking fast. At last the whispering, some words in the crowd, probably reached her. She lowered her eyes, took a step over the threshold, and stood in the room, but again right in the doorway.

The confession and communion were over. Katerina Ivanovna went up to her husband’s bed again. The priest stepped back, and as he left he turned to say a few words of farewell and comfort to Katerina Ivanovna.

“And where am I to put these ones?” she interrupted sharply and irritably, pointing to the little ones.

“God is merciful; hope for the help of the Most High,” the priest began.

“Eh-eh! He is merciful, but not to us!”

“That is a sin, a sin, madam,” the priest remarked, shaking his head.

“And isn’t this a sin?” cried Katerina Ivanovna, pointing to the dying man.

“Perhaps those who were the involuntary cause will agree to compensate you, at least for the loss of income...”

“You don’t understand me!” Katerina Ivanovna cried irritably, waving her hand. “And compensate for what? He crawled under the horses himself, drunk! What income? He brought us no income, only torment. Why, the drunkard drank everything away. He robbed us and carried it to the tavern; he wasted their lives and mine in the tavern! And thank God he is dying! It’s less loss!”

“You should forgive in the hour of death; this is a sin, madam, such feelings are a great sin!”

Katerina Ivanovna was busy about the injured man; she gave him something to drink, wiped the sweat and blood from his head, straightened his pillows, and talked with the priest, now and then managing to turn to him in between. But now she suddenly fell on him almost in a frenzy.

“Eh, Father! Words and nothing but words! Forgive! If he had come home drunk today, if he hadn’t been run over, with only the one shirt on him, all worn out and in rags, he would have flopped down to snore, and I would have been splashing in water till dawn, washing his rags and the children’s, and then I would have dried them outside the window, and then, as soon as it was light, I would have sat down to darn them: that is my night!.. So why talk about forgiveness here! I have forgiven him as it is!”

A deep, terrible cough broke off her words. She coughed into her handkerchief and thrust it out for the priest to see, painfully holding her chest with her other hand. The handkerchief was all covered in blood...

The priest bowed his head and said nothing.

Marmeladov was in his last agony; he did not take his eyes off the face of Katerina Ivanovna, who was bending over him again. He kept wanting to tell her something; he even began, moving his tongue with an effort and pronouncing the words indistinctly, but Katerina Ivanovna, understanding that he wanted to ask her forgiveness, at once shouted at him imperiously:

“Be quie-e-et! There’s no need!.. I know what you want to say!..” And the injured man fell silent; but at that very moment his wandering gaze fell on the door, and he saw Sonya...

Until then he had not noticed her: she was standing in the corner, in the shadow.

“Who is that? Who is that?” he said suddenly in a hoarse, gasping voice, all in alarm, pointing with his eyes in horror at the door where his daughter was standing, and struggling to raise himself.

“Lie down! Lie dow-n!” Katerina Ivanovna began to shout.

But with an unnatural effort he managed to prop himself on his arm. He looked wildly and fixedly at his daughter for some time, as if not recognising her. And indeed he had never once seen her in such clothes. Suddenly he recognised her, humbled, crushed, dressed up and ashamed, meekly waiting her turn to say goodbye to her dying father. Infinite suffering showed in his face.

“Sonya! Daughter! Forgive me!” he cried, and tried to stretch out his hand to her; but losing his support, he slipped and crashed from the sofa, face down on the floor; they rushed to lift him and laid him down, but he was already passing. Sonya gave a faint cry, ran to him, put her arms round him, and froze like that in this embrace. He died in her arms.

“He has got what he wanted!” cried Katerina Ivanovna, seeing her husband’s body. “Well, what am I to do now! What am I to bury him with! And what, what am I to feed them with tomorrow?”

Raskolnikov went up to Katerina Ivanovna.

“Katerina Ivanovna,” he began, “last week your late husband told me his whole life and all the circumstances... Be assured that he spoke of you with rapturous respect. From that evening, when I learned how devoted he was to you all, and how he especially respected and loved you, Katerina Ivanovna, in spite of his unhappy weakness, from that evening we became friends... Allow me now... to do something towards paying my debt to my late friend. Here... twenty roubles, I think; and if this can be of help to you, then... I... in a word, I will come round, I will certainly come round... perhaps I will come round tomorrow... Goodbye!”

And he went quickly out of the room, pushing his way as fast as he could through the crowd to the stairs; but in the crowd he suddenly ran into Nikodim Fomich, who had heard of the accident and wished to give orders in person. They had not seen each other since the scene at the office, but Nikodim Fomich recognised him at once.

“Ah, it’s you?” he asked him.

“He is dead,” answered Raskolnikov. “The doctor came, the priest came, everything is in order. Don’t trouble the poor woman too much; she is consumptive as it is. Cheer her up, if you can in any way... You are a kind man, I know...” he added with a smile, looking him straight in the eyes.

“But how you have got yourself soaked in blood,” remarked Nikodim Fomich, noticing by the light of the lantern several fresh stains on Raskolnikov’s waistcoat.

“Yes, I am soaked... I am all over blood!” Raskolnikov said with a peculiar look; then he smiled, nodded, and went down the stairs.

He went down quietly, unhurriedly, all in a fever and, without being aware of it, full of a single, new, immense sensation of full and mighty life that had suddenly flooded in on him. This sensation might be like that of a man condemned to death who is suddenly and unexpectedly told that he is pardoned. Halfway down the stairs the priest, returning home, overtook him; Raskolnikov silently let him go ahead, exchanging a wordless bow with him. But as he was going down the last steps, he suddenly heard hurried footsteps behind him. Someone was catching up with him. It was Polenka; she was running after him and calling: “Listen! Listen!”

He turned round to her. She ran down the last flight and stopped right in front of him, one step above him. A dim light came in from the courtyard. Raskolnikov made out the thin but sweet little face of the girl, smiling at him and looking at him cheerfully, like a child. She had run after him with a message, which she evidently liked very much herself.

“Listen, what is your name?.. And another thing: where do you live?” she asked hurriedly, in a breathless little voice.

He put both hands on her shoulders and looked at her with a kind of happiness. It was so pleasant for him to look at her; he did not know why himself.

“And who sent you?”

“Sister Sonya sent me,” answered the girl, smiling still more cheerfully.

“I knew it was Sister Sonya who sent you.”

“Mama sent me too. When Sister Sonya started sending me, Mama came up too and said: ‘Run quickly, Polenka!’”

“Do you love Sister Sonya?”

“I love her more than anyone!” Polenka said with a peculiar firmness, and her smile suddenly became more serious.

“And will you love me?”

Instead of an answer he saw the little girl’s face coming closer to him, and her plump little lips naively held out to kiss him. Suddenly her arms, thin as matchsticks, hugged him tight, tight, her head bent onto his shoulder, and the little girl began to cry softly, pressing her face to him more and more tightly.

“I’m sorry for Papa!” she said a minute later, raising her tear-stained little face and wiping away the tears with her hands. “Such misfortunes keep happening now,” she added unexpectedly, with that especially solid air that children try hard to put on when they suddenly want to talk like “grown-ups”.

“And did your papa love you?”

“He loved Lidochka more than all of us,” she went on very seriously and without smiling, now exactly as grown-ups talk, “he loved her because she’s little, and also because she’s ill, and he always brought her presents; and he taught us to read, and me grammar and scripture,” she added with dignity, “and Mama didn’t say anything, but we knew she liked it, and Papa knew too, and Mama wants to teach me French, because it’s time I got an education.”

“And do you know how to pray?”

“Oh, of course we do! For a long time now; I pray to myself, because I’m big now, and Kolya and Lidochka pray aloud with Mama; first they say ‘Mother of God’, and then another prayer: ‘God, forgive and bless Sister Sonya’, and then another: ‘God, forgive and bless our other papa’, because our older papa has died already, and this one is our other one, and we pray for that one too.”

“Polechka, my name is Rodion; pray for me too sometime: ‘and thy servant Rodion’, nothing more.”

“I’ll pray for you all my future life,” the little girl said fervently, and suddenly laughed again, rushed to him and hugged him tight again.

Raskolnikov told her his name, gave her his address and promised to come round without fail the next day. The little girl went away completely delighted with him. It was past ten when he came out into the street. Five minutes later he was standing on the bridge, exactly on the spot from which the woman had thrown herself earlier.

“Enough!” he said resolutely and solemnly. “Away with mirages, away with feigned fears, away with phantoms!.. There is life! Have I not just been living? My life has not died yet together with the old woman! God rest her soul and, enough, my dear, it’s time you were at rest! Now it is the reign of reason and light and... and will, and strength... and now we shall see! Now we shall measure our strength!” he added defiantly, as if addressing some dark force and challenging it. “And I had already agreed to live on a square yard of space!

“...I am very weak at this moment, but... I think the whole illness has passed. I knew it would pass when I went out earlier. By the way: Pochinkov’s house is only two steps away. I will certainly go to Razumikhin’s, even if it weren’t two steps... let him win his bet!.. Let him have his fun too; no matter, let him!.. Strength, strength is what is needed: without strength you can take nothing; and strength must be won by strength; that is what they don’t know,” he added proudly and self-confidently, and walked off the bridge, hardly able to move his legs. Pride and self-confidence grew in him every minute; already the next minute he was not the same man he had been the minute before. But what had happened, so special, that had turned him round like this? He did not know himself; like a man clutching at a straw, it had suddenly seemed to him that he too “could live, that there was still life, that his life had not died together with the old woman”. Perhaps he was too hasty in his conclusion, but he did not think about that.

“But I did ask her to remember thy servant Rodion,” suddenly flashed through his head. “Well, that was... just in case!” he added, and laughed at once at his own boyish prank. He was in the most excellent spirits.

He easily found Razumikhin; at Pochinkov’s house the new lodger was already known, and the caretaker showed him the way at once. Already from halfway up the stairs one could make out the noise and lively talk of a large gathering. The door onto the stairs was wide open; shouts and arguments could be heard. Razumikhin’s room was fairly large, and there were about fifteen people there. Raskolnikov stopped in the entrance. Here, behind a partition, two of the landlady’s servants were busy around two big samovars, around bottles, plates and dishes of pie and snacks brought from the landlady’s kitchen. Raskolnikov sent for Razumikhin. He came running in delight. At the first glance one could see that he had drunk an unusual amount, and though Razumikhin could almost never get really drunk, this time something showed.

“Listen,” Raskolnikov hastened to say, “I have only come to tell you that you have won your bet, and that indeed nobody knows what may happen to him. But I can’t come in: I am so weak that I shall fall down in a moment. And so, hello and goodbye! And come to see me tomorrow...”

“Do you know what, I’ll see you home! If you say yourself that you’re weak, then...”

“And your guests? Who is that curly-haired one who just looked in here?”

“That one? The devil knows! A friend of my uncle’s, I suppose, or perhaps he just came by himself... I’ll leave my uncle with them; he’s a most precious man; a pity you can’t meet him now. But anyway, to the devil with them all! They don’t need me now, and I need some fresh air, so, brother, you’ve come just at the right time: two minutes more and I would have got into a fight there, by God! They talk such wild nonsense... You can’t imagine how far a man can talk himself into lies in the end! But then, how can you not imagine it? Don’t we lie ourselves? Well, let them lie: then they won’t lie later on... Sit down a minute, I’ll bring Zosimov.”

Zosimov fell upon Raskolnikov with a kind of eagerness; a kind of special curiosity was noticeable in him; soon his face cleared.

“Bed at once,” he decided, having examined the patient as far as he could, “and you should take one little thing for the night. Will you take it? I prepared it earlier... a little powder.”

“Two, if you like,” answered Raskolnikov.

The powder was taken right there.

“It’s very good that you are taking him home yourself,” Zosimov remarked to Razumikhin. “We shall see what tomorrow brings, but today he’s really not bad at all: a considerable change since earlier. Live and learn...”

“Do you know what Zosimov whispered to me just now, as we were going out?” Razumikhin blurted out as soon as they were in the street. “I’ll tell you everything straight, brother, because they are fools. Zosimov told me to chatter with you on the way and to make you chatter, and then to tell him, because he has an idea... that you are... mad, or close to it. Just imagine! In the first place, you are three times cleverer than he is; in the second, if you are not mad, you can spit on his having such nonsense in his head; and in the third, this lump of meat, a surgeon by profession, has gone crazy now about mental illness, and what finally turned him round about you was your conversation with Zamyotov today.”

“Zamyotov told you everything?”

“Everything, and he did very well. I understand the whole inside story now, and so does Zamyotov... Well, in a word, Rodya... the thing is... I am a tiny bit drunk now... But that’s nothing... the thing is that this idea... do you understand? really was beginning to peck its way out in them... do you understand? That is, none of them dared say it aloud, because it’s the most absurd nonsense, and especially when they arrested that painter, it all burst and went out for ever. But why are they fools? I gave Zamyotov a bit of a thrashing then (that’s between ourselves, brother; please don’t let on by the slightest hint that you know; I have noticed that he is touchy); it was at Laviza’s; but today, today everything became clear. Above all, that Ilya Petrovich! He took advantage of your fainting at the office then, and was ashamed of himself afterwards; I know...”

Raskolnikov listened greedily. Razumikhin was blurting things out in his drunkenness.

“I fainted then because it was stuffy and smelled of oil paint,” said Raskolnikov.

“He explains it, too! And it wasn’t only the paint: the inflammation had been coming on all month; Zosimov is witness! But you can’t imagine how crushed that boy is now! ‘I’m not worth that man’s little finger,’ he says. Yours, that is. He has good feelings sometimes, brother. But the lesson, the lesson you gave him today at the Crystal Palace, that was the height of perfection! You frightened him at first, you drove him to convulsions! You almost made him convinced again of all that hideous nonsense, and then suddenly you stuck your tongue out at him: ‘There, take that!’ Perfection! He is crushed, annihilated now! You’re a master, by God, that’s how to treat them. Eh, I wasn’t there! He was terribly eager to see you now; Porfiry wants to meet you too...”

“Ah... him too... But why have they put me down as mad?”

“Not as mad, that is. I have told you too much, brother, I think... You see, what struck him earlier was that only that one point interests you; now it’s clear why it interests you, knowing all the circumstances... and how it irritated you then, and got mixed up with your illness... I am a little drunk, brother, only the devil knows, he has some idea of his own... I tell you: he has gone crazy about mental illness. But you just spit on it...”

For half a minute both were silent.

“Listen, Razumikhin,” Raskolnikov began, “I want to tell you straight: I have just been with a dead man; an official died... I gave all my money there... and besides, I was kissed just now by a being who, even if I had killed someone, would also... in a word, I saw another being there too... with a fiery feather... but I am rambling; I am very weak; hold me up... the stairs are just here...”

“What’s the matter with you? What’s the matter with you?” asked Razumikhin in alarm.

“My head is spinning a little, only that’s not the point; the point is that I am so sad, so sad! Like a woman... really! Look, what’s that? Look! Look!”

“What is it?”

“Don’t you see? A light in my room, do you see? Through the crack...”

They were already standing at the last flight, beside the landlady’s door, and indeed one could see from below that there was a light in Raskolnikov’s little room.

“Strange! Nastasya, perhaps,” remarked Razumikhin.

“She is never in my room at this time, and she has been asleep long ago, but... it’s all the same to me! Goodbye!”

“What do you mean? I’m seeing you in, we’ll go in together!”

“I know we’ll go in together, but I want to shake your hand here and say goodbye to you here. Well, give me your hand, goodbye!”

“What’s the matter with you, Rodya?”

“Nothing; come on; you’ll be a witness...”

They began to climb the stairs, and the thought flashed through Razumikhin’s mind that Zosimov might be right after all. “Eh! I have upset him with my chatter!” he muttered to himself. Suddenly, as they came up to the door, they heard voices in the room.

“What’s going on here?” cried Razumikhin.

Raskolnikov took hold of the door first and flung it wide open; he opened it and stood on the threshold as if rooted to the spot.

His mother and sister were sitting on his sofa and had been waiting for an hour and a half. Why was it that he had least of all expected them and least of all thought of them, in spite of the news, repeated even that day, that they were setting out, travelling, would arrive at any moment? All that hour and a half they had been vying with each other in questioning Nastasya, who was standing before them even now and had already managed to tell them the whole inside story. They were beside themselves with fright when they heard that he had “run away today”, ill and, as was evident from her story, certainly delirious! “God, what is the matter with him!” Both had been crying; both had gone through the torment of the cross in that hour and a half of waiting.

A joyful, rapturous cry greeted Raskolnikov’s appearance. Both rushed to him. But he stood like a dead man; an unbearable sudden realisation had struck him like thunder. And his arms would not rise to embrace them: they could not. His mother and sister clasped him in their arms, kissed him, laughed, wept... He took a step, swayed, and crashed to the floor in a faint.

Alarm, cries of horror, groans... Razumikhin, who was standing on the threshold, flew into the room, seized the sick man in his powerful arms, and in an instant he was on the sofa.

“It’s nothing, nothing!” he shouted to the mother and sister. “It’s a faint, it’s rubbish! The doctor has only just said that he is much better, that he is perfectly well! Water! There, he is coming to already, there, he has come round!..”

And seizing Dunechka by the arm so that he nearly wrenched it out of joint, he bent her down to look at how “he has come round already”. And both mother and sister looked at Razumikhin as at Providence, with tenderness and gratitude; they had already heard from Nastasya what this “quick young man” had been to their Rodya all through his illness, as Pulkheria Alexandrovna Raskolnikova herself called him that same evening in an intimate talk with Dunya.
