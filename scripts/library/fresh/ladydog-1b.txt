## III

At home in Moscow everything was already wintry; the stoves were lit, and in the mornings, when the children were getting ready for school and drinking their tea, it was dark, and the nanny would light the lamp for a little while. The frosts had already begun. When the first snow falls, on the first day of sledging, it is pleasant to see the white ground and the white roofs; one breathes softly and easily, and at such a time one remembers the days of one’s youth. The old limes and birches, white with hoarfrost, have a good-natured look; they are nearer to the heart than cypresses and palms, and near them one no longer wants to think of mountains and the sea.

Gurov was a Muscovite. He came back to Moscow on a fine frosty day, and when he put on his fur coat and warm gloves and took a walk along Petrovka, and when on Saturday evening he heard the bells ringing, his recent trip and the places he had been lost all their charm for him. Little by little he plunged into Moscow life; now he greedily read three newspapers a day and said he did not read the Moscow papers on principle. He was drawn now to restaurants, clubs, dinner parties and anniversary celebrations, and it flattered him that well-known lawyers and actors came to his house and that at the Doctors’ Club he played cards with a professor. He could already eat a whole helping of Moscow-style fish stew straight from the pan...

In a month or so, it seemed to him, Anna Sergeyevna would be covered with mist in his memory, and only now and then would he dream of her with her touching smile, as he dreamed of the others. But more than a month went by, deep winter came, and everything was as clear in his memory as if he had parted from Anna Sergeyevna only yesterday. And his memories burned more and more strongly. Whether in the quiet of the evening the voices of his children doing their lessons reached him in his study, or he heard a song or an organ in a restaurant, or the blizzard howled in the chimney, suddenly everything would come back to life in his memory: what had happened on the pier, and the early morning with the mist on the mountains, and the steamer from Feodosia, and the kisses. He would walk about the room for a long time, remembering and smiling, and then his memories turned into dreams, and in his imagination the past mingled with what was to come. Anna Sergeyevna did not appear in his dreams; she followed him everywhere like a shadow and watched him. Closing his eyes, he saw her as if she were alive, and she seemed more beautiful, younger, more tender than she had been; and he himself seemed better than he had been then, in Yalta. In the evenings she looked at him from the bookcase, from the fireplace, from the corner; he heard her breathing, the soft rustle of her dress. In the street he followed women with his eyes, looking for one who resembled her...

And now he was tormented by a strong desire to share his memories with someone. But at home he could not talk of his love, and away from home there was no one to talk to. Not with his tenants, surely, nor at the bank. And what was there to say? Had he loved her then? Had there been anything beautiful, poetic, or instructive, or simply interesting in his relations with Anna Sergeyevna? And he had to talk vaguely of love, of women, and nobody guessed what it was about, and only his wife twitched her dark eyebrows and said:

“The part of a ladies’ man doesn’t suit you at all, Dimitry.”

One night, coming out of the Doctors’ Club with his partner at cards, an official, he could not hold back and said:

“If you only knew what a charming woman I met in Yalta!”

The official got into his sledge and drove off, but suddenly turned round and called out:

“Dmitry Dmitrich!”

“What?”

“You were right this evening: the sturgeon was a bit off!”

These words, so ordinary, for some reason suddenly outraged Gurov; they seemed to him degrading and unclean. What savage manners, what faces! What senseless nights, what dull, unremarkable days! Frantic card-playing, gluttony, drunkenness, endless talk always about the same things. Useless affairs and talk always about the same things take up the best part of one’s time, the best of one’s strength, and in the end what is left is a kind of docked, wingless life, a kind of nonsense; and one cannot walk away or escape, just as if one were sitting in a madhouse or a prison gang!

Gurov did not sleep all night, and was indignant, and then spent the whole day with a headache. And the following nights he slept badly; he kept sitting up in bed thinking, or pacing from corner to corner. His children bored him, the bank bored him; he did not want to go anywhere or talk about anything.

In December, during the holidays, he got ready for a journey and told his wife he was going to Petersburg to see about a post for a young man; and he went to S. What for? He hardly knew himself. He wanted to see Anna Sergeyevna and talk to her, to arrange a meeting if he could.

He arrived in S. in the morning and took the best room in the hotel, where the whole floor was covered with grey army cloth, and on the table stood an inkstand, grey with dust, in the shape of a horseman on a horse, with his hand raised holding a hat, and his head broken off. The porter gave him the information he needed: von Diderits lived on Old Goncharnaya Street, in his own house; it was not far from the hotel; he lived well, in style, kept his own horses; everyone in town knew him. The porter pronounced it “Drydyrits”.

Gurov walked unhurriedly to Old Goncharnaya Street and found the house. Right opposite the house stretched a fence, grey, long, with nails along the top.

“A fence like that would make anyone run away,” thought Gurov, looking now at the windows, now at the fence.

He reasoned: today was a holiday, and her husband was probably at home. And anyway, it would be tactless to go into the house and upset her. If he sent a note, it might fall into the husband’s hands, and then everything might be ruined. Best of all to trust to chance. And he kept walking up and down the street, and near the fence, waiting for that chance. He saw a beggar go in at the gate and the dogs fly at him; then, an hour later, he heard someone playing the piano, and the sounds came faint and indistinct. Anna Sergeyevna must be playing. The front door suddenly opened, and out came an old woman, and after her ran the familiar white Pomeranian. Gurov wanted to call the dog, but his heart suddenly began to pound, and in his agitation he could not remember the dog’s name.

He walked about, and hated the grey fence more and more, and was already thinking with irritation that Anna Sergeyevna had forgotten him and was perhaps already amusing herself with someone else; and that would be so natural in the position of a young woman who was forced to look at that damned fence from morning till night. He went back to his room and sat on the sofa for a long time, not knowing what to do; then he had dinner; then he slept for a long time.

“How stupid and upsetting all this is,” he thought when he woke up and looked at the dark windows; it was already evening. “And now I’ve had a good sleep, for some reason. What am I going to do tonight?”

He sat on the bed, which was covered with a cheap grey blanket like a hospital blanket, and taunted himself with vexation:

“So much for your lady with the little dog... So much for your adventure... Now sit here.”

That morning at the station he had noticed a poster in very large letters: *The Geisha* was opening that night. He remembered it and drove to the theatre.

“It’s quite possible she goes to first nights,” he thought.

The theatre was full. Here, as in all provincial theatres, there was a haze above the chandelier, and the gallery was noisy and restless; in the front row, before the performance began, the local dandies stood with their hands behind their backs; and here too, in the governor’s box, the governor’s daughter sat in front in a feather boa, while the governor himself hid modestly behind the curtain, and only his hands could be seen; the curtain swayed, and the orchestra spent a long time tuning up. All the while the audience was coming in and taking their seats, Gurov searched eagerly with his eyes.

Anna Sergeyevna came in too. She sat down in the third row, and when Gurov looked at her his heart contracted, and he understood clearly that for him there was now no one in the whole world closer, dearer or more important; she, lost in this provincial crowd, this little woman, in no way remarkable, with a vulgar lorgnette in her hand, now filled his whole life; she was his grief, his joy, the only happiness he now wished for himself; and to the sounds of the bad orchestra, the wretched provincial violins, he thought how lovely she was. He thought and dreamed.

With Anna Sergeyevna came a young man with short side-whiskers, very tall and stooping, who sat down beside her; he nodded his head at every step and seemed to be constantly bowing. This was probably the husband whom she had called a lackey in Yalta, in a burst of bitter feeling. And indeed, in his long figure, his side-whiskers, his small bald patch, there was something of a lackey’s modesty; he smiled sweetly, and in his buttonhole gleamed some badge of a learned society, like a lackey’s number.

In the first interval the husband went out to smoke; she stayed in her seat. Gurov, who was also sitting in the stalls, went up to her and said in a trembling voice, with a forced smile:

“Good evening.”

She glanced at him and turned pale, then glanced again in horror, not believing her eyes, and gripped her fan and lorgnette tightly together in her hands, evidently struggling with herself not to faint. Both were silent. She sat, and he stood, frightened by her confusion, not daring to sit down beside her. The violins and a flute began to sound as they were tuned; it suddenly became frightening; it seemed that people were looking from every box. But then she got up and walked quickly to the exit; he followed her, and they both walked aimlessly along corridors and up and down stairs, and people in the uniforms of judges, teachers and officials of the imperial estates flashed before their eyes, all wearing badges; ladies flashed by, and fur coats on the pegs; there was a draught, carrying the smell of cigarette ends. And Gurov, whose heart was beating violently, thought:

“Oh, Lord! What are these people for, this orchestra...”

And at that moment he suddenly remembered how, that evening at the station after seeing Anna Sergeyevna off, he had told himself that it was all over and they would never see each other again. But how far it still was from the end!

On a narrow, gloomy staircase with the sign “To the Amphitheatre” she stopped.

“How you frightened me!” she said, breathing heavily, still pale and stunned. “Oh, how you frightened me! I’m barely alive. Why have you come? Why?”

“But understand, Anna, understand...” he said in an undertone, hurriedly. “I beg you, understand...”

She looked at him with fear, with entreaty, with love; she looked at him intently, to keep his features more firmly in her memory.

“I suffer so!” she went on, not listening to him. “All this time I have thought only of you; I have lived by thinking of you. And I wanted to forget, to forget; but why, why have you come?”

Higher up, on the landing, two schoolboys were smoking and looking down, but Gurov did not care; he drew Anna Sergeyevna to him and began kissing her face, her cheeks, her hands.

“What are you doing, what are you doing!” she said in horror, pushing him away. “We have gone mad. Go away today, go away at once... I beg you by all that is holy, I implore you... Someone is coming!”

Someone was coming up the stairs.

“You must go away...” Anna Sergeyevna went on in a whisper. “Do you hear, Dmitry Dmitrich? I will come to you in Moscow. I have never been happy; I am unhappy now, and I shall never, never be happy, never! Don’t make me suffer still more! I swear I will come to Moscow. But now let us part! My dear, my good, my darling, let us part!”

She pressed his hand and began to go quickly down the stairs, looking back at him all the time, and her eyes showed that she really was not happy. Gurov stood for a while, listened, and then, when everything was quiet, found his peg and left the theatre.

## IV

And Anna Sergeyevna began coming to see him in Moscow. Once every two or three months she left S. and told her husband she was going to consult a professor about a women’s complaint, and her husband believed her and did not believe her. When she came to Moscow she stayed at the Slavyansky Bazaar hotel and at once sent a man in a red cap to Gurov. Gurov went to see her, and nobody in Moscow knew about it.

One winter morning he was going to see her like this (the messenger had come the evening before but had not found him in). With him walked his daughter, whom he wanted to take to school; it was on the way. Large, wet snowflakes were falling.

“It’s three degrees above freezing, and yet it’s snowing,” Gurov said to his daughter. “But that warmth is only at the surface of the earth; in the upper layers of the atmosphere the temperature is quite different.”

“Papa, why is there no thunder in winter?”

He explained that too. He talked, and thought that here he was going to a rendezvous and not a living soul knew about it, and probably never would. He had two lives: one open, seen and known by everyone who needed to know, full of conventional truth and conventional deception, exactly like the lives of his friends and acquaintances; and another, which went on in secret. And through some strange coincidence of circumstances, perhaps an accidental one, everything that was important, interesting and necessary to him, everything in which he was sincere and did not deceive himself, everything that made up the core of his life, went on in secret from others; while everything that was his lie, the shell in which he hid himself to conceal the truth, such as his work at the bank, his arguments at the club, his “lower race”, his going with his wife to anniversary celebrations, all that was in the open. And he judged others by himself, did not believe what he saw, and always assumed that every man’s real and most interesting life went on under the cover of secrecy, as under the cover of night. Every personal existence rests on a secret, and perhaps that is partly why civilised people are so nervously anxious that personal secrecy should be respected.

Having seen his daughter to school, Gurov went to the Slavyansky Bazaar. He took off his fur coat downstairs, went up and knocked softly at the door. Anna Sergeyevna, wearing his favourite grey dress, tired from the journey and the waiting, had been expecting him since the evening before; she was pale, looked at him without smiling, and he had hardly come in before she was already pressed against his chest. As if they had not seen each other for two years, their kiss was long, lingering.

“Well, how are you getting on there?” he asked. “What news?”

“Wait, I’ll tell you in a moment... I can’t.”

She could not speak, because she was crying. She turned away from him and pressed her handkerchief to her eyes.

“Well, let her have her cry; I’ll sit down meanwhile,” he thought, and sat down in an armchair.

Then he rang and ordered tea; and then, while he drank his tea, she went on standing with her back to him, facing the window... She was crying from emotion, from the sorrowful awareness that their life had turned out so sadly; they saw each other only in secret, hid from people like thieves! Was not their life broken?

“Come, stop it!” he said.

It was obvious to him that this love of theirs would not end soon; no one knew when. Anna Sergeyevna was growing more and more attached to him; she adored him, and it would have been unthinkable to tell her that all this must surely come to an end one day; and she would not have believed it anyway.

He went up to her and took her by the shoulders, to caress her, to make a joke, and at that moment he saw himself in the mirror.

His hair was already beginning to turn grey. And it seemed strange to him that he had aged so much in the last few years, had lost his looks so. The shoulders on which his hands lay were warm and trembling. He felt compassion for this life, still so warm and beautiful, but probably already near the point where it would begin to fade and wither, like his own life. Why did she love him so? To women he had always seemed other than he was, and they had loved in him not himself but the man their imagination had created and whom they had been eagerly seeking all their lives; and afterwards, when they noticed their mistake, they had loved him all the same. And not one of them had been happy with him. Time had passed; he had met women, become intimate with them, parted from them, but not once had he loved; there had been anything you like, but not love.

And only now, when his hair had turned grey, had he fallen in love properly, truly, for the first time in his life.

Anna Sergeyevna and he loved each other like people very close and dear, like husband and wife, like tender friends; it seemed to them that fate itself had meant them for each other, and it was incomprehensible why he was married and she was married; it was as if they were two birds of passage, a male and a female, who had been caught and forced to live in separate cages. They had forgiven each other what they were ashamed of in their pasts; they forgave everything in the present, and they felt that this love of theirs had changed them both.

Before, in sad moments, he had calmed himself with whatever reasoning came into his head; but now he had no thought for reasoning; he felt a deep compassion; he wanted to be sincere, tender...

“Stop crying, my darling,” he said. “You’ve had your cry, and that’s enough... Now let’s talk; let’s think of something.”

Then they talked it over for a long time, about how to free themselves from the need to hide, to deceive, to live in different towns and not see each other for long stretches. How could they free themselves from these unbearable bonds?

“How? How?” he asked, clutching his head. “How?”

And it seemed that a little more, and the solution would be found, and then a new and beautiful life would begin; and it was clear to both of them that the end was still far, far away, and that the most complicated and difficult part was only just beginning.
