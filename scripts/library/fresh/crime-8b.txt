## PART III · CHAPTER I

Raskolnikov raised himself and sat up on the sofa.

He waved weakly at Razumikhin to stop the whole flood of his incoherent, heated consolations addressed to his mother and sister, took them both by the hands, and for about two minutes looked silently now at one, now at the other. His mother was frightened by his look. In that look there showed a feeling strong to the point of suffering, but at the same time there was something fixed in it, even almost mad. Pulkheria Alexandrovna began to cry.

Avdotya Romanovna was pale; her hand trembled in her brother’s hand.

“Go home... with him,” he said in a broken voice, pointing to Razumikhin. “Until tomorrow; tomorrow, everything... Have you been here long?”

“Since the evening, Rodya,” answered Pulkheria Alexandrovna. “The train was terribly late. But, Rodya, nothing will make me leave you now! I’ll spend the night here, beside you...”

“Don’t torment me!” he said, waving his hand irritably.

“I’ll stay with him!” cried Razumikhin. “I won’t leave him for a minute, and to the devil with all my people there, let them climb the walls! My uncle is presiding over them.”

“How, how can I ever thank you!” Pulkheria Alexandrovna began, pressing Razumikhin’s hands again, but Raskolnikov interrupted her once more:

“I can’t, I can’t,” he kept repeating irritably. “Don’t torment me! Enough, go away... I can’t!..”

“Let’s go, Mama, at least let’s go out of the room for a minute,” whispered the frightened Dunya. “We are killing him, anyone can see it.”

“Am I not even to look at him, after three years!” wept Pulkheria Alexandrovna.

“Wait!” he stopped them again. “You keep interrupting, and my thoughts get muddled... Have you seen Luzhin?”

“No, Rodya, but he already knows we have arrived. We heard, Rodya, that Pyotr Petrovich was so kind as to visit you today,” Pulkheria Alexandrovna added with some timidity.

“Yes... he was so kind... Dunya, earlier today I told Luzhin I would throw him down the stairs, and I sent him to the devil...”

“Rodya, what are you saying! Surely you... you don’t mean to say...” Pulkheria Alexandrovna began in alarm, but stopped, looking at Dunya.

Avdotya Romanovna was gazing intently at her brother, waiting for what came next. Both had already been told of the quarrel by Nastasya, as far as she had been able to understand and tell it, and they had been tormented by bewilderment and suspense.

“Dunya,” Raskolnikov went on with an effort, “I do not want this marriage, and so you must refuse Luzhin tomorrow, at the very first word, so that there isn’t a whiff of him left.”

“My God!” cried Pulkheria Alexandrovna.

“Brother, think what you are saying!” Avdotya Romanovna began hotly, but at once restrained herself. “Perhaps you are not in a fit state just now; you are tired,” she said gently.

“Delirious? No... You are marrying Luzhin for my sake. And I do not accept the sacrifice. And so, by tomorrow, write a letter... refusing him... Give it to me to read in the morning, and that’s the end of it!”

“I cannot do that!” cried the offended girl. “By what right...”

“Dunechka, you are hot-tempered too, stop it, tomorrow... Can’t you see...” the mother cried in fright, rushing to Dunya. “Oh, let us go, it’s better!”

“He’s raving!” shouted the tipsy Razumikhin. “How else would he dare! Tomorrow all this nonsense will jump out of him... But today he really did throw him out. That’s just how it was. Well, and the other one got angry... He made speeches here, showed off his learning, and went away with his tail between his legs...”

“So it’s true?” cried Pulkheria Alexandrovna.

“Until tomorrow, brother,” Dunya said with compassion. “Come, Mama... Goodbye, Rodya!”

“Do you hear, sister,” he repeated after her, gathering his last strength, “I am not delirious; this marriage is a vileness. I may be a scoundrel, but you must not... one of us is enough... and though I am a scoundrel, I would not count such a sister as my sister. It is me or Luzhin! Go...”

“You’ve gone out of your mind! Tyrant!” roared Razumikhin, but Raskolnikov no longer answered, and perhaps had no strength to answer. He lay down on the sofa and turned to the wall in utter exhaustion. Avdotya Romanovna looked at Razumikhin with curiosity; her black eyes flashed: Razumikhin even started under that look. Pulkheria Alexandrovna stood as if thunderstruck.

“I cannot go, nothing will make me!” she whispered to Razumikhin, almost in despair. “I’ll stay here, somewhere... see Dunya home.”

“And spoil everything!” Razumikhin whispered too, losing his temper. “Let’s at least go out onto the stairs. Nastasya, a light! I swear to you,” he went on in a half-whisper, already on the stairs, “that earlier today he nearly beat us, me and the doctor! Do you understand that? The doctor himself! And the doctor gave way so as not to irritate him, and went off, and I stayed downstairs on guard, and he dressed meanwhile and slipped away. And he will slip away now too, if you irritate him, at night, and do something to himself...”

“Oh, what are you saying!”

“And Avdotya Romanovna can’t possibly stay in those rooms alone without you! Think where you are staying! Couldn’t that scoundrel Pyotr Petrovich have found you better lodgings... But you know, I am a little drunk, and so I... swore; don’t pay any...”

“But I will go to the landlady here,” Pulkheria Alexandrovna insisted. “I will beg her to give Dunya and me a corner for tonight. I cannot leave him like this, I cannot!”

As they talked they were standing on the stairs, on the landing, right in front of the landlady’s door. Nastasya was lighting them from the bottom step. Razumikhin was in an extraordinary state of excitement. Half an hour earlier, seeing Raskolnikov home, he had been, though over-talkative (and he knew it), perfectly brisk and almost fresh, in spite of the dreadful amount of wine he had drunk that evening. Now his state was like a kind of rapture, and at the same time it was as if all the wine he had drunk had rushed to his head again, all at once and with doubled force. He stood with both ladies, having seized both of them by the hand, persuading them and laying out his reasons with astonishing frankness, and, probably to be more convincing, at almost every word he squeezed their hands, tight, as in a vice, until it hurt, and he seemed to devour Avdotya Romanovna with his eyes, without the slightest embarrassment. Sometimes, from the pain, they would pull their hands out of his enormous bony paw, but he not only failed to notice what the matter was, he drew them still more firmly towards him. If they had told him at that moment to throw himself head first down the stairs in their service, he would have done it at once, without reasoning or doubting. Pulkheria Alexandrovna, all alarm at the thought of her Rodya, though she felt that the young man was very eccentric indeed and was squeezing her hand far too painfully, nevertheless, since he was at the same time Providence to her, did not wish to notice all these eccentric details. But Avdotya Romanovna, in spite of the same alarm, and though she was not of a timid character, met with amazement and almost with fear the looks blazing with wild fire from her brother’s friend, and only the boundless trust inspired by Nastasya’s stories about this strange man kept her from attempting to run away from him and drag her mother after her. She also understood that perhaps it was impossible to run away from him now. Within ten minutes, however, she had calmed down considerably: Razumikhin had the quality of expressing his whole self in a moment, whatever his mood, so that everyone very soon knew whom they were dealing with.

“Impossible to go to the landlady, and the most dreadful nonsense!” he cried, persuading Pulkheria Alexandrovna. “Though you are his mother, if you stay you will drive him to frenzy, and then the devil knows what will happen! Listen, this is what I’ll do: Nastasya will sit with him now, and I will take you both to your rooms, because you can’t go through the streets alone; here in Petersburg, in that respect... Well, never mind!.. Then from your place I’ll run straight back here, and in a quarter of an hour, on my most honest word, I’ll bring you a report: how is he? asleep or not? and everything else. Then, listen! Then from you I’ll dash home, there are guests at my place, all drunk, I’ll fetch Zosimov, that’s the doctor who is treating him, he is sitting at my place now, not drunk; that one isn’t drunk, that one is never drunk! I’ll drag him to Rodka and then straight to you, so within an hour you will have two reports on him, and from the doctor, you understand, from the doctor himself; that’s not the same as from me! If he’s bad, I swear I’ll bring you here myself, and if he’s well, then you go to bed. And I will spend the whole night here, in the passage, he won’t even hear me, and I’ll tell Zosimov to spend the night at the landlady’s, so as to be at hand. Well, what is better for him now, you or the doctor? Why, the doctor is more useful, more useful. So go home! And going to the landlady is impossible; possible for me, but impossible for you: she won’t let you in, because... because she is a fool. She will be jealous over me with Avdotya Romanovna, if you want to know, and with you too... And with Avdotya Romanovna certainly. It’s a completely, completely unexpected character! Though I am a fool too... Never mind! Let’s go! Do you trust me? Well, do you trust me or not?”

“Let’s go, Mama,” said Avdotya Romanovna. “He will certainly do as he promises. He has already brought my brother back to life, and if it’s true that the doctor will agree to spend the night here, what could be better?”

“There, you... you... understand me, because you are an angel!” cried Razumikhin in rapture. “Let’s go! Nastasya! Upstairs this instant, and sit there with him, with a light; I’ll be back in a quarter of an hour...”

Pulkheria Alexandrovna, though not completely convinced, did not resist any further. Razumikhin took them both by the arm and pulled them down the stairs. He worried her, though: “He may be quick and kind, but is he able to do what he promises? In such a state!..”

“Ah, I understand, you are thinking that I am in such a state!” Razumikhin interrupted her thoughts, guessing them and striding along the pavement with his enormous strides, so that both ladies could hardly keep up with him, which, however, he did not notice. “Nonsense! that is... I am drunk as a blockhead, but that’s not the point; I am not drunk from wine. It was seeing you that went to my head... But never mind me! Don’t pay any attention: I am lying; I am not worthy of you... I am in the highest degree unworthy of you!.. And as soon as I have taken you home, at once, right here in the canal, I’ll pour two tubs of water over my head, and I’ll be ready... If you only knew how I love you both!.. Don’t laugh and don’t be angry!.. Be angry with everyone, but don’t be angry with me! I am his friend, and so I am your friend. That’s what I want... I had a presentiment of this... last year, there was one such moment... Though I had no presentiment at all, because you dropped from the sky. And I won’t sleep all night, I dare say... That Zosimov was afraid earlier that he might go out of his mind... That’s why he must not be irritated...”

“What are you saying!” cried the mother.

“Did the doctor himself really say that?” asked Avdotya Romanovna, frightened.

“He did, but that’s not it, not it at all. He gave him a medicine, too, a powder, I saw it, and then you arrived... Eh!.. You would have done better to come tomorrow! It’s good that we left. And within an hour Zosimov himself will report to you on everything. Now he is not drunk! And I won’t be drunk... And why did I get so sozzled? Because they dragged me into an argument, the damned lot! I had sworn an oath not to argue!.. Such rubbish they talk! I nearly got into a fight! I left my uncle there as chairman... Well, would you believe it: they demand complete impersonality and find the very relish of it in that! Anything so as not to be oneself, anything so as to be as little like oneself as possible! That is what they consider the very highest progress. And if only they lied in their own way, but as it is...”

“Listen,” Pulkheria Alexandrovna interrupted timidly, but this only added fuel to the fire.

“What do you think?” shouted Razumikhin, raising his voice still more. “You think I am against them because they lie? Nonsense! I love it when people lie! Lying is the one human privilege over all other organisms. Tell a lie, and you will get to the truth! It is because I lie that I am a man. Nobody ever got to a single truth without first lying fourteen times, or perhaps a hundred and fourteen, and that is honourable in its way; but we can’t even lie with our own minds! Lie to me, but lie in your own way, and then I will kiss you. To lie in your own way is almost better than to tell the truth in someone else’s way; in the first case you are a man, and in the second you are no better than a bird! The truth won’t run away, but life can be nailed shut; there have been examples. Well, what are we now? All of us, every one without exception, in science, development, thought, invention, ideals, aspirations, liberalism, reason, experience, and everything, everything, everything, everything, everything else, are still sitting in the first preparatory class of the gymnasium! We have taken a liking to living on other people’s minds, and we have got used to it! Isn’t that so? Am I right?” shouted Razumikhin, shaking and squeezing the hands of both ladies. “Am I?”

“Oh my God, I don’t know,” said poor Pulkheria Alexandrovna.

“Yes, yes... though I don’t agree with you in everything,” Avdotya Romanovna added seriously, and at once cried out, so painfully did he squeeze her hand this time.

“Yes? You say yes? Well, after that you... you...” he shouted in rapture, “you are a fountain of goodness, purity, reason and... perfection! Give me your hand, give it... you give me yours too, I want to kiss your hands here, now, on my knees!”

And he knelt down in the middle of the pavement, which, fortunately, was deserted at that moment.

“Stop it, I beg you, what are you doing?” cried Pulkheria Alexandrovna, alarmed to the extreme.

“Get up, get up!” laughed Dunya, alarmed too.

“Not for anything, until you give me your hands! There, that’s it, and that’s enough, and I’m up, and let’s go! I am an unhappy blockhead, I am not worthy of you, and drunk, and ashamed... I am not worthy to love you, but to bow down before you is the duty of everyone, unless he is a complete brute! And I have bowed down... Here are your rooms, and Rodion was right in that alone, that he threw out your Pyotr Petrovich today! How dared he put you in such rooms? It’s a scandal! Do you know who they let in here? And you are a bride! You are his bride, yes? Well then, I tell you, your fiancé is a scoundrel after this!”

“Listen, Mr Razumikhin, you are forgetting yourself...” Pulkheria Alexandrovna began.

“Yes, yes, you are right, I have forgotten myself, I am ashamed!” Razumikhin recollected himself. “But... but... you can’t be angry with me for speaking like this! Because I speak sincerely, and not because... hm! that would be base; in a word, not because I am... hm!.. well, so be it, never mind, I won’t say why, I don’t dare!.. But we all understood earlier, as soon as he came in, that this man was not of our sort. Not because he came in curled at the hairdresser’s, not because he was in a hurry to show off his intelligence, but because he is a spy and a speculator; because he is a Jew and a mountebank, and that shows. Do you think he is clever? No, he is a fool, a fool! Well, is he a match for you? Oh my God! You see, ladies,” he stopped suddenly, already going up the stairs to the rooms, “though they are all drunk at my place, still they are all honest, and though we lie, because I lie too, still we will lie our way through to the truth at last, because we are on an honourable road, and Pyotr Petrovich... is not on an honourable road. Though I was cursing them just now up hill and down dale, I respect them all; even Zamyotov, though I don’t respect him, I love, because he is a puppy! Even that brute Zosimov, because he is honest and knows his work... But enough, everything has been said and forgiven. Forgiven? Is that so? Well, let’s go. I know this corridor, I have been here; there was a scandal here, in number three... Well, where are you? Which number? Eight? Well then, lock yourselves in for the night, let no one in. In a quarter of an hour I’ll be back with news, and then in another half-hour with Zosimov, you’ll see! Goodbye, I’m running!”

“My God, Dunechka, what will come of this?” said Pulkheria Alexandrovna, turning anxiously and fearfully to her daughter.

“Calm yourself, Mama,” answered Dunya, taking off her hat and cape. “God himself has sent us this gentleman, even if he has come straight from some drinking party. He can be relied on, I assure you. And all that he has already done for my brother...”

“Ah, Dunechka, God knows whether he will come! And how could I bring myself to leave Rodya!.. And not at all, not at all how I imagined finding him! How stern he was, as if he were not glad to see us...”

Tears appeared in her eyes.

“No, that’s not so, Mama. You didn’t look closely, you were crying all the time. He is very upset after a serious illness; that is the cause of it all.”

“Ah, this illness! What will happen, what will happen! And how he spoke to you, Dunya!” said the mother, looking timidly into her daughter’s eyes to read all her thoughts, and already half consoled by the fact that Dunya herself was defending Rodya and so must have forgiven him. “I am sure he will think better of it tomorrow,” she added, probing to the end.

“And I am sure that tomorrow too he will say the same thing... about that,” Avdotya Romanovna cut her short, and of course this was the sticking point, because here was the very matter Pulkheria Alexandrovna was now far too afraid to speak of. Dunya went up and kissed her mother. Her mother hugged her tightly in silence. Then she sat down in anxious expectation of Razumikhin’s return, and timidly watched her daughter, who, with folded arms, and waiting too, began walking up and down the room, thinking to herself. Such walking from corner to corner, deep in thought, was Avdotya Romanovna’s habit, and her mother was always somehow afraid to disturb her musing at such times.

Razumikhin, of course, was ridiculous with his sudden passion for Avdotya Romanovna, kindled by drink; but anyone who looked at Avdotya Romanovna, especially now, as she walked about the room with folded arms, sad and thoughtful, might well have excused him, to say nothing of his eccentric state. Avdotya Romanovna was remarkably good-looking: tall, wonderfully well-made, strong, self-assured, which showed in her every gesture and yet took nothing away from the softness and grace of her movements. In face she was like her brother, but she could even be called a beauty. Her hair was dark brown, a little lighter than her brother’s; her eyes almost black, flashing, proud, and at the same time sometimes, for moments, extraordinarily kind. She was pale, but not sickly pale; her face shone with freshness and health. Her mouth was rather small, and her lower lip, fresh and scarlet, jutted forward a very little, together with her chin: the only irregularity in that beautiful face, but one that gave it a special character and, among other things, a kind of haughtiness. The expression of her face was always more serious than merry, thoughtful; but how well a smile suited that face, how well laughter suited her, merry, young, wholehearted laughter! It is understandable that the hot-headed, frank, simple, honest Razumikhin, strong as a hero of the old songs, and drunk, who had never seen anything like her, lost his head at first sight. Besides, chance, as if on purpose, had shown him Dunya for the first time at a beautiful moment of love and joy at meeting her brother. Then he had seen her lower lip quiver with indignation in answer to her brother’s insolent and ungratefully cruel commands, and he could not resist.

He had, however, told the truth when he blurted out drunkenly on the stairs earlier that Raskolnikov’s eccentric landlady, Praskovya Pavlovna, would be jealous over him not only with Avdotya Romanovna but perhaps with Pulkheria Alexandrovna herself. Although Pulkheria Alexandrovna was already forty-three, her face still kept traces of its former beauty; besides, she seemed much younger than her years, which almost always happens with women who have kept clarity of spirit, freshness of impressions and an honest, pure warmth of heart into old age. Let us say in parentheses that to keep all this is the only means of not losing one’s beauty even in old age. Her hair was already beginning to turn grey and thin, little radiating wrinkles had long since appeared around her eyes, her cheeks had grown hollow and dry from care and grief, and still this face was beautiful. It was a portrait of Dunechka’s face, only twenty years later, except for the expression of the lower lip, which did not jut forward in hers. Pulkheria Alexandrovna was sensitive, though not to the point of sentimentality, timid and yielding, but only up to a certain line: she could give way on much, could agree to much, even to what contradicted her convictions, but there was always a line of honesty, principle and deepest conviction which no circumstances could make her cross.

Exactly twenty minutes after Razumikhin had left, there came two soft but hurried knocks at the door; he had come back.

“I won’t come in, no time!” he hurried, when the door was opened. “He is sleeping like a log, splendidly, peacefully, and God grant he sleeps ten hours. Nastasya is with him; I told her not to leave until I come. Now I’ll drag Zosimov here, he will report to you, and then off to bed with you; you are worn out beyond anything, I can see.”

And he set off from them down the corridor.

“What a quick and... devoted young man!” exclaimed Pulkheria Alexandrovna, extremely gladdened.

“He seems a splendid person!” Avdotya Romanovna answered with some warmth, beginning to walk up and down the room again.

Almost an hour later there were steps in the corridor and another knock at the door. Both women had been waiting, this time fully believing Razumikhin’s promise; and indeed he had managed to drag Zosimov along. Zosimov had at once agreed to abandon the feast and go and look at Raskolnikov, but he went to the ladies reluctantly and with great suspicion, not trusting the drunken Razumikhin. But his vanity was at once soothed and even flattered: he understood that he really had been awaited like an oracle. He stayed exactly ten minutes and fully succeeded in convincing and calming Pulkheria Alexandrovna. He spoke with extraordinary sympathy, but with restraint and a kind of studied seriousness, exactly like a twenty-seven-year-old doctor at an important consultation, and not by a single word did he stray from the subject or show the slightest wish to enter into more personal and private relations with the two ladies. Having noticed as he came in how dazzlingly beautiful Avdotya Romanovna was, he at once made a point of not even noticing her at all during the whole visit, and addressed himself solely to Pulkheria Alexandrovna. All this gave him extraordinary inner satisfaction. Of the patient himself he said that he found him at the present moment in a very satisfactory condition. According to his observations, the patient’s illness, apart from the poor material circumstances of the last months of his life, also had certain moral causes, “was, so to speak, the product of many complex moral and material influences, anxieties, apprehensions, cares, certain ideas... and so forth.” Noticing in passing that Avdotya Romanovna had begun listening with special attention, Zosimov enlarged somewhat further on this theme. To Pulkheria Alexandrovna’s anxious and timid question about “some supposed suspicions of insanity”, he answered with a calm and candid smile that his words had been greatly exaggerated; that of course a certain fixed idea was noticeable in the patient, something suggesting monomania, since he, Zosimov, was now following that extremely interesting branch of medicine with particular attention; but one must remember that almost up to today the patient had been delirious, and... and of course the arrival of his family would strengthen him, distract him, and have a salutary effect, “if only fresh special shocks can be avoided,” he added significantly. Then he stood up, took his leave solidly and cordially, accompanied by blessings, warm gratitude, entreaties, and even by Avdotya Romanovna’s little hand held out for him to shake without his seeking it, and went out extremely pleased with his visit and still more with himself.

“And we’ll talk tomorrow; go to bed, now, without fail!” Razumikhin sealed it, leaving with Zosimov. “Tomorrow, as early as possible, I’ll be with you with a report.”

“What a ravishing girl that Avdotya Romanovna is, though!” remarked Zosimov, almost licking his lips, when they had both come out into the street.

“Ravishing? You said ravishing!” roared Razumikhin, and suddenly hurled himself at Zosimov and seized him by the throat. “If you ever dare... Do you understand? Do you understand?” he shouted, shaking him by the collar and pinning him to the wall. “Did you hear?”

“Let go, you drunken devil!” Zosimov fought him off, and then, when the other had let him go, he looked at him intently and suddenly burst out laughing. Razumikhin stood before him with his arms hanging down, in gloomy and serious thought.

“Of course I am an ass,” he said, gloomy as a storm cloud, “but then... so are you.”

“Oh no, brother, not at all so am I. I don’t dream about foolishness.”

They walked on in silence, and only as they were approaching Raskolnikov’s lodging did Razumikhin, greatly preoccupied, break the silence.

“Listen,” he said to Zosimov, “you are a fine fellow, but besides all your bad qualities you are also a lecher, that I know, and one of the dirty kind. You are a nervous, weak piece of rubbish, you are capricious, you have grown fat and can deny yourself nothing, and that is what I call dirt, because it leads straight to dirt. You have pampered yourself so much that, I confess, what I least of all understand is how, with all that, you can be a good and even self-sacrificing doctor. He sleeps on a feather bed (a doctor!) and gets up at night for a patient! In three years’ time you won’t be getting up for a patient... Well, the devil, that’s not the point; the point is this: tonight you are sleeping in the landlady’s flat (I barely talked her into it!), and I in the kitchen: here is your chance to get better acquainted! Not what you think! There isn’t a shadow of that here, brother...”

“But I’m not thinking anything at all.”

“Here, brother, there is modesty, silence, bashfulness, a fierce chastity, and with all that, sighs, and she melts like wax, simply melts! Rid me of her, for the sake of all the devils in the world! She’s so very prepossessing!.. I’ll repay you, I’ll repay you with my head!”

Zosimov laughed harder than before.

“Look how it’s got hold of you! But what do I want with her?”

“I assure you, it’s not much trouble; just talk any slop you like, just sit beside her and talk. Besides, you are a doctor, start treating her for something. I swear you won’t regret it. She has a clavichord; you know I strum a little; I have a song there, a Russian one, a real one: ‘I will drown in burning tears...’ She loves the real ones, well, it all began with the song; and you are a virtuoso on the piano, a maestro, a Rubinstein... I assure you, you won’t regret it!”

“But have you made her some sort of promises? Signed something in due form? Promised to marry her, perhaps...”

“Nothing, nothing, absolutely nothing of the kind! And she isn’t like that at all; Chebarov tried his luck with her...”

“Well, then drop her!”

“But you can’t just drop her like that!”

“But why can’t you?”

“Well, somehow you just can’t, and that’s all! There is a drawing-in principle here, brother.”

“Then why did you lure her on?”

“But I didn’t lure her on at all; perhaps I was lured on myself, out of my own stupidity, and it will be absolutely all the same to her, you or me, as long as someone sits beside her and sighs. Here, brother... I can’t put it to you, here, well, you know mathematics well, and you are still studying it now, I know... well, start taking her through the integral calculus, by God I am not joking, I am speaking seriously, it will be absolutely all the same to her: she will look at you and sigh, and so for a whole year on end. I talked to her, among other things, for a very long time, two days on end, about the Prussian House of Lords (because what else is there to talk to her about?), and she only sighed and grew warm! Only don’t start talking about love, she is bashful to the point of convulsions, but do give the impression that you can’t tear yourself away, and that’s enough. It is terribly comfortable; exactly like home: read, sit, lie down, write... You can even kiss her, carefully...”

“But what do I want with her?”

“Eh, I can’t explain it to you at all! You see: you are both perfectly suited to each other! I thought of you before... After all, you will end up with this! So isn’t it all the same to you, sooner or later? Here, brother, there lies a sort of feather-bed principle, eh! and not only feather-bed! It draws you in; here is the end of the world, an anchor, a quiet haven, the navel of the earth, the three-fish foundation of the world, the essence of pancakes, rich pies, the evening samovar, quiet sighs and warm jackets, well-heated stove-beds; well, just as if you had died and at the same time were alive, both advantages at once! Well, brother, the devil, I’ve talked myself into nonsense, it’s time for bed! Listen: I sometimes wake up at night, so I’ll go and look in on him. But it’s nothing, rubbish, all is well. Don’t you worry too much either, but if you like, go and look in once too. But if you notice anything at all, delirium, for instance, or fever, or anything, wake me at once. Though it can’t be...”
