## Part One · Book One: The Story of One Little Family · Chapter I · Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov

Alexei Fyodorovich Karamazov was the third son of a landowner of our district, Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov, so well known in his day (and still remembered among us) because of his tragic and dark end, which came about exactly thirteen years ago and which I shall tell of in its place. For now I will say of this “landowner” (as he was called among us, though all his life he hardly lived on his estate at all) only that he was a strange type, yet one met fairly often: precisely the type of a man not only worthless and depraved but at the same time muddle-headed; but one of those muddle-headed men who know very well how to manage their little property affairs, and only those, it seems. Fyodor Pavlovich, for example, began with almost nothing; he was the smallest of landowners; he ran about dining at other people’s tables and angled to become a hanger-on; and yet at the moment of his death he turned out to have as much as a hundred thousand roubles in ready money. And at the same time he went on all his life being one of the most muddle-headed cranks in the whole of our district. I repeat: this is not stupidity; most of these cranks are rather clever and cunning; it is precisely muddle-headedness, and a special, national kind of it.

He was married twice, and he had three sons: the eldest, Dmitry Fyodorovich, by his first wife, and the other two, Ivan and Alexei, by his second. Fyodor Pavlovich’s first wife came from a fairly rich and distinguished noble family, the Miusovs, who were also landowners in our district. Exactly how it came about that a girl with a dowry, and a beautiful girl besides, and moreover one of those lively clever girls who are so common among us in the present generation but who were already appearing in the last one, could marry such a worthless “weakling”, as everyone called him then, I will not undertake to explain at length. After all, I once knew a young lady, still of the “romantic” generation before last, who after several years of mysterious love for a certain gentleman, whom, by the way, she could always have married in the most peaceful way, ended nevertheless by inventing insurmountable obstacles for herself, and on a stormy night threw herself from a high bank, like a cliff, into a rather deep and swift river and perished in it, decidedly out of her own caprice, solely in order to resemble Shakespeare’s Ophelia; and indeed, if that cliff, so long since chosen and loved by her, had not been so picturesque, if there had been only a prosaic flat bank in its place, perhaps the suicide would not have happened at all. This is a true fact, and one must suppose that in our Russian life, in the last two or three generations, not a few such facts, or facts of the same kind, have taken place. In the same way the act of Adelaida Ivanovna Miusova was without doubt an echo of foreign influences, and also the irritation of a captive mind. Perhaps she wanted to assert a woman’s independence, to go against social conventions, against the despotism of her relations and family; and an obliging imagination persuaded her, let us suppose for only a moment, that Fyodor Pavlovich, in spite of his status as a hanger-on, was all the same one of the boldest and most mocking men of that epoch of transition to everything better, whereas he was only a wicked buffoon and nothing more. What made it more piquant was that the affair was carried out by an elopement, and this greatly attracted Adelaida Ivanovna. As for Fyodor Pavlovich, even by his social position he was very well prepared then for all such escapades, for he passionately wished to make his career by any means whatever; and to attach himself to a good family and take a dowry was very tempting. As for mutual love, there seems to have been none at all, either on the bride’s side or on his, in spite of Adelaida Ivanovna’s beauty. So that this was perhaps the only case of its kind in the life of Fyodor Pavlovich, a most sensual man all his life, ready in an instant to cling to any skirt whatever, if only it beckoned to him. And yet this woman alone made no particular impression on him from the passionate side.

Immediately after the elopement Adelaida Ivanovna saw in a flash that she felt nothing for her husband but contempt. So the consequences of the marriage showed themselves with extraordinary swiftness. Although the family even fairly soon became reconciled to the event and gave the runaway her dowry, the most disorderly life and endless scenes began between the spouses. It was said that the young wife showed incomparably more nobility and loftiness in it than Fyodor Pavlovich, who, as is now known, pinched all her money from her at once, as much as twenty-five thousand, as soon as she received it, so that from then on those thousands simply sank, as far as she was concerned, as if into the water. The little village and the rather good town house which had also formed part of her dowry he tried for a long time, with all his might, to transfer to his own name by means of some suitable deed, and he would surely have succeeded, if only from the contempt and disgust, so to speak, which he aroused in his wife every minute by his shameless extortions and beggings, simply from her weariness of soul, just so that he would leave her alone. But fortunately Adelaida Ivanovna’s family stepped in and restrained the grabber. It is positively known that there were frequent fights between the spouses, but, according to tradition, it was not Fyodor Pavlovich who did the beating but Adelaida Ivanovna, a hot-tempered, bold, dark-skinned, impatient lady, gifted with remarkable physical strength. At last she abandoned the house and ran away from Fyodor Pavlovich with a seminary-trained teacher who was perishing of poverty, leaving the three-year-old Mitya on Fyodor Pavlovich’s hands. Fyodor Pavlovich instantly set up a whole harem in the house and the most dissolute drinking, and in the intervals drove round almost the whole province, complaining tearfully to everyone of Adelaida Ivanovna for leaving him, and in doing so he told such details as a husband should be far too ashamed to tell about his married life. Above all, it seemed to please him and even to flatter him to play before everyone his ridiculous part of the injured husband, and even to paint the details of his injury with embellishments. “One would think you had been given a promotion, Fyodor Pavlovich, you are so pleased, in spite of all your grief,” the mockers used to say to him. Many even added that he was glad to appear in a refurbished role as a buffoon, and that he deliberately pretended not to notice his comic position, so as to make the laughter louder. Who knows, though; perhaps it was naive in him. At last he managed to find the traces of his runaway. The poor woman turned out to be in Petersburg, where she had moved with her seminarist and where she had thrown herself recklessly into the fullest emancipation. Fyodor Pavlovich at once began to bustle and prepare to go to Petersburg; what for? he did not know himself, of course. Really, perhaps he would have gone then; but, having taken this decision, he at once considered himself especially entitled, to keep up his spirits before the journey, to plunge again into the most boundless drinking. And it was just at this time that his wife’s family received the news of her death in Petersburg. She had died somehow suddenly, in some garret, of typhus according to some accounts, and according to others supposedly of hunger. Fyodor Pavlovich learned of his wife’s death while drunk; they say he ran out into the street and began to shout, raising his hands to heaven in joy: “Lord, now lettest thou thy servant depart”; and according to others he sobbed like a little child, so much that, they say, it was pitiful even to look at him, in spite of all the disgust he aroused. It is very possible that both happened, that is, that he rejoiced at his liberation and wept for his liberator, all at once. In most cases people, even villains, are much more naive and simple-hearted than we generally conclude about them. And so are we ourselves.

## Chapter II · He Gets Rid of His First Son

Of course, one can imagine what kind of upbringer and father such a man could be. With him as a father, exactly what was bound to happen did happen: that is, he abandoned his child by Adelaida Ivanovna entirely and completely, not out of malice towards him or from any wounded conjugal feelings, but simply because he forgot about him completely. While he was wearying everyone with his tears and complaints and had turned his house into a den of debauchery, the three-year-old boy Mitya was taken into his care by a faithful servant of the house, Grigory, and if he had not looked after him then, perhaps there would have been no one even to change the child’s little shirt. Besides, it so happened that the child’s relations on his mother’s side also seemed to forget him at first. His grandfather, that is, Mr Miusov himself, Adelaida Ivanovna’s father, was no longer alive then; his widow, Mitya’s grandmother, who had moved to Moscow, had fallen seriously ill; the sisters had all married; so that Mitya had to spend almost a whole year with the servant Grigory, living with him in the servants’ cottage. However, even if his papa had remembered him (he could not really have been unaware of his existence), he himself would have sent him back to the cottage, since the child would after all have been in the way of his debauchery. But it so happened that a cousin of the late Adelaida Ivanovna returned from Paris, Pyotr Alexandrovich Miusov, who afterwards lived abroad for many years on end, a very young man still at that time, but a man exceptional among the Miusovs, enlightened, metropolitan, foreign, and moreover a European all his life, and at the end of his life a liberal of the forties and fifties. In the course of his career he had been in touch with many of the most liberal people of his epoch, both in Russia and abroad; he had known personally both Proudhon and Bakunin, and he especially liked to remember and tell, already near the end of his wanderings, of the three days of the February revolution in Paris in forty-eight, hinting that he himself had almost taken part in it on the barricades. This was one of the most gratifying memories of his youth. He had an independent fortune, by the old reckoning about a thousand souls. His excellent estate lay just outside our little town and bordered on the land of our famous monastery, with which Pyotr Alexandrovich, while still very young, as soon as he received his inheritance, at once began an endless lawsuit over the rights to some fishing in the river or some wood-cutting in the forest, I don’t know exactly, but he even considered it his civic and enlightened duty to begin a lawsuit against the “clericals”. Hearing all about Adelaida Ivanovna, whom of course he remembered and had once even noticed, and learning that Mitya had been left behind, he took up this business, in spite of all his youthful indignation and contempt for Fyodor Pavlovich. That was when he first became acquainted with Fyodor Pavlovich. He declared to him straight out that he would like to take the child’s upbringing upon himself. He used to tell long afterwards, as a characteristic trait, that when he began talking to Fyodor Pavlovich about Mitya, the latter for some time looked as if he did not understand at all what child was being talked about, and even seemed surprised that he had a little son somewhere in the house. Even if there was exaggeration in Pyotr Alexandrovich’s story, there must still have been something like the truth in it. But in fact Fyodor Pavlovich all his life loved to pose, suddenly to play some unexpected part before you, and above all sometimes without any need at all, even to his own direct harm, as in the present case, for instance. This trait, however, is characteristic of a very great many people, even very clever ones, not only of Fyodor Pavlovich. Pyotr Alexandrovich pursued the matter warmly and was even appointed guardian of the child (together with Fyodor Pavlovich), because after all a small property had been left by the mother: a house and an estate. Mitya did indeed move to this cousin once removed, but the latter had no family of his own, and since he himself, as soon as he had settled and secured the income from his estates, hastened back to Paris for a long time, he entrusted the child to one of his aunts, a certain Moscow lady. It so happened that, once he had settled in Paris, he too forgot the child, especially when that same February revolution came, which so struck his imagination and which he could never forget all his life. And the Moscow lady died, and Mitya passed to one of her married daughters. It seems that he afterwards changed his nest a fourth time as well. I will not enlarge on this now, all the more since there will be much more to tell about this first-born of Fyodor Pavlovich, and for now I shall limit myself only to the most necessary facts about him, without which I cannot even begin my novel.

In the first place, this Dmitry Fyodorovich was the only one of Fyodor Pavlovich’s three sons who grew up in the conviction that he did after all have some property, and that when he came of age he would be independent. His boyhood and youth passed in a disorderly way: he did not finish at the gymnasium; he then got into a military school; then he found himself in the Caucasus, was promoted, fought a duel, was reduced to the ranks, was promoted again, caroused a great deal and comparatively spent a fair amount of money. He did not begin to receive money from Fyodor Pavlovich until he came of age, and until then he ran into debt. He first knew and saw Fyodor Pavlovich, his father, only after he came of age, when he came to our parts on purpose to have it out with him about his property. It seems he did not like his parent even then; he stayed with him only a short time and left quickly, having managed only to get a certain sum from him and to enter into a certain arrangement with him about further receipts of income from the estate, of which (a remarkable fact) he did not on that occasion manage to get from Fyodor Pavlovich either the income or the value. Fyodor Pavlovich noticed then, from the very first (and this should be remembered), that Mitya had an exaggerated and false idea of his property. Fyodor Pavlovich was very pleased by this, having his own special calculations in view. He concluded only that the young man was frivolous, wild, passionate, impatient, a carouser, and that if only he could snatch something for the time being, he would at once calm down, if only for a short while, of course. And this is what Fyodor Pavlovich began to exploit: that is, he got off with small handouts, with sums sent from time to time, and in the end it came about that when, some four years later, Mitya, losing patience, appeared in our little town a second time to settle his affairs with his parent once and for all, it suddenly turned out, to his greatest amazement, that he had absolutely nothing left, that it was even hard to reckon up, that he had already drawn in money the whole value of his property from Fyodor Pavlovich and perhaps was even in debt to him himself; that by such and such deals, which he himself had wished to enter into at such and such a time, he had no right to demand anything more, and so on and so on. The young man was stunned, suspected falsehood and deceit, was almost beside himself and seemed to lose his mind. It was this very circumstance that led to the catastrophe whose account will make up the subject of my first introductory novel, or rather its outward side. But before I go on to this novel, I must also tell about Fyodor Pavlovich’s other two sons, Mitya’s brothers, and explain where they came from.
