## The Stillest Hour

“What has happened to me, my friends? You see me troubled, driven away, unwillingly obedient, ready to go: ah, to go away from you!

Yes, Zarathustra must go back into his solitude once more: but this time the bear goes back into his cave unwillingly!

What has happened to me? Who commands this? Ah, my angry mistress wills it so; she spoke to me: have I ever told you her name?

Yesterday towards evening my stillest hour spoke to me: that is the name of my terrible mistress.

And this is how it happened; for I must tell you everything, so that your hearts do not harden against the one who is suddenly leaving!

Do you know the fright of someone falling asleep?

He is frightened down to his toes, because the ground gives way beneath him and the dream begins.

I tell you this as a parable. Yesterday, at the stillest hour, the ground gave way beneath me: the dream began.

The hand moved on, the clock of my life drew breath; never had I heard such stillness around me, so that my heart took fright.

Then it spoke to me without a voice: ‘You know it, Zarathustra?’

And I cried out in fright at this whisper, and the blood left my face: but I kept silent.

Then again it spoke to me without a voice: ‘You know it, Zarathustra, but you do not say it!’

And at last I answered, like someone defiant: ‘Yes, I know it, but I will not say it!’

Then again it spoke to me without a voice: ‘You will not, Zarathustra? Is that really true? Do not hide in your defiance!’

And I wept and trembled like a child and said: ‘Ah, I would like to, but how can I! Let me off this! It is beyond my strength!’

Then again it spoke to me without a voice: ‘What do you matter, Zarathustra! Speak your word and break!’

And I answered: ‘Ah, is it my word? Who am I? I am waiting for someone more worthy; I am not worthy even to break on it.’

Then again it spoke to me without a voice: ‘What do you matter? You are not yet humble enough for me. Humility has the toughest hide.’

And I answered: ‘What has the hide of my humility not borne already! I live at the foot of my height: how high are my peaks? No one has told me yet. But I know my valleys well.’

Then again it spoke to me without a voice: ‘O Zarathustra, whoever has mountains to move moves valleys and lowlands too.’

And I answered: ‘My word has not yet moved any mountains, and what I said did not reach men. I did go to men, but I have not yet arrived among them.’

Then again it spoke to me without a voice: ‘What do you know of that! The dew falls on the grass when the night is most silent.’

And I answered: ‘They mocked me when I found my own way and went it; and in truth my feet trembled then.

And so they said to me: “You have unlearned the way; now you are unlearning how to walk too!”’

Then again it spoke to me without a voice: ‘What does their mockery matter! You are one who has unlearned how to obey: now you shall command!

Do you not know who is most needed by all? The one who commands great things.

To do great things is hard: but harder still is to command great things.

This is what is most unforgivable in you: you have the power, and you will not rule.’

And I answered: ‘I lack the lion’s voice for all commanding.’

Then again it spoke to me like a whisper: ‘It is the stillest words that bring the storm. Thoughts that come on doves’ feet guide the world.

O Zarathustra, you shall go as a shadow of what must come: so you will command, and in commanding go ahead.’

And I answered: ‘I am ashamed.’

Then again it spoke to me without a voice: ‘You must still become a child, and without shame.

The pride of youth is still upon you; you became young late: but whoever wants to become a child must overcome his youth as well.’

And I thought it over for a long time, and trembled. But at last I said what I had said at first: ‘I will not.’

Then there was laughter all around me. Alas, how that laughter tore my insides and slit open my heart!

And it spoke to me for the last time: ‘O Zarathustra, your fruits are ripe, but you are not ripe for your fruits!

So you must go back into solitude: for you must still grow mellow.’

And again it laughed, and fled: then it became still around me, as with a twofold stillness. But I lay on the ground, and the sweat poured from my limbs.

Now you have heard everything, and why I must go back into my solitude. I have kept nothing from you, my friends.

But you have also heard from me who is still the most silent of all men, and wants to be!

Ah, my friends! I would have something more to say to you, I would have something more to give you! Why do I not give it? Am I miserly, then?”

But when Zarathustra had spoken these words, the force of his pain and the nearness of parting from his friends overcame him, so that he wept aloud; and no one knew how to comfort him. But at night he went away alone and left his friends.

## Third Part

> “You look upward when you long to be lifted up. And I look down, because I am lifted up.
> Who among you can laugh and be lifted up at the same time?
> Whoever climbs the highest mountains laughs at all tragedies, in plays and in life.”
— Zarathustra, On Reading and Writing

## The Wanderer

It was midnight when Zarathustra set off over the ridge of the island, so as to reach the other shore early in the morning: for there he meant to take ship. There was a good harbour there, where foreign ships too liked to anchor; they took with them many a person who wanted to go over the sea from the blessed isles. Now as Zarathustra climbed the mountain, he thought on the way of all the lonely wandering he had done since his youth, and of how many mountains and ridges and peaks he had already climbed.

“I am a wanderer and a mountain climber,” he said to his heart. “I do not love the plains, and it seems I cannot sit still for long.

And whatever comes to me now as fate and experience, there will be wandering in it and mountain climbing: in the end one experiences only oneself.

The time is past when accidents could still happen to me; and what could still fall to me now that was not already my own!

It only returns, it comes home to me at last: my own self, and whatever of it has long been abroad and scattered among all things and accidents.

And I know one thing more: I am standing now before my last peak, and before what has been saved up for me longest. Ah, I must climb my hardest path! Ah, I have begun my loneliest wandering!

But whoever is of my kind does not escape such an hour: the hour that says to him: ‘Only now are you going your way of greatness! Peak and abyss: they are now joined in one!

You are going your way of greatness: now what was until now your last danger has become your last refuge!

You are going your way of greatness: now this must be your best courage, that there is no longer any way behind you!

You are going your way of greatness; no one shall creep after you here! Your own foot has wiped out the way behind you, and over it is written: Impossibility.

And if from now on all ladders fail you, then you must know how to climb on your own head: how else would you climb upward?

On your own head, and away over your own heart! Now the gentlest thing in you must become the hardest.

Whoever has always spared himself a great deal grows sickly in the end from so much sparing. Praised be what makes hard! I do not praise the land where butter and honey flow!

To learn to look away from oneself is necessary in order to see much: this hardness is needed by every mountain climber.

But whoever is pushy with his eyes as a seeker of knowledge, how should he see more of all things than their foregrounds!

But you, O Zarathustra, wanted to see the ground and background of all things: so you must climb over yourself, up, upward, until you have even your stars beneath you!’

Yes! To look down on myself and even on my stars: only that would I call my peak; that was still left to me as my last peak!”

So Zarathustra spoke to himself as he climbed, comforting his heart with hard little sayings: for he was sore at heart as never before. And when he came to the top of the ridge, look, there the other sea lay spread out before him: and he stood still and was silent for a long time. But the night was cold at this height, and clear and bright with stars.

“I recognise my lot,” he said at last, sadly. “Well then! I am ready. My last solitude has just begun.

Ah, this black, sad sea beneath me! Ah, this pregnant, night-like sullenness! Ah, fate and sea! Now I must go down to you!

I stand before my highest mountain and before my longest wandering: that is why I must first go down deeper than I ever climbed:

deeper down into pain than I ever climbed, down into its blackest flood! So my fate wills it: well then! I am ready.

Where do the highest mountains come from? So I once asked. Then I learned that they come out of the sea.

This testimony is written in their stone and in the walls of their peaks. From the deepest the highest must come to its height.”

So Zarathustra spoke on the top of the mountain, where it was cold; but when he came near the sea and at last stood alone among the cliffs, he had grown tired on the way and more full of longing than ever before.

“Everything is still asleep now,” he said; “the sea is asleep too. Its eye looks at me drunk with sleep, and strange.

But it breathes warmly; I feel that. And I feel too that it is dreaming. Dreaming, it tosses on hard pillows.

Listen! Listen! How it groans with bad memories! Or bad expectations?

Ah, I am sad with you, you dark monster, and angry with myself on your account.

Ah, that my hand has not strength enough! Gladly, truly, I would free you from bad dreams!”

And as Zarathustra spoke so, he laughed at himself with melancholy and bitterness. “What! Zarathustra!” he said. “Do you want to sing comfort to the sea as well?

Ah, you loving fool Zarathustra, you who are blissful with trust! But you were always like this: you always came trustingly to everything terrible.

You wanted to stroke every monster. A breath of warm breath, a little soft fur on the paw: and at once you were ready to love it and coax it.

Love is the danger of the loneliest one, love of everything, if only it lives! Truly, my folly and my modesty in love are laughable!”

So Zarathustra spoke, and laughed a second time: but then he thought of the friends he had left, and, as if he had wronged them with his thoughts, he was angry with himself for his thoughts. And soon it happened that the one who had laughed wept: Zarathustra wept bitterly with anger and longing.

## On the Vision and the Riddle

## 1

When it became known among the sailors that Zarathustra was on the ship (for a man who came from the blessed isles had gone on board with him), there was great curiosity and expectation. But Zarathustra was silent for two days, and was cold and deaf with sadness, so that he answered neither looks nor questions. But on the evening of the second day he opened his ears again, though he was still silent: for there was much that was strange and dangerous to hear on this ship, which came from far away and meant to go still farther. And Zarathustra was a friend of all who make long journeys and do not care to live without danger. And look! at last, as he listened, his own tongue was loosened, and the ice of his heart broke: then he began to speak thus:

To you, the bold seekers and searchers, and whoever has ever set out with cunning sails on terrible seas;

to you, drunk on riddles, glad of the twilight, whose souls are lured by flutes to every treacherous gulf:

for you do not want to grope along a thread with a cowardly hand; and where you can guess, you hate to work it out;

to you alone I tell the riddle that I saw: the vision of the loneliest one.

Lately I walked gloomily through a corpse-coloured twilight, gloomy and hard, with lips pressed together. Not only one sun had set for me.

A path that climbed defiantly through scree, a malicious, lonely path, which neither herb nor bush cheered any more: a mountain path crunched under the defiance of my foot.

Striding silently over the mocking clink of pebbles, crushing the stone that made it slip: so my foot forced its way upward.

Upward: in spite of the spirit that drew it downward, drew it towards the abyss, the spirit of gravity, my devil and arch-enemy.

Upward: although he sat on me, half dwarf, half mole; lame; making lame; dripping lead into my ear, leaden-drop thoughts into my brain.

“O Zarathustra,” he whispered mockingly, syllable by syllable, “you stone of wisdom! You threw yourself high, but every stone that is thrown must fall!

O Zarathustra, you stone of wisdom, you sling-stone, you star-destroyer! You threw yourself so high, but every stone that is thrown must fall!

Condemned to yourself and to your own stoning: O Zarathustra, far indeed you threw the stone, but it will fall back on you!”

Then the dwarf fell silent; and that lasted a long time. But his silence weighed on me; and to be two like this is truly lonelier than to be one!

I climbed, I climbed, I dreamed, I thought; but everything weighed on me. I was like a sick man whom his bad torment makes tired, and whom a worse dream wakes again as he is falling asleep.

But there is something in me that I call courage: until now it has struck dead every despondency in me. This courage at last told me to stand still and say: “Dwarf! You! Or I!”

For courage is the best slayer: courage that attacks; for in every attack there is the sound of the band playing.

But man is the most courageous animal: with this he has overcome every animal. With the band playing he has overcome even every pain; but human pain is the deepest pain.

Courage also slays the dizziness at abysses: and where does man not stand at abysses! Is not seeing itself seeing abysses?

Courage is the best slayer: courage slays pity too. But pity is the deepest abyss: as deeply as man sees into life, so deeply does he see into suffering.

But courage is the best slayer, courage that attacks: it slays even death, for it says: “Was that life? Well then! Once more!”

In such a saying there is much sound of the band playing. Whoever has ears, let him hear.

## 2

“Stop! Dwarf!” I said. “I! Or you! But I am the stronger of us two: you do not know my abysmal thought! That you could not bear!”

Then something happened that made me lighter: for the dwarf jumped down from my shoulder, the inquisitive one! And he squatted on a stone in front of me. But just where we had stopped there was a gateway.

“Look at this gateway, dwarf!” I went on. “It has two faces. Two paths come together here: no one has yet walked either of them to its end.

This long lane back: it lasts an eternity. And that long lane out: that is another eternity.

They contradict each other, these paths; they butt each other head-on; and here, at this gateway, is where they come together. The name of the gateway is written above it: ‘Moment’.

But whoever went on along one of them, farther and farther, and ever farther: do you believe, dwarf, that these paths contradict each other eternally?”

“Everything straight lies,” the dwarf muttered contemptuously. “All truth is crooked; time itself is a circle.”

“You spirit of gravity!” I said angrily, “do not make it too easy for yourself! Or I shall leave you squatting where you squat, lame-foot; and I carried you high!

Look,” I went on, “at this moment! From this gateway, Moment, a long, eternal lane runs backward: behind us lies an eternity.

Must not whatever can run, of all things, have already run along this lane once? Must not whatever can happen, of all things, have already happened, been done, run past, once?

And if everything has been here before: what do you think, dwarf, of this moment? Must not this gateway too have been here before?

And are not all things knotted together so firmly that this moment draws after it all things that are to come? And so, itself as well?

For whatever can run, of all things: in this long lane out, too, it must run once more!

And this slow spider that crawls in the moonlight, and this moonlight itself, and I and you in the gateway, whispering together, whispering of eternal things: must we not all have been here before?

And come back, and run in that other lane, out, ahead of us, in this long, dreadful lane: must we not come back eternally?”

So I spoke, and ever more softly: for I was afraid of my own thoughts and the thoughts behind them. Then, suddenly, I heard a dog howl nearby.

Had I ever heard a dog howl like that? My thoughts ran back. Yes! When I was a child, in my most distant childhood:

then I heard a dog howl like that. And I saw it too, its hair bristling, its head raised, trembling, in the stillest midnight, when even dogs believe in ghosts:

so that I pitied it. For just then the full moon was going over the house, silent as death; just then it stood still, a round glow, still on the flat roof, as if on someone else’s property:

that was what had terrified the dog then: for dogs believe in thieves and ghosts. And when I heard it howl like that again, I pitied it again.

Where was the dwarf now? And the gateway? And the spider? And all the whispering? Was I dreaming, then? Was I waking up? All at once I stood among wild cliffs, alone, desolate, in the most desolate moonlight.

But there lay a man! And there! The dog, leaping, bristling, whining: now it saw me coming; then it howled again, then it cried out. Had I ever heard a dog cry for help like that?

And truly, what I saw, I had never seen the like of. I saw a young shepherd, writhing, choking, twitching, his face distorted, with a heavy black snake hanging out of his mouth.

Had I ever seen so much disgust and pale horror on one face? He must have been asleep. Then the snake crawled into his throat, and there it bit fast.

My hand pulled at the snake and pulled: in vain! It could not pull the snake out of his throat. Then it cried out of me: “Bite! Bite!

Bite its head off! Bite!” So it cried out of me, my horror, my hatred, my disgust, my pity, all my good and bad cried out of me with one cry.

You bold ones around me! You seekers, searchers, and whoever among you has set out with cunning sails on unexplored seas! You who are glad of riddles!

Then guess me the riddle that I saw then, interpret for me the vision of the loneliest one!

For it was a vision and a foreseeing: what did I see then in a parable? And who is it who must still come one day?

Who is the shepherd into whose throat the snake crawled like that? Who is the man into whose throat all that is heaviest and blackest will crawl like that?

But the shepherd bit, as my cry had advised him; he bit with a good bite! Far away he spat the snake’s head, and sprang up.

No longer a shepherd, no longer a man: one transformed, surrounded with light, who laughed! Never yet on earth has any man laughed as he laughed!

O my brothers, I heard a laughter that was no human laughter; and now a thirst gnaws at me, a longing that never grows still.

My longing for this laughter gnaws at me: oh, how can I bear to go on living! And how could I bear to die now!

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
