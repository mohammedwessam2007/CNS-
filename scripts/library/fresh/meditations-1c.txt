## Book Four

1. When the master within us is in keeping with nature, it stands towards whatever happens in such a way that it always adapts easily to what is possible and what is given. For it loves no fixed material, but moves towards its aims with a reservation, and turns whatever is brought in against it into material for itself, as fire does when it masters what is thrown on it. A small lamp would have been put out by those things; but a bright fire very quickly makes what is heaped on it its own, eats it up, and rises higher because of it.

2. Let no action be done at random, nor otherwise than by the rule that makes the art complete.

3. People look for retreats for themselves, in the country, by the sea, in the mountains; and you too are used to longing for such things most of all. But this is altogether the mark of a very ordinary person, when you can retreat into yourself at any hour you like. For nowhere can a person retreat to a quieter or more untroubled place than into his own soul, especially if he has within him things such that, by looking into them, he is at once completely at ease; and by ease I mean nothing else than good order. So give yourself this retreat constantly, and renew yourself. Let the principles you meet there be short and basic, so that, as soon as you come upon them, they will be enough to wash away all your distress, and to send you back without resentment to the things you return to.

For what will you resent? The badness of people? Recall the conclusion that rational beings were born for one another; that putting up with them is part of justice; that they do wrong against their will; and how many people, after quarrelling, suspecting, hating and fighting to the death, have been laid out and burned to ashes; and stop at last. Or will you resent what is given to you out of the whole? Recall the choice: either providence or atoms; and all the proofs that the world is a kind of city. Or will the things of the body still get hold of you? Consider that the mind does not mix with the breath, whether it moves smoothly or roughly, once it has drawn itself apart and recognised its own power; and remember too everything you have heard about pain and pleasure, and agreed with.

Or will a little fame pull you away? Look at how quickly everything is forgotten, and the abyss of endless time on either side, and the emptiness of applause, and how changeable and undiscerning are the people who seem to speak well of you, and how narrow the space it is confined to. For the whole earth is a point, and how tiny a corner of it is this place where we live! And here, how many will praise you, and what sort of people are they?

So from now on, remember the retreat into this little plot of land that is yourself; and above all do not be pulled about or strain yourself, but be free, and look at things as a man, as a human being, as a citizen, as a mortal creature. And among the things most ready to hand, which you will look into, let there be these two. First, that things do not touch the soul, but stand outside it, quite still; and our troubles come only from the judgement within. Second, that all these things you see will change in a moment, and will be no more; and keep constantly in mind how many changes you have yourself already seen. The world is change; life is judgement.

4. If mind is common to us all, then so is reason, by which we are rational beings. If so, then the reason that tells us what to do and what not to do is common too. If so, law is common too. If so, we are citizens. If so, we share in some kind of commonwealth. If so, the world is a kind of city. For in what other common commonwealth could one say the whole human race shares? And from there, from this common city, comes our mind itself, and our reason, and our sense of law; or where else? For just as the earthy part of me has been given me from some earth, and the watery from another element, and the breath from some source, and the hot and fiery from its own source (for nothing comes from nothing, just as nothing goes away into nothing), so the mind too has come from somewhere.

5. Death is like birth, a mystery of nature: a coming together of the same elements, and a coming apart into the same. Nothing in it, at all, to be ashamed of; for it is not out of keeping with a thinking creature, nor with the plan of its making.

6. Such things are bound to come from such people, of necessity; and whoever does not want this wants the fig tree not to have its juice. In any case, remember this: in a very short time both you and he will be dead, and soon not even your names will be left.

7. Take away the judgement, and “I have been harmed” is taken away. Take away “I have been harmed”, and the harm is taken away.

8. What does not make a man worse than he was does not make his life worse either, nor harm him, from outside or from within.

9. The nature of what is useful has been bound to do this.

10. That “everything that happens, happens justly”. You will find it so if you watch carefully. I do not mean only in the sense that it follows in order, but that it is just, as if someone were giving each thing what it deserves. So go on watching, as you have begun; and whatever you do, do it with this: with being good, in the proper sense of the word “good”. Keep to this in every action.

11. Do not see things as the man who insults you judges them, or as he wants you to judge them; but see them as they really are.

12. You should always have these two things ready. First, to do only what the reason of the ruling and law-giving art suggests for the good of people. Second, to change your mind, if someone is there to correct you and move you away from some opinion. But the change must always come from being persuaded that something is just, or for the common good, or the like; never because it seemed pleasant or popular.

13. “Do you have reason?” “I do.” “Then why not use it? For if that is doing its work, what more do you want?”

14. You have existed as a part. You will vanish into what made you; or rather, you will be taken back, by change, into its seed-reason.

15. Many grains of incense on the same altar. One drops first, another later; it makes no difference.

16. Within ten days you will seem a god to the very people to whom you now seem a beast and an ape, if you go back to your principles and to honouring reason.

17. Do not act as if you were going to live ten thousand years. What must be hangs over you. While you live, while you can, become good.

18. How much leisure a man gains who does not look at what his neighbour said or did or thought, but only at what he himself is doing, that it may be just and holy. Do not look about at other people’s dark characters, but run straight along the line, not swerving.

19. The man who is excited about fame after death does not picture to himself that every one of those who remember him will very soon die himself; and then again the one who comes after him, until the whole memory of him goes out, passed along by people who are lit and then put out. But suppose that those who will remember you were immortal, and the memory immortal too: what is that to you? I do not say that it is nothing to the dead man; but what is praise to the living, except perhaps for some practical use? For now you are letting go, out of season, of the gift that nature gives, while clinging to something else.

20. Everything that is beautiful in any way is beautiful in itself, and complete in itself, and praise is no part of it. A thing is made neither worse nor better by being praised. I say this even of the things commonly called beautiful, such as material things and works of art. As for what is truly beautiful, what need has it of anything? No more than law, no more than truth, no more than kindness or modesty. Which of these is beautiful because it is praised, or spoiled because it is blamed? Is an emerald made worse than it was if it is not praised? Or gold, ivory, purple, a lyre, a little knife, a flower, a shrub?

21. If souls live on, how has the air made room for them from all eternity? And how does the earth make room for the bodies of those who have been buried for so long? Just as here, after the bodies have lasted a while, their change and decay makes room for other dead bodies, so the souls that move into the air, after lasting a while, change and spread and are kindled, being taken back into the seed-reason of the whole; and in this way they make room for those that come to live there after them. This is the answer one might give, supposing that souls live on.

And we should consider not only the number of bodies buried in this way, but also the number of animals eaten every day by us and by other animals. How great a number is used up and, so to speak, buried in the bodies of those who feed on them; and yet the space receives them, by their being turned into blood, and changed into air or fire.

What is the way to the truth in this? To divide it into the material and the cause.

22. Do not wander off; but in every impulse give what is just, and in every impression keep to what you clearly grasp.

23. Everything that suits you suits me, O world. Nothing is too early or too late for me that is in good time for you. Everything is fruit to me that your seasons bring, O nature. From you are all things, in you are all things, to you are all things. The poet says, “Dear city of Cecrops”; and will you not say, “Dear city of Zeus”?

24. “Do few things,” says Democritus, “if you want to be at peace.” Would it not be better to do what is necessary, and what the reason of a creature born for the city requires, and in the way it requires? For this brings not only the peace that comes from doing things well, but also the peace that comes from doing few things. For most of what we say and do is not necessary, and if a man takes it away, he will have more leisure and less disturbance. So at every turn one should remind oneself: “Is this one of the unnecessary things?” And we should cut out not only unnecessary actions, but unnecessary thoughts too; for then the unnecessary actions that follow them will not follow.

25. Try how the life of the good man goes for you: the man who is content with what is given him out of the whole, and satisfied with his own just action and kindly disposition.

26. You have seen those things; look at these too. Do not trouble yourself; make yourself simple. Someone does wrong? He does wrong to himself. Something has happened to you? Good: everything that happens was fated for you from the beginning out of the whole, and spun for you. In a word, life is short; you must make the most of the present with good sense and justice. Be sober, but relaxed.

27. Either an ordered world, or a jumble thrown together. But surely an ordered world. Or can there be order in you, and disorder in the whole? And that when all things are so distinct, and yet spread through one another, and feel with one another.

28. A black character, a soft character, a stubborn character; brutish, cattle-like, childish, stupid, false, buffoonish, huckstering, tyrannical.

29. If he is a stranger in the world who does not know what is in it, no less a stranger is he who does not know what happens in it. He is an exile who runs away from the reason of the city; blind, who shuts the eye of the mind; a beggar, who needs another and does not have from himself everything useful for life; an abscess on the world, who stands apart and separates himself from the reason of our common nature by being displeased with what happens (for the same nature that produced you produces this); a splinter broken off from the city, who breaks off his own soul from the soul of rational beings, which is one.

30. One man practises philosophy without a shirt, another without a book. Here is another, half naked: “I have no bread,” he says, “and I stay true to reason.” And I have no food from my studies, and I stay true.

31. Love the little craft you have learned, and rest in it. Go through the rest of your life as one who has handed over everything he has to the gods with his whole soul, and has made himself neither tyrant nor slave to any human being.

32. Think, for example, of the times of Vespasian. You will see all the same things: people marrying, raising children, falling ill, dying, making war, feasting, trading, farming, flattering, being arrogant, suspecting, plotting, praying for others to die, grumbling at their lot, falling in love, hoarding, wanting consulships, wanting kingdoms. Well, that life of theirs is no longer anywhere.

Move on again to the times of Trajan. Again all the same things. That life too is dead. In the same way look at the records of other times and of whole nations, and see how many people, after straining every nerve, fell soon after and were broken up into their elements. And above all, go over in your mind those you yourself have known, chasing after empty things, and neglecting to do what was in keeping with their own nature, and to hold on to it tightly and be content with it. And here you must remember that the attention given to each action has its own value and proportion; for then you will not lose heart, if you do not spend more time than is fitting on smaller things.

33. Words that were common long ago are now obscure; and so the names of those who were once much praised are now, in a way, obscure too: Camillus, Caeso, Volesus, Dentatus; and a little later Scipio and Cato, then Augustus too, then Hadrian and Antoninus. For all things fade quickly and become legend, and quickly too complete oblivion buries them. And I say this of those who shone in some wonderful way; as for the rest, as soon as they have breathed their last, they are “unseen, unheard of”. And what, after all, is everlasting remembrance? Altogether empty. What, then, is it that we should give our effort to? This one thing: a just mind, actions for the common good, speech that never lies, and a disposition that welcomes everything that happens as necessary, as familiar, as flowing from the same kind of beginning and spring.

34. Give yourself up willingly to Clotho, the Fate who spins, and let her weave you into whatever things she wishes.

35. Everything lasts only a day, both the one who remembers and the one remembered.

36. Watch continually how all things come about by change, and get used to thinking that the nature of the whole loves nothing so much as to change the things that are and to make new things like them. For everything that exists is in a way the seed of what will come from it. But you think only of seeds that are dropped into the earth or the womb; and that is very unthinking.

37. You will soon be dead, and you are not yet simple, nor untroubled, nor free from suspecting that you can be harmed from outside, nor kind to everyone, nor do you yet place wisdom only in acting justly.

38. Look into their ruling minds, even those of the wise: what they run from, and what they chase after.

39. Your harm does not lie in someone else’s ruling mind, nor in any change or alteration of what surrounds you. Where, then? Where your power of judging about harms is. So let that not make the judgement, and all is well. Even if what is closest to it, your poor body, is cut, burned, festers or rots, let the part that judges about these things keep quiet: that is, let it judge that nothing is bad or good which can happen equally to a bad man and a good one. For what happens equally to the one who lives against nature and the one who lives according to nature is neither according to nature nor against it.

40. Think constantly of the world as one living being, with one substance and one soul; and how everything goes back into its one awareness; and how it does everything by one impulse; and how all things work together as causes of all that happens; and how they are spun and woven together.

41. You are a little soul carrying a corpse, as Epictetus used to say.

42. There is nothing bad for things in the process of change, as there is nothing good for things that come into being by change.

43. Time is a kind of river of things that happen, and a violent current. As soon as each thing has been seen, it is swept away, and another is being carried past, and that too will be swept away.

44. Everything that happens is as usual and familiar as the rose in spring and fruit in summer; so too are illness and death and slander and plotting, and everything that delights or grieves fools.

45. What comes after always follows naturally on what went before. It is not like a list of separate items that merely follow one another by necessity, but a reasonable connection; and just as the things that exist are arranged together in harmony, so the things that come into being show not a mere succession, but a wonderful kinship.

46. Always remember the saying of Heraclitus, that the death of earth is to become water, and the death of water to become air, and of air to become fire, and the other way round. Remember too the man who forgets where the road leads; and that people are at odds with the very thing they are most constantly in touch with, the reason that governs everything; and that what they meet with every day seems strange to them; and that we must not act and speak as if asleep, for in sleep too we think we act and speak; and that we must not be like children who take things from their parents, that is, simply because that is how we were taught.

47. If one of the gods told you that you would die tomorrow, or at any rate the day after, you would not think it much better to die the day after rather than tomorrow, unless you were utterly mean-spirited; for how big is the difference? So too, do not think it a great thing to die after many years rather than tomorrow.

48. Think constantly how many doctors have died, after frowning so often over their patients; how many astrologers, after foretelling the deaths of others as if it were something great; how many philosophers, after arguing endlessly about death or immortality; how many great warriors, after killing so many; how many tyrants, after using their power over men’s lives with terrible arrogance, as if they were immortal; how many whole cities, if I may put it so, have died: Helice, Pompeii, Herculaneum, and countless others.

Go over, too, all the people you know, one after another: one buried another and was then laid out himself, and another buried someone else, and all in a short time. In a word: always see how short-lived and cheap human things are; yesterday a drop of mucus, tomorrow a mummy or ashes. So pass through this tiny moment of time in keeping with nature, and end your journey content, as an olive might fall when it is ripe, praising the earth that bore it and thanking the tree that grew it.

49. Be like the headland, against which the waves break continually; but it stands firm, and the swelling waters around it are put to sleep.

“I am unlucky, because this has happened to me.” No: “I am lucky, because, though this has happened to me, I go on free from distress, neither crushed by the present nor afraid of what is coming.” For this could have happened to anyone; but not everyone would have gone on free from distress. Why, then, is that a misfortune, rather than this a piece of good fortune? Do you call anything at all a misfortune for a man that is not a failure of man’s nature? And do you think it a failure of man’s nature, when it is not against the will of his nature? Well then.

You have learned what that will is. Does what has happened prevent you, then, from being just, high-minded, self-controlled, sensible, careful in judgement, truthful, modest, free, and all the rest, by whose presence man’s nature has what is its own? From now on, whenever something leads you towards distress, remember to use this principle: not “this is a misfortune”, but “to bear it nobly is good fortune”.

50. A plain but effective help towards despising death is to go over in your mind those who clung tightly to life. What did they gain more than those who died young? They all lie somewhere at last: Cadicianus, Fabius, Julianus, Lepidus, and any others like them, who carried many out to burial, and then were carried out themselves. Altogether, the interval is small; and through how many troubles, and with what kind of people, and in how feeble a body, it is dragged out! So do not make much of it. Look at the vast gulf of time behind you, and the other infinity in front. In this, what difference is there between a baby that lives three days and a man who lives three generations, like Nestor?

51. Always run by the short road; and the short road is the one according to nature: so as to say and do everything in the soundest way. For such a purpose frees a man from toil and strain, and from all managing and cleverness.
