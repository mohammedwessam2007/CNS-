## Part One · Underground

Both the author of these notes and the Notes themselves are, of course, invented. Nevertheless, people like the writer of such notes not only may but must exist in our society, considering the circumstances under which our society in general has been formed. I wanted to bring before the public, more clearly than usual, one of the characters of the recent past. He is one of the representatives of a generation that is still living out its days. In this first part, entitled “Underground”, this person introduces himself and his view of things, and tries, as it were, to explain the reasons why he appeared and was bound to appear among us. In the second part come this person’s actual “notes” about certain events in his life. (Fyodor Dostoevsky)

## I

I am a sick man... I am a spiteful man. An unattractive man, that’s what I am. I think there is something wrong with my liver. But then, I don’t understand a damned thing about my illness, and I don’t know for certain what is wrong with me. I am not being treated and never have been, though I respect medicine and doctors. Besides, I am superstitious in the extreme; well, at least superstitious enough to respect medicine. (I am educated enough not to be superstitious, but I am superstitious.) No, sir, I refuse to be treated out of spite. That, I expect, you will not be so good as to understand. Well, sir, I understand it. Of course, I shan’t be able to explain to you exactly whom I am getting at with my spite in this case; I know perfectly well that I can’t possibly do the doctors any harm by not being treated by them; I know better than anyone that all this will hurt only me and nobody else. But still, if I am not being treated, it is out of spite. My liver hurts: well, let it hurt even more!

I have been living like this for a long time now: about twenty years. Now I am forty. I used to be in the service, but I am not any more. I was a spiteful official. I was rude, and I enjoyed it. After all, I didn’t take bribes, so I had to reward myself somehow. (A bad joke, but I won’t cross it out. I wrote it thinking it would be very witty; and now that I see for myself that I only wanted to show off disgustingly, I won’t cross it out on purpose!) When people came up to the desk where I sat to ask for information, I used to gnash my teeth at them and feel an inexorable pleasure whenever I managed to upset someone. I almost always managed. Most of them were timid people: petitioners, you know. But among the dandies there was one officer in particular I couldn’t stand. He would not submit at any price, and he rattled his sword in a disgusting way. I waged war with him over that sword for a year and a half. In the end I won. He stopped rattling.

That happened in my youth, by the way. But do you know, gentlemen, what the main point of my spite was? Why, the whole thing, the vilest thing of all, was that every minute, even at the moment of my most violent bile, I was shamefully aware that I was not only not a spiteful man but not even an embittered one, that I was only frightening sparrows for nothing and amusing myself with it. I am foaming at the mouth, but bring me some little doll, give me a cup of tea with sugar, and I dare say I’ll calm down. I’ll even be touched to the bottom of my soul, though afterwards, no doubt, I’ll gnash my teeth at myself and suffer from insomnia for months out of shame. That’s my way.

I was lying about myself just now when I said I was a spiteful official. I lied out of spite. I was simply playing games, both with the petitioners and with the officer, and in reality I could never become spiteful. Every minute I was aware of a great, great many elements in me utterly opposed to it. I felt them simply swarming in me, these opposite elements. I knew that they had been swarming in me all my life and begging to be let out, but I would not let them out, would not, deliberately would not let them out. They tormented me to the point of shame; they drove me to convulsions, and I got sick of them at last, so sick of them! Doesn’t it seem to you, gentlemen, that I am now repenting of something before you, that I am asking your forgiveness for something?... I am sure it seems so to you... But, I assure you, it’s all the same to me if it does...

I could not become anything, not even spiteful: neither spiteful nor kind, neither a scoundrel nor an honest man, neither a hero nor an insect. And now I am living out my life in my corner, taunting myself with the spiteful and useless consolation that an intelligent man cannot seriously become anything, and that only a fool becomes something. Yes, sir, an intelligent man of the nineteenth century must be, and is morally obliged to be, a creature essentially without character; while a man of character, a man of action, is a creature essentially limited. That is my conviction of forty years. I am forty now, and forty years, you know, is a whole lifetime; it is extreme old age. To live past forty is indecent, vulgar, immoral! Who lives past forty? Answer sincerely, honestly. I’ll tell you who: fools and scoundrels. I’ll say it to the faces of all the old men, all those venerable old men, all those silver-haired and sweet-smelling old men! I’ll say it to the face of the whole world! I have the right to talk like this, because I myself will live to sixty. I’ll live to seventy! I’ll live to eighty!... Wait! Let me catch my breath...

You probably think, gentlemen, that I want to make you laugh? Wrong again. I am not at all such a cheerful fellow as you think, or as you perhaps think. However, if, irritated by all this chatter (and I can already feel that you are irritated), you take it into your heads to ask me who exactly I am, I will answer: I am a collegiate assessor. I worked in the service so as to have something to eat (but only for that), and when last year one of my distant relatives left me six thousand roubles in his will, I immediately retired and settled down in my corner. I lived in this corner before, but now I have settled down in it. My room is wretched, nasty, on the edge of the city. My servant is a peasant woman, old, spiteful out of stupidity, and she always smells bad besides. I am told that the Petersburg climate is getting bad for me, and that with my insignificant means it is very expensive to live in Petersburg. I know all that, I know it better than all those experienced and wise advisers and nodders. But I am staying in Petersburg; I will not leave Petersburg! I won’t leave because... Oh, but it’s all the same, really, whether I leave or not.

And anyway: what can a decent man talk about with the greatest pleasure?

Answer: himself.

Well then, I too will talk about myself.
