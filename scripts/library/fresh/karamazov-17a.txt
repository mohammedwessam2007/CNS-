## Book Seven, Chapter II · Such a Moment

Father Paisy was not mistaken, of course, when he decided that his “dear boy” would come back again, and perhaps he even saw (not entirely, but still with insight) into the true meaning of Alyosha’s state of mind. Nevertheless I confess frankly that it would be very hard for me myself now to convey clearly the exact meaning of this strange and indefinite moment in the life of the hero of my story, so much loved by me and still so young. To Father Paisy’s sorrowful question, aimed at Alyosha, “Can you too be with those of little faith?” I could of course answer firmly for Alyosha: “No, he is not with those of little faith.” More than that: here it was even quite the opposite: all his confusion came precisely from his having believed much. But the confusion was there all the same; it did happen, and it was so tormenting that even afterwards, long afterwards, Alyosha considered that sorrowful day one of the most painful and fateful days of his life. If I am asked straight out, “Can all this anguish and such alarm really have arisen in him only because the body of his elder, instead of at once beginning to work cures, had on the contrary undergone an early corruption?”, I will answer without beating about the bush: “Yes, that is really how it was.” I would only ask the reader not to be in too much of a hurry to laugh at the pure heart of my young man. For my part, not only do I not intend to ask forgiveness for him, or to excuse and justify his simple faith by his youth, for instance, or by his small progress in the studies he had followed before, and so on, and so on, but I shall do the very opposite and declare firmly that I feel a sincere respect for the nature of his heart. No doubt another young man, who takes in the impressions of the heart cautiously, who already knows how to love not hotly but only warmly, with a mind that is sound but, for his age, much too reasonable (and therefore cheap), such a young man, I say, would have escaped what happened to mine; but in some cases, truly, it is more honourable to give way to some passion, unreasonable though it may be, yet arising from a great love, than not to give way to it at all. And all the more in youth, for a young man who is too constantly reasonable is not to be relied upon and is worth little: that is my opinion! “But,” sensible people will exclaim here perhaps, “not every young man can believe in such a superstition, and your young man is no example for the rest.” To this I will answer again: yes, my young man believed, believed sacredly and unshakeably, but all the same I do not ask forgiveness for him.

You see, though I declared above (and perhaps too hastily) that I would not explain, excuse or justify my hero, I see that something must still be made clear for the further understanding of the story. This is what I will say: it was not miracles exactly. It was not a frivolous expectation of miracles in its impatience. And it was not for the triumph of some convictions that Alyosha needed miracles then (that not at all), nor for some former, preconceived idea that was to triumph quickly over another; oh no, not at all. Here, in all this, before everything, in the first place, there stood before him a face, and only a face: the face of his beloved elder, the face of that righteous man whom he revered to the point of worship. That was just it: all the love for “all and everything” that lay hidden in his young and pure heart had at that time, and all through the year before it, been concentrated at moments, and perhaps even wrongly, on one being above all, at least in the strongest impulses of his heart: on his beloved elder, now departed. True, this being had stood before him so long as an undisputed ideal that all his young strength and all its striving could not but be directed exclusively towards this ideal, and at moments even to the point of forgetting “all and everything”. (He remembered afterwards himself that on that heavy day he had quite forgotten his brother Dmitri, about whom he had been so concerned and anxious the day before; forgotten too to take Ilyushechka’s father the two hundred roubles, which he had so ardently meant to do the day before as well.) But again it was not miracles he needed, but only “a higher justice”, which, as he believed, had been violated, and by which his heart had been so cruelly and suddenly wounded. And what does it matter that this “justice”, in Alyosha’s expectations, by the very course of things took the form of miracles expected at once from the remains of his adored former guide? Why, everyone in the monastery thought and expected the same, even those before whose intelligence Alyosha bowed, Father Paisy himself, for instance; and so Alyosha, not troubling himself with any doubts, clothed his dreams too in the same form in which everyone clothed theirs. Besides, it had long since been so arranged in his heart, through a whole year of his monastic life, and his heart had acquired the habit of expecting in this way. But it was justice he thirsted for, justice, and not miracles only! And now the one who ought, by his hopes, to have been exalted above all in the whole world, that very one, instead of the glory due to him, was suddenly cast down and disgraced! What for? Who had judged? Who could have judged so? These were the questions that at once tormented his inexperienced and virginal heart. He could not endure without offence, even without an anger of the heart, that the most righteous of the righteous should be given up to such mocking and spiteful derision by a crowd so frivolous and so far beneath him. Well, let there have been no miracles at all, let nothing wonderful have appeared and the immediate expectation not have been fulfilled; but why should dishonour appear, why should disgrace be permitted, why this hasty corruption that “forestalled nature”, as the spiteful monks said? Why this “sign”, which they now drew with such triumph, together with Father Ferapont, and why did they believe that they had even been given the right to draw it? Where was providence and its finger? Why had it hidden its finger “at the very moment it was needed” (thought Alyosha), as if it had itself wanted to submit to the blind, dumb, pitiless laws of nature?

That was why Alyosha’s heart was bleeding; and of course, as I have said, above all there stood a face, the one he loved more than anything in the world, and that same face “disgraced”, that same face “dishonoured”! This murmuring of my young man may have been frivolous and unreasonable; but again, I repeat for the third time (and I agree beforehand that perhaps this too is frivolous), I am glad that my young man turned out not to be so reasonable at such a moment, for reason will always come in time to a man who is no fool; but if there is no love in a young man’s heart at so exceptional a moment, when will it come? I will not, however, keep silent on this occasion about a certain strange thing that appeared, if only for an instant, in Alyosha’s mind at this fateful and confusing moment. This new thing that showed itself and flashed by consisted of a certain tormenting impression from his conversation of the day before with his brother Ivan, which Alyosha kept recalling unceasingly now. Precisely now. Oh, not that anything among the basic, elemental beliefs of his soul, so to speak, had been shaken. He loved his God and believed in him unshakeably, though he had suddenly murmured against him. But all the same, some vague but tormenting and evil impression from recalling the conversation of the day before with his brother Ivan suddenly stirred in his soul again now and more and more begged to come up to its surface. When it was already getting very dark, Rakitin, passing through the pine wood from the hermitage to the monastery, suddenly noticed Alyosha lying under a tree, face to the ground, motionless, as if asleep. He went up and called to him.

“You here, Alexei? Can you really have…” he began in surprise, but stopped without finishing. He had meant to say: “Can you really have come to this?” Alyosha did not look at him, but from a certain movement he made Rakitin guessed at once that he heard and understood him.

“But what is the matter with you?” he went on in surprise, but the surprise was already beginning to give way on his face to a smile that took on a more and more mocking expression.

“Listen, I have been looking for you for over two hours. You suddenly vanished from over there. What are you doing here? What sanctimonious nonsense is this? At least look at me…”

Alyosha raised his head, sat up and leaned his back against the tree. He was not crying, but his face expressed suffering, and irritation showed in his eyes. He was not looking at Rakitin, however, but somewhere to the side.

“You know, your face has quite changed. None of that famous meekness of yours. Are you angry with someone, or what? Has someone hurt you?”

“Leave me alone!” Alyosha said suddenly, still not looking at him, and waved his hand wearily.

“Oho, so that’s how we are! Shouting at people just like other mortals. And this from one of the angels! Well, Alyoshka, you have surprised me, do you know that? I say it sincerely. I haven’t been surprised by anything here for a long time. I did take you for an educated man, though…”

Alyosha looked at him at last, but somehow absently, as if he still hardly understood him.

“Can it really be just because your old man has begun to stink? Did you seriously believe he would start working miracles?” Rakitin exclaimed, passing again into the most sincere amazement.

“I believed, I believe, and I want to believe, and I will believe; what more do you want!” Alyosha shouted irritably.

“Nothing at all, my dear. Ugh, the devil, even a schoolboy of thirteen doesn’t believe that nowadays. But still, the devil… So now you are angry with your God, you have rebelled: passed over for promotion, as it were, not given a decoration for the holiday! Oh, you people!”

Alyosha looked at Rakitin long, with his eyes somehow narrowed, and something suddenly flashed in his eyes… but not anger at Rakitin.

“I am not rebelling against my God; I simply ‘do not accept his world’,” Alyosha suddenly smiled crookedly.

“What do you mean, you don’t accept his world?” Rakitin thought a little over his answer. “What gibberish is that?”

Alyosha did not answer.

“Well, enough of nonsense; now to business: have you eaten today?”

“I don’t remember… I think I have.”

“You need something to keep you going, to judge by your face. It makes one feel sorry to look at you. You didn’t sleep last night either; I heard you had a meeting there. And then all this fuss and mess… I expect you have chewed only a scrap of the holy bread. I have some sausage in my pocket; I brought it from town on my way here just in case; only you won’t eat sausage…”

“Give me the sausage.”

“Hey! So that’s how it is! So it’s a regular revolt, barricades! Well, brother, that is not to be sneezed at. Let’s go to my place… I could do with a drop of vodka myself now, I’m dead tired. I don’t suppose you’ll go so far as vodka… or will you?”

“Give me vodka too.”

“Well I never! This is a wonder, brother!” Rakitin looked at him wildly. “Well, one way or another, vodka or sausage, this is a fine, dashing business and not to be missed; come on!”

Alyosha got up from the ground in silence and went after Rakitin.

“If your brother Vanechka could see this, how amazed he would be! By the way, your brother Ivan Fyodorovich went off to Moscow this morning, did you know?”

“I know,” Alyosha said listlessly, and suddenly the image of his brother Dmitri flashed through his mind, but only flashed; and though it reminded him of something, some urgent business that could not be put off another minute, some debt, a terrible obligation, yet this memory too made no impression on him, did not reach his heart, and in the same instant flew out of his memory and was forgotten. But Alyosha remembered it long afterwards.

“Your brother Vanechka once declared that I was ‘a talentless liberal windbag’. And you too, once, couldn’t restrain yourself and let me understand that I was ‘dishonourable’… So be it! Now let’s have a look at your talent and honour” (Rakitin finished this to himself, in a whisper). “Ugh, listen!” he began again aloud. “Let’s go round the monastery and take the path straight into town… Hm. I ought to call in at Khokhlakova’s, by the way. Imagine: I wrote to her about everything that had happened, and, just think, she answers me at once with a note in pencil (that lady is terribly fond of writing notes), that ‘she never expected such conduct from so venerable an elder as Father Zosima!’ That’s just what she wrote: ‘conduct’! She’s angry too; oh, you people, all of you! Wait!” he suddenly shouted again, stopped all at once and, holding Alyosha by the shoulder, stopped him too.

“You know, Alyoshka,” he looked searchingly into his eyes, entirely under the impression of a sudden new thought that had just lit him up, and though he was laughing outwardly, he was evidently afraid to say this sudden new thought aloud, so little could he yet believe in the mood, strange to him and wholly unexpected, in which he now saw Alyosha. “Alyoshka, do you know where it would be best of all for us to go now?” he brought out at last, timidly and ingratiatingly.

“It doesn’t matter… wherever you like.”

“Let’s go to Grushenka’s, eh? Will you come?” Rakitin brought out at last, trembling all over with timid expectation.

“Let’s go to Grushenka’s,” Alyosha answered calmly and at once, and this was so unexpected for Rakitin, that is, such a quick and calm agreement, that he almost jumped back.

“W-well!… There!” he began to shout in amazement, but suddenly, taking Alyosha firmly by the arm, he quickly drew him along the path, still terribly afraid that his resolve would vanish. They walked in silence; Rakitin was even afraid to speak.

“And how glad she will be, how glad…” he began to mutter, but fell silent again. And it was not at all for Grushenka’s gladness that he was drawing Alyosha to her; he was a serious man and undertook nothing without an aim profitable to himself. His aim now was twofold: in the first place, vengeful, that is, to see “the disgrace of the righteous” and Alyosha’s probable “fall from saint to sinner”, which he was already gloating over in advance; and in the second place he had in view here a certain material aim, very profitable to him, of which more will be said below.

“So such a moment has come,” he thought to himself, gleefully and spitefully. “Well then, we’ll catch it by the scruff of the neck, this moment, for it suits us very well.”
