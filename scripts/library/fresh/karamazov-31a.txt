## Book Eleven, Chapter IX · The Devil. Ivan Fyodorovich’s Nightmare

I am not a doctor, and yet I feel that the moment has come when it is absolutely necessary for me to explain to the reader at least something of the nature of Ivan Fyodorovich’s illness. Running ahead, I will say only one thing: he was now, that evening, precisely on the very eve of brain fever, which at last took complete possession of his organism, long since disordered but stubbornly resisting the disease. Knowing nothing of medicine, I venture to suggest that perhaps by a terrible effort of will he really had managed for a time to put off the illness, dreaming, of course, of overcoming it altogether. He knew he was unwell, but he was loath to be ill at this time, in these approaching fateful moments of his life, when he had to be present, to speak his word boldly and decisively, and himself “to justify himself to himself”. He had, however, once been to the new doctor who had come from Moscow, sent for by Katerina Ivanovna because of a certain fancy of hers which I have already mentioned. The doctor, having listened to him and examined him, concluded that he had even something like a disorder of the brain, and was not in the least surprised by a certain confession which Ivan, with disgust, nevertheless made to him. “Hallucinations in your condition are very possible,” the doctor decided, “though they ought to be verified… In general you must begin serious treatment without losing a minute, or things will go badly.” But Ivan Fyodorovich, on leaving him, did not follow this sensible advice and neglected to go to bed for treatment. “I am walking about, I have strength for the present; if I collapse, that is another matter; then let anyone who likes treat me,” he decided, with a wave of his hand. And so he was sitting now, almost aware himself that he was delirious, and, as I have said, gazing persistently at some object on the sofa by the opposite wall. There, suddenly, someone turned out to be sitting, who had come in God knows how, for he had not been in the room when Ivan Fyodorovich came back from Smerdyakov’s and entered it. It was a gentleman, or, better to say, a Russian gentleman of a certain kind, no longer young, “qui frisait la cinquantaine”, as the French say, close on fifty, with not very much grey in his dark, rather long and still thick hair and in his small pointed beard. He was dressed in a sort of brown jacket, evidently from the best tailor, but already worn, made perhaps two years before and quite out of fashion, so that no well-to-do people of the world had worn such for two years. His linen, his long scarf-like tie, all was as with all smart gentlemen; but the linen, if one looked closer, was rather dirty, and the wide scarf very frayed. The visitor’s checked trousers fitted excellently, but again were too light and somehow too narrow, as they are no longer worn, as was the soft white felt hat which the visitor had brought with him, much out of season. In a word, there was a look of respectability on very slender means. It looked as if the gentleman belonged to the class of former idle landowners who flourished in the days of serfdom; evidently a man who had seen the world and decent society, who had once had connections and had kept them perhaps even to this day, but who, gradually impoverished after a gay life in his youth and the recent abolition of serfdom, had turned into a sort of well-bred hanger-on, going the rounds of good old acquaintances, who received him for his easy, accommodating character, and also in view of his being, after all, a respectable man whom one could even sit at one’s table in anyone’s presence, though of course in a modest place. Such hangers-on, gentlemen of accommodating character, who can tell a story, make up a hand at cards, and decidedly dislike being given any errands if they are thrust on them, are usually solitary, either bachelors or widowers; perhaps they have children, but their children are always being brought up somewhere far away, by some aunts or other, whom the gentleman almost never mentions in decent society, as if a little ashamed of such kinship. He gradually loses the habit of his children altogether, receiving from them now and then letters of congratulation for his name day and for Christmas, and sometimes even answering them. The unexpected visitor’s face was not so much good-natured as, again, accommodating and ready, according to circumstances, for any amiable expression. He had no watch, but he had a tortoiseshell lorgnette on a black ribbon. On the middle finger of his right hand gleamed a massive gold ring with a cheap opal. Ivan Fyodorovich was spitefully silent and did not want to begin talking. The visitor waited, and sat precisely like a hanger-on who has just come down from the room assigned to him to keep his host company at tea, but is meekly silent because his host is busy and thinking frowningly about something; ready, however, for any amiable conversation as soon as the host begins it. Suddenly his face expressed a sort of sudden concern.

“Listen,” he began to Ivan Fyodorovich, “forgive me, it is only to remind you: you went to Smerdyakov’s to find out about Katerina Ivanovna, and you came away without finding out anything about her; you must have forgotten…”

“Ah yes!” broke from Ivan suddenly, and his face darkened with worry. “Yes, I forgot… Still, it’s all the same now; everything till tomorrow,” he muttered to himself. “And you,” he turned irritably to the visitor, “I would have remembered that myself in a moment, because it was precisely that which was tormenting me with anguish! Just because you popped up, am I to believe that you prompted me, and that I didn’t remember it myself?”

“Then don’t believe it,” the gentleman smiled kindly. “What is faith by force? Besides, in matters of faith no proofs help, especially material ones. Thomas believed not because he saw the risen Christ but because he wanted to believe before that. Take the spiritualists, for instance… I am very fond of them… imagine, they suppose they are serving the faith because the devils show them their little horns from the other world. ‘That,’ they say, ‘is proof, material proof, so to speak, that the other world exists.’ The other world and material proofs, tra-la-la! And finally, if the devil is proved, it is still not known whether God is proved. I want to join an idealist society; I’ll be the opposition there: ‘I am a realist, you see, but not a materialist, he-he!’”

“Listen,” Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly got up from the table. “I am as if delirious now… and of course I am delirious… lie as you like; it’s all the same to me! You won’t drive me to frenzy as you did last time. Only I feel ashamed of something… I want to walk about the room… Sometimes I don’t see you and don’t even hear your voice, as last time, but I always guess what you are drivelling, because it is I, I myself who am speaking, and not you! Only I don’t know whether I was asleep last time or saw you awake. I’ll wet a towel with cold water and put it on my head, and perhaps you will evaporate.”

Ivan Fyodorovich went into the corner, took a towel, did as he said, and with the wet towel on his head began walking up and down the room.

“I like it that we have gone straight to saying ‘thou’ to each other,” the visitor began.

“Fool,” laughed Ivan, “do you think I shall say ‘you’ to you? I am cheerful now, only my temple aches… and the crown of my head… only, please, don’t philosophise as you did last time. If you can’t clear off, then tell me something amusing. Gossip; you are a hanger-on, so gossip. What a nightmare to fasten on one! But I am not afraid of you. I shall get the better of you. They won’t take me to a madhouse!”

“C’est charmant (charming): a hanger-on. Why, I am in my true character. What am I on earth but a hanger-on? By the way, I am listening to you and wondering a little: upon my word, you seem to be beginning little by little to take me for something real, and not only for your fancy, as you insisted last time…”

“Not for a single moment do I take you for the real truth,” Ivan cried, even with a sort of fury. “You are a lie, you are my illness, you are a phantom. Only I don’t know how to destroy you, and I see that I must suffer for a time. You are my hallucination. You are the incarnation of myself, but only of one side of me… of my thoughts and feelings, but only the nastiest and stupidest of them. From that side you might even be of interest to me, if only I had time to bother with you…”

“Allow me, allow me, I’ll catch you out: this evening by the lamp, when you flew at Alyosha and shouted at him: ‘You learned it from him! How did you know he comes to see me?’ You were thinking of me then. So for one little instant you did believe, you did believe that I really exist,” the gentleman laughed softly.

“Yes, that was a weakness of nature… but I couldn’t believe in you. I don’t know whether I was asleep or awake last time. Perhaps I only saw you in a dream then, and not awake at all…”

“And why were you so stern with him this evening, with Alyosha? He is a dear; I am to blame before him for Elder Zosima.”

“Not a word about Alyosha! How dare you, lackey!” Ivan laughed again.

“You swear, and yet you laugh: a good sign. You are much more amiable with me today than last time, though, and I understand why: that great resolution…”

“Not a word about the resolution!” Ivan cried savagely.

“I understand, I understand, c’est noble, c’est charmant (it is noble, it is charming); you are going to defend your brother tomorrow and sacrifice yourself… c’est chevaleresque (it is chivalrous).”

“Be quiet, or I’ll kick you!”

“I shall be partly glad, for then my aim will be achieved: if it comes to kicks, it means you believe in my reality, because one doesn’t kick a phantom. Joking aside: it is all the same to me; swear if you like; but still it is better to be a drop more polite, even with me. Otherwise ‘fool’ and ‘lackey’: what words!”

“Abusing you, I abuse myself!” Ivan laughed again. “You are I, I myself, only with a different mug. You say precisely what I already think… and you are unable to tell me anything new!”

“If I coincide with you in my thoughts, that only does me honour,” the gentleman said with delicacy and dignity.

“Only you take all my nasty thoughts, and above all the stupid ones. You are stupid and vulgar. You are terribly stupid. No, I can’t stand you! What am I to do, what am I to do!” Ivan ground out.

“My friend, all the same I want to be a gentleman and to be received as one,” the visitor began, in a fit of a certain purely hanger-on’s ambition, already yielding and good-natured in advance. “I am poor, but… I won’t say very honest, but… in society it is usually accepted as an axiom that I am a fallen angel. Upon my word, I can’t imagine how I could ever have been an angel. If I ever was, it was so long ago that it is no sin to have forgotten it. Now I value only my reputation as a decent man, and live as best I can, trying to be agreeable. I love people sincerely; oh, I have been much slandered! Here, when I move in with you from time to time, my life goes on like something real, and that is what I like most of all. For I too, like you, suffer from the fantastic, and that is why I love your earthly realism. Here with you everything is outlined; here there is formula, here there is geometry; while with us it is all some sort of indeterminate equations! I walk about here and dream. I love to dream. Besides, on earth I become superstitious; don’t laugh, please: that is just what I like, that I become superstitious. Here I take on all your habits: I have grown fond of going to the public baths, can you imagine that? And I love to steam myself with merchants and priests. My dream is to become incarnate, but once for all, irrevocably, in some fat merchant’s wife weighing two hundred and fifty pounds, and to believe everything she believes. My ideal is to go into a church and light a candle from a pure heart, upon my word. Then there would be an end to my sufferings. I have grown fond of being treated by your doctors too: in the spring there was smallpox about, and I went and was vaccinated at the foundling hospital; if you knew how pleased I was that day: I gave ten roubles for our Slav brothers!… But you aren’t listening. You know, you are very much out of sorts today,” the gentleman paused a little. “I know you went to that doctor yesterday… well, how is your health? What did the doctor tell you?”

“Fool!” Ivan snapped.

“How clever you are, though. Swearing again? I don’t ask out of sympathy, just like that. Don’t answer if you like. Now the rheumatism has started again…”

“Fool,” Ivan repeated again.

“You keep on with your own; but last year I caught such a rheumatism that I still remember it.”

“The devil has rheumatism?”

“Why not, if I become incarnate sometimes? If I become incarnate, I accept the consequences. Satan sum et nihil humanum a me alienum puto (I am Satan, and I count nothing human foreign to me).”

“What, what? Satan sum et nihil humanum… That isn’t stupid, for a devil!”

“Glad to have pleased you at last.”

“But you didn’t get that from me,” Ivan suddenly stopped, as if struck. “That never entered my head; that is strange…”

“C’est du nouveau, n’est-ce pas? (That is something new, isn’t it?) This time I shall act honestly and explain it to you. Listen: in dreams, and especially in nightmares, well, from indigestion or something, a man sometimes sees such artistic dreams, such a complex and real reality, such events, or even a whole world of events, woven together with such a plot, with such unexpected details, from your highest manifestations to the last button on a shirt front, that, I swear to you, Lev Tolstoy could not invent it; and yet such dreams are sometimes seen not by writers at all, but by the most ordinary people, officials, journalists, priests… There is even a whole problem about it: one minister even confessed to me himself that all his best ideas come to him when he is asleep. Well, so it is now. Though I am your hallucination, yet, as in a nightmare, I say original things that have never entered your head till now, so that I am not repeating your thoughts at all; and yet I am only your nightmare and nothing more.”

“You lie. Your aim is precisely to convince me that you exist in yourself and are not my nightmare, and now you yourself confirm that you are a dream.”

“My friend, today I have adopted a special method; I’ll explain it to you later. Wait, where did I stop? Yes, I caught cold then, only not here with you, but still over there…”

“Where over there? Tell me, will you be staying with me long; can’t you go away?” Ivan exclaimed almost in despair. He stopped walking, sat down on the sofa, again leaned his elbows on the table and squeezed his head in both hands. He tore the wet towel off and threw it aside in vexation: evidently it did not help.

“Your nerves are out of order,” the gentleman observed with a free-and-easy, careless, but perfectly friendly air. “You are angry with me even for being able to catch cold, and yet it happened in the most natural way. I was hurrying then to a diplomatic evening at the house of a lady of high Petersburg society who was aiming at ministers. Well, evening dress, white tie, gloves; and yet I was still God knows where, and to reach you on earth I still had to fly through space… of course it is only an instant, but then a ray of light from the sun takes a full eight minutes; and imagine, in evening dress and an open waistcoat. Spirits don’t freeze, but once I had become incarnate… in a word, I was reckless and set off; and in those spaces, you know, in the ether, in ‘the waters which were above the firmament’, why, it is such frost… I mean, what frost? It can’t even be called frost; can you imagine: a hundred and fifty degrees below zero! You know the village girls’ game: in thirty degrees of frost they invite a newcomer to lick an axe; his tongue freezes to it instantly, and the dolt tears the skin off it till it bleeds. And that is only at thirty degrees; but at a hundred and fifty, I think if you merely put your finger to the axe, there would be no finger left, if only… if only there could be an axe there…”

“And can there be an axe there?” Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly interrupted, absently and with disgust. He was resisting with all his might, so as not to believe in his delirium and fall finally into madness.

“An axe?” the visitor asked in surprise.

“Well, yes, what would become of an axe there?” Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly cried with a sort of savage and insistent obstinacy.

“What would become of an axe in space? Quelle idée! (What an idea!) If it landed far enough out, it would begin, I think, to fly round the earth, without knowing why itself, as a satellite. The astronomers would calculate the rising and setting of the axe, Gatsuk would put it in his almanac, and that’s all.”

“You are stupid, terribly stupid!” Ivan said perversely. “Lie more cleverly, or I won’t listen. You want to overcome me with realism, to convince me that you exist; but I don’t want to believe that you exist! I won’t believe it!!”

“But I am not lying; it is all true; unfortunately the truth is almost always unwitty. I see you are decidedly expecting something great of me, and perhaps something beautiful. That is a great pity, for I give only what I can.”

“Don’t philosophise, you ass!”

“What philosophy is there, when my whole right side is paralysed and I groan and moan? I have been to all the doctors: they diagnose splendidly; they will tell you the whole illness on their fingers; but cure it they can’t. There was an enthusiastic little student here: ‘If you do die,’ he says, ‘at least you will know perfectly what illness you died of!’ And again this way of theirs of sending you to specialists: we only diagnose, they say, but go to such and such a specialist, and he will cure you. The old doctor who treated all diseases has quite, quite disappeared, I tell you; now there are only specialists, and they all advertise in the newspapers. If your nose aches, they send you to Paris: there, they say, is a European specialist who treats noses. You go to Paris; he examines your nose: ‘I can only cure your right nostril,’ he says, ‘for I don’t treat left nostrils; that is not my speciality; but after me go to Vienna; there a special specialist will finish curing your left nostril.’ What is one to do? I resorted to folk remedies; a German doctor advised me to rub myself with honey and salt on the top shelf at the baths. I went, solely so as to go to the baths once more: I got myself all smeared, and no good at all. In despair I wrote to Count Mattei in Milan; he sent a book and some drops, God bless him. And imagine: Hoff’s malt extract helped! I bought it by chance, drank a bottle and a half, and I could have danced; it all went as if by magic. I resolved without fail to print a ‘thank you’ to him in the newspapers; my feeling of gratitude spoke up; and imagine, then another story began: not one editorial office would take it! ‘It will be very retrograde,’ they say; ‘no one will believe it; le diable n’existe point (the devil does not exist any more). Publish it anonymously,’ they advise. Well, what sort of ‘thank you’ is it, if it is anonymous? I laugh with the clerks: ‘It is believing in God that is retrograde in our age,’ I say, ‘but I am the devil; one may believe in me.’ ‘We understand,’ they say; ‘who doesn’t believe in the devil? But still it can’t be done; it might harm our tendency. Perhaps as a joke?’ Well, as a joke, I thought, it would be unwitty. So they didn’t print it. And would you believe it, it has even stayed on my heart. My very best feelings, gratitude for instance, are formally forbidden me solely by my social position.”

“Into philosophy again!” Ivan ground out with hatred.

“God preserve me; but one can’t help complaining sometimes. I am a slandered man. Here you keep telling me every minute that I am stupid. That is just like a young man. My friend, intelligence isn’t everything! I have by nature a kind and merry heart; ‘I too write little vaudevilles, you know.’ You seem decidedly to take me for a grey-haired Khlestakov, and yet my fate is much more serious. By some pre-temporal decree, which I have never been able to make out, I have been appointed ‘to deny’, and yet I am sincerely kind and quite incapable of denying. No, go and deny; without denial, they say, there will be no criticism; and what sort of journal is it without a ‘critical department’? Without criticism there would be nothing but ‘hosanna’. But ‘hosanna’ alone is not enough for life; this ‘hosanna’ must pass through the crucible of doubt, and so on, in that vein. I don’t meddle in all that, though; I didn’t create it, and I am not answerable for it. Well, they chose a scapegoat; they made me write in the critical department, and life resulted. We understand this comedy: I, for instance, simply and plainly demand to be annihilated. No, live, they say, because without you there will be nothing. If everything on earth were sensible, nothing would happen. Without you there will be no events, and there must be events. So I serve against my will, so that there may be events, and I do the unreasonable by order. People take this whole comedy for something serious, even with all their undoubted intelligence. That is their tragedy. Well, and they suffer, of course, but… still, they live; they live really, not fantastically; for suffering is life. Without suffering what pleasure would there be in it? Everything would turn into one endless prayer service: it is holy, but a little dull. And I? I suffer, and still I don’t live. I am the x in an indeterminate equation. I am some sort of phantom of life who has lost all ends and beginnings, and has even forgotten at last what to call himself. You laugh… no, you are not laughing; you are angry again. You are for ever angry; all you want is intelligence; but I repeat to you again that I would give all this super-stellar life, all ranks and honours, just to become incarnate in the soul of a two-hundred-and-fifty-pound merchant’s wife and light candles to God.”

“So even you don’t believe in God?” Ivan smiled with hatred.

“How shall I put it to you, if you are serious…”

“Is there a God or not?” Ivan cried again with savage insistence.

“Ah, so you are serious? My dear fellow, upon my word, I don’t know. There, I have said a great word.”

“You don’t know, and yet you see God? No, you are not by yourself; you are I; you are I and nothing more! You are rubbish; you are my fancy!”

“That is, if you like, I share your philosophy; that would be fair. Je pense, donc je suis (I think, therefore I am); that I know for certain; but all the rest around me, all these worlds, God and even Satan himself, all that is not proved for me: whether it exists in itself or is only my emanation, a consistent development of my self, which exists pre-temporally and uniquely… in a word, I break off quickly, because I think you are about to jump up and fight.”

“You had better tell me some anecdote!” Ivan said painfully.

“There is an anecdote, and precisely on our subject; that is, not an anecdote, just a legend. You reproach me with unbelief: ‘you see, and yet you don’t believe’. But, my friend, I am not the only one like that; everyone over there has gone muddled now, and all because of your sciences. While there were still atoms, the five senses, the four elements, well, then everything held together somehow. There were atoms in the ancient world too. But as soon as we learned over there that you had discovered the ‘chemical molecule’ and ‘protoplasm’ and the devil knows what else, we simply put our tails between our legs. A regular hubbub began; above all superstition, gossip; for there is as much gossip with us as with you, even a little more; and finally informing too; for we too have a certain department where certain ‘information’ is received. So, this wild legend, from our Middle Ages, not yours but ours, and no one believes it even among us, except two-hundred-and-fifty-pound merchants’ wives, again not yours but ours. Everything you have, we have too; I am telling you one of our secrets out of friendship, though it is forbidden. The legend is about paradise. There was, they say, a certain thinker and philosopher here on your earth, who ‘rejected everything, laws, conscience, faith’, and above all the future life. He died, thinking he would go straight into darkness and death, and there before him was the future life. He was amazed and indignant: ‘This,’ he said, ‘goes against my convictions.’ And for that he was sentenced… that is, you see, excuse me, I am only passing on what I heard; it is only a legend… he was sentenced, you see, to walk a quadrillion kilometres in darkness (we have gone over to kilometres now, you know), and when he finishes that quadrillion, the gates of paradise will be opened to him and everything forgiven…”

“And what torments have you in the other world, besides the quadrillion?” Ivan interrupted with a strange animation.

“What torments? Ah, don’t ask: before, there was this and that, but nowadays they are mostly moral ones, ‘pangs of conscience’ and all that nonsense. That too came from you, from ‘the softening of your manners’. And who has gained by it? Only the unscrupulous, for what are pangs of conscience to a man who has no conscience at all? Instead, decent people have suffered, who still had some conscience and honour left… There you are: reforms on unprepared soil, and copied from foreign institutions too: nothing but harm! The old fire was better. Well, this man sentenced to the quadrillion stood, looked, and lay down across the road: ‘I don’t want to go; on principle I won’t go!’ Take the soul of a Russian enlightened atheist and mix it with the soul of the prophet Jonah, who sulked in the belly of the whale for three days and three nights, and there you have the character of this thinker who lay down on the road.”

“And what did he lie down on there?”

“Well, there must have been something there. You aren’t laughing?”

“Good man!” cried Ivan, still with the same strange animation. He was now listening with a sort of unexpected curiosity. “Well, and is he lying there still?”

“That’s just it: no. He lay there for almost a thousand years, and then got up and went.”

“What an ass!” exclaimed Ivan, laughing nervously, and still as if intently thinking something out. “Isn’t it all the same whether he lies there for ever or walks a quadrillion versts? Why, that is a billion years’ walk, isn’t it?”

“Much more even; only I haven’t a pencil and paper, or one could work it out. But he got there long ago, and that is where the anecdote begins.”

“What, he got there! But where did he get a billion years from?”

“Why, you keep thinking of our present earth! But the present earth may itself have repeated itself a billion times; well, it lived out its time, froze, cracked, crumbled, broke down into its constituent elements; again ‘the waters which were above the firmament’, then a comet again, a sun again, from the sun the earth again; why, this development may have repeated itself an infinite number of times already, and always in exactly the same form, down to the last detail. A most indecent bore…”

“Well, well, what happened when he got there?”

“Why, as soon as they opened paradise to him and he went in, before he had been there two seconds, and that by the watch, by the watch (though his watch, in my opinion, must long since have broken down into its constituent elements in his pocket on the way), before he had been there two seconds he exclaimed that for those two seconds it was worth walking not only a quadrillion, but a quadrillion of quadrillions, and raised to the quadrillionth power! In a word, he sang ‘hosanna’, and overdid it, so that some there, with a nobler cast of mind, at first even refused to shake hands with him: he had jumped over to the conservatives too precipitately, they said. The Russian nature. I repeat: it is a legend. I sell it as I bought it. That is the kind of ideas that are going about among us there about all these subjects.”

“I have caught you!” Ivan cried with an almost childish joy, as if he had finally remembered something. “That anecdote about the quadrillion years, I made it up myself! I was seventeen then, I was at the high school… I made up that anecdote then and told it to a schoolfellow; his name was Korovkin; it was in Moscow… The anecdote is so characteristic that I couldn’t have got it from anywhere. I had forgotten it… but it has come back to me now unconsciously, to me myself; you didn’t tell it! As thousands of things sometimes come back unconsciously, even when one is being taken to execution… it came back to me in a dream. You are that dream! You are a dream and you don’t exist!”

“From the passion with which you reject me,” the gentleman laughed, “I am convinced that you believe in me all the same.”

“Not in the least! I don’t believe in you one hundredth part!”

“But a thousandth part you do. Homoeopathic doses are perhaps the strongest. Admit that you believe, well, to the ten-thousandth part…”

“Not for one minute!” Ivan cried furiously. “Though I should like to believe in you!” he strangely added suddenly.

“Aha! There’s an admission, though! But I am kind; I will help you here too. Listen: it is I who have caught you, not you me! I told you your own anecdote, which you had forgotten, on purpose, so that you would lose your belief in me completely.”

“You lie! The aim of your appearing is to convince me that you exist.”

“Precisely. But hesitation, but uneasiness, but the struggle between belief and unbelief: that is sometimes such torment for a conscientious man like you that it is better to hang oneself. Knowing that you believe in me a tiny bit, I deliberately let in some unbelief once for all by telling you that anecdote. I lead you alternately between belief and unbelief, and I have my own aim in it. A new method, sir: for when you have lost your belief in me completely, you will at once begin assuring me to my face that I am not a dream but really exist; I know you already; and then I shall have achieved my aim. And my aim is a noble one. I shall throw only a tiny seed of faith into you, and from it an oak will grow, and such an oak that you, sitting in the oak, will want to join ‘the desert fathers and the holy women’; for secretly you want that very, very much; you will eat locusts; you will drag yourself off to the wilderness to save your soul!”

“So you, you scoundrel, are working for the salvation of my soul?”

“One must do a good deed sometime. How angry you are, though, I see!”

“Buffoon! And did you ever tempt such ones as that, who eat locusts and pray for seventeen years in the bare wilderness till they are overgrown with moss?”

“My dear fellow, that is all I have ever done. You forget the whole world and worlds, and fasten on to one such, because the diamond is so very precious; one such soul is sometimes worth a whole constellation; we have our own arithmetic. The victory is precious! And some of them, upon my word, are not beneath you in development, though you won’t believe it: they can contemplate such abysses of belief and unbelief at one and the same moment that sometimes, truly, it seems that only one more hair, and the man will go flying ‘head over heels’, as the actor Gorbunov says.”

“Well, and did you go away with a long nose?”

“My friend,” the visitor remarked sententiously, “it is better, after all, to go away with a long nose than sometimes with no nose at all, as an ailing marquis (a specialist must have treated him) said not long ago at confession to his spiritual father, a Jesuit. I was present; simply charming. ‘Give me back my nose!’ he says, and beats his breast. ‘My son,’ the father wriggles, ‘by the inscrutable decrees of providence everything is made good, and a visible misfortune sometimes brings with it an extraordinary, though invisible, advantage. If stern fate has deprived you of your nose, your advantage is that now no one in all your life will dare to say that you were left with a long nose.’ ‘Holy father, that is no consolation!’ cries the desperate man. ‘On the contrary, I would be delighted all my life to be left with a long nose every day, if only it were in its proper place!’ ‘My son,’ sighs the father, ‘one cannot demand all blessings at once, and this is already murmuring against providence, which even here has not forgotten you; for if you cry out, as you cried out just now, that you would gladly be left with a long nose all your life, then even here your wish has already been fulfilled indirectly; for, having lost your nose, you have by that very thing, as it were, been left with a long nose…’”

“Ugh, how stupid!” cried Ivan.

“My friend, I only wanted to make you laugh; but I swear it is real Jesuit casuistry, and I swear it all happened word for word as I have told you. It was a recent case and caused me a lot of trouble. The unhappy young man went home and shot himself that same night; I was with him constantly to the last moment… As for those little Jesuit confession boxes, they are truly my sweetest diversion in the sad moments of life. Here is another case for you, only the other day. A little blonde comes to an old priest, a Norman girl of about twenty. Beauty, figure, nature: it makes your mouth water. She bends down and whispers her sin to the priest through the little hole. ‘What, my daughter, have you fallen again already?’ exclaims the priest. ‘O Sancta Maria (O holy Mary), what do I hear: and not with the same one this time. But how long is this to go on, and how is it you are not ashamed!’ ‘Ah, mon père (ah, my father),’ answers the sinner, all in penitent tears, ‘ça lui fait tant de plaisir, et à moi si peu de peine!’ (‘it gives him so much pleasure, and me so little trouble!’) Well, imagine such an answer! Even I drew back at that: it is the cry of nature itself; it is, if you like, better than innocence itself! I absolved her sin on the spot and turned to go, but I was at once forced to come back: I hear the priest through the little hole making an appointment with her for the evening; and the old man was flint, and he fell in an instant! Nature, the truth of nature, had its way! What, turning up your nose again, angry again? I really don’t know how to please you…”

“Leave me alone; you are knocking in my brain like an inescapable nightmare,” Ivan moaned painfully, powerless before his vision. “I am bored with you, unbearably and tormentingly! I would give a great deal to be able to drive you away!”

“I repeat, moderate your demands; don’t demand ‘everything great and beautiful’ of me, and you will see how amicably we shall get on together,” the gentleman said impressively. “Truly, you are angry with me because I didn’t appear to you in some red glow, ‘thundering and flashing’, with scorched wings, but have presented myself in such a modest form. You are offended, in the first place, in your aesthetic feelings, and in the second, in your pride: how, you think, could such a vulgar devil come to such a great man? No, there is that romantic streak in you, which Belinsky laughed at so long ago. What’s to be done, young man? I thought this evening, as I was getting ready to come to you, of appearing for a joke as a retired actual state councillor who had served in the Caucasus, with the star of the Lion and the Sun on my coat; but I was decidedly afraid, for you would have beaten me just for daring to pin the Lion and the Sun on my coat, and not at least the Pole Star or Sirius. And you keep on that I am stupid. But my God, I have no pretensions to compare with you in intelligence. Mephistopheles, appearing to Faust, testified of himself that he wills evil and does only good. Well, that is as he pleases; I am quite the opposite. I am perhaps the only being in all nature who loves the truth and sincerely desires good. I was there when the Word, who died on the cross, was ascending into heaven, carrying on his bosom the soul of the thief crucified on his right hand; I heard the joyful shrieks of the cherubim singing and crying ‘Hosanna’, and the thunderous cry of rapture of the seraphim, which shook heaven and all creation. And, I swear by all that is holy, I wanted to join the choir and cry with them all: ‘Hosanna!’ It was already escaping, already bursting from my breast… for, you know, I am very sensitive and artistically impressionable. But common sense, oh, the unhappiest trait of my nature, kept me within due bounds even then, and I let the moment pass! For what, I thought at that same moment, what would have come of it after my ‘hosanna’? Everything in the world would have gone out at once, and no events would have happened any more. And so, solely from a sense of duty and because of my social position, I was forced to stifle the good moment in myself and remain with the dirty tricks. Someone takes all the honour of goodness for himself, and only dirty tricks are left as my portion. But I don’t envy the honour of living by sponging; I am not ambitious. Why, of all creatures in the world, am I alone condemned to be cursed by all decent people, and even to be kicked with boots, for, becoming incarnate, I must sometimes accept even such consequences? I know there is a secret in it, but they won’t reveal the secret to me for anything, because then, perhaps, guessing what is what, I would bellow ‘hosanna’, and at once the indispensable minus would vanish, and good sense would begin all over the world, and with it, of course, the end of everything, even of newspapers and journals, for who would subscribe to them then? I know that in the end I shall be reconciled; I too shall walk my quadrillion and learn the secret. But until that happens, I sulk, and against my will I fulfil my destiny: to ruin thousands so that one may be saved. How many souls, for instance, had to be ruined and how many honest reputations disgraced to get just one righteous Job, over whom they caught me out so spitefully in days of old! No, until the secret is revealed, there are two truths for me: one over there, theirs, quite unknown to me as yet, and the other mine. And it is not yet known which will turn out the cleaner… Have you fallen asleep?”

“I should think so,” Ivan moaned spitefully. “Everything that is stupid in my nature, long since lived through, ground over in my mind, thrown away like carrion, you bring me as if it were some novelty!”

“Missed again! And I thought I would even charm you with my literary style: that ‘hosanna’ in heaven, really, I did it rather well, didn’t I? And then this sarcastic tone just now, à la Heine, eh, isn’t that so?”

“No, I was never such a lackey! How could my soul have given birth to such a lackey as you?”

“My friend, I know a most charming and most delightful young Russian gentleman: a young thinker and a great lover of literature and elegant things, author of a poem of great promise entitled ‘The Grand Inquisitor’… It was only him I had in mind!”

“I forbid you to speak of ‘The Grand Inquisitor’,” Ivan exclaimed, crimson all over with shame.

“Well, and ‘The Geological Upheaval’? Do you remember? Now that was a little poem!”

“Be quiet, or I’ll kill you!”

“Kill me, you mean? No, excuse me, I’ll have my say. That is what I came for, to treat myself to this pleasure. Oh, I love the dreams of my ardent young friends, trembling with thirst for life! ‘There are new people,’ you decided last spring, when you were getting ready to come here; ‘they propose to destroy everything and begin with cannibalism. Fools, they didn’t ask me! In my opinion there is no need to destroy anything; one need only destroy the idea of God in mankind; that is where one must set to work! That, that is where one must begin; oh, blind men, who understand nothing! Once mankind has renounced God, every one of them (and I believe that this period, parallel to the geological periods, will come about), then of itself, without cannibalism, the whole former world view will fall, and above all the whole former morality, and everything new will begin. People will unite to take from life all that it can give, but only for happiness and joy in this world alone. Man will be exalted with a spirit of divine, titanic pride, and the man-god will appear. Conquering nature hourly, now without limits, by his will and by science, man will by that very thing hourly feel so lofty a delight that it will replace for him all his former hopes of heavenly delights. Everyone will know that he is wholly mortal, without resurrection, and will accept death proudly and calmly, like a god. Out of pride he will understand that he has no reason to murmur that life is a moment, and he will love his brother without any reward. Love will satisfy only a moment of life, but the very consciousness of its momentariness will strengthen its fire as much as it was formerly dissipated in hopes of a love beyond the grave and infinite…’ and so on, and so on in the same vein. Most charming!”

Ivan was sitting with his hands pressed over his ears, looking at the ground, but he had begun to tremble all over. The voice went on:

“The question now, my young thinker thought, is this: is it possible that such a period will ever come or not? If it comes, everything is decided, and mankind will be settled once for all. But since, in view of man’s inveterate stupidity, it may not be settled even in a thousand years, everyone who already knows the truth even now is permitted to settle himself entirely as he pleases, on the new principles. In that sense ‘everything is permitted’ to him. More than that: even if this period never comes at all, still, since there is no God and no immortality anyway, the new man is permitted to become a man-god, even if he is the only one in the whole world, and of course, in his new rank, to leap over with a light heart every former moral barrier of the former slave-man, if need be. For God there is no law! Where God stands, there is God’s place! Where I stand, there will at once be the first place… ‘everything is permitted’, and that’s that! All this is very charming; only, if one wants to swindle, why, one would think, the sanction of truth as well? But such is our modern Russian little man: he won’t bring himself to swindle without a sanction, so much has he come to love the truth…”

The visitor was talking, evidently carried away by his own eloquence, raising his voice more and more and looking mockingly at his host; but he did not manage to finish: Ivan suddenly seized a glass from the table and flung it with all his might at the orator.

“Ah, mais c’est bête enfin! (Ah, but this is stupid, really!)” exclaimed the latter, jumping up from the sofa and flicking the drops of tea off himself with his fingers. “He remembered Luther’s inkpot! He thinks me a dream himself, and he throws glasses at a dream! That is like a woman! And I suspected that you were only pretending to stop your ears, and you were listening…”

Suddenly there was a firm and insistent knocking at the window frame from the yard. Ivan Fyodorovich jumped up from the sofa.

“Do you hear? You had better open,” cried the visitor. “It is your brother Alyosha with the most unexpected and curious news, I can answer for it!”

“Be quiet, you deceiver; I knew it was Alyosha before you did; I had a presentiment of him; and of course he has not come for nothing; of course he has ‘news’!…” Ivan exclaimed in a frenzy.

“Open, then, open to him. There is a blizzard outside, and he is your brother. Monsieur, sait-il le temps qu’il fait? C’est à ne pas mettre un chien dehors… (Does monsieur know what weather it is? One wouldn’t put a dog out in it…)”

The knocking went on. Ivan wanted to rush to the window; but something seemed suddenly to bind his legs and arms. He strained with all his might as if to break his bonds, but in vain. The knocking at the window grew more and more and louder. At last the bonds suddenly broke, and Ivan Fyodorovich jumped up on the sofa. He looked round wildly. Both candles had almost burned out; the glass he had just thrown at his visitor stood before him on the table; and on the opposite sofa there was no one. The knocking at the window frame, though it went on insistently, was not nearly so loud as it had seemed to him just now in his dream; on the contrary, it was very restrained.

“It was not a dream! No, I swear it was not a dream; it all happened just now!” cried Ivan Fyodorovich, rushed to the window and opened the small pane.

“Alyosha, I told you not to come!” he shouted savagely to his brother. “In two words: what do you want? In two words, do you hear?”

“An hour ago Smerdyakov hanged himself,” Alyosha answered from the yard.

“Come round to the porch; I’ll open the door to you at once,” said Ivan, and went to let Alyosha in.
