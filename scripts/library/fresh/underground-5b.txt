## X

A quarter of an hour later I was running back and forth about the room in furious impatience, every minute going up to the screen and peeping through the crack at Liza. She was sitting on the floor with her head leaning against the bed, and must have been crying. But she did not go away, and that was what irritated me. This time she knew everything. I had insulted her finally, but... there is no need to tell it. She had guessed that the outburst of my passion was precisely revenge, a new humiliation for her, and that to my earlier, almost objectless hatred there was now added a personal, envious hatred of her... However, I don’t claim that she understood all this clearly; but she did understand fully that I was a vile man and, above all, incapable of loving her.

I know I shall be told that this is incredible, that it is incredible to be as spiteful and stupid as I was; perhaps they will add that it was incredible not to love her, or at least not to value that love. Why incredible? In the first place, I could no longer love, because, I repeat, to love meant for me to tyrannise and to be morally superior. All my life I could not even imagine any other love, and I have come to the point that I sometimes think now that love consists precisely in the right, voluntarily given by the beloved, to tyrannise over her. Even in my underground dreams I never imagined love except as a struggle; I always began it with hatred and ended it with moral subjugation, and afterwards I could not even imagine what to do with the subjugated object. And what is incredible in it, when I had already managed to corrupt myself morally so far, had become so unused to “living life”, that just now I had thought of reproaching her and shaming her for having come to me to hear “pitiful words”; and I had not guessed myself that she had come not at all to hear pitiful words, but to love me, because for a woman it is in love that all resurrection lies, all salvation from any ruin whatever, and all rebirth; and it cannot show itself in any other way. However, I did not hate her so very much when I was running about the room and peeping through the crack behind the screen. It was only unbearably hard for me that she was here. I wanted her to vanish. I wanted “peace”; I wanted to be left alone in the underground. “Living life”, which I was not used to, had crushed me so that it was even hard to breathe.

But several more minutes passed, and still she did not get up, as if she were unconscious. I had the shamelessness to knock softly on the screen to remind her... She suddenly started, jumped up, and rushed to look for her shawl, her hat, her coat, as if escaping from me somewhere... Two minutes later she came slowly out from behind the screen and looked at me heavily. I grinned spitefully, but forcibly, for appearance’s sake, and turned away from her look.

“Goodbye,” she said, going towards the door.

I suddenly ran up to her, seized her hand, opened it, put something in it... and closed it again. Then at once I turned away and sprang quickly into the other corner, so as at least not to see...

I wanted to lie just now, to write that I did this accidentally, beside myself, at a loss, foolishly. But I don’t want to lie, and so I say straight out that I opened her hand and put that in it... out of spite. It occurred to me to do it while I was running back and forth about the room and she was sitting behind the screen. But this I can say for certain: I did this cruel thing, though on purpose, not from my heart but from my wretched head. This cruelty was so put-on, so cerebral, so deliberately invented, so bookish, that I could not bear it myself even for a minute: first I sprang into the corner so as not to see, and then in shame and despair I rushed after Liza. I opened the door to the passage and began to listen.

“Liza! Liza!” I called down the stairs, but timidly, in an undertone...

There was no answer; I thought I heard her steps on the bottom stairs.

“Liza!” I called louder.

No answer. But at that very moment I heard from below the stiff glass outer door to the street open heavily, with a screech, and slam stiffly shut. The boom rose up the stairs.

She had gone. I went back into the room, deep in thought. I felt terribly heavy.

I stopped by the table, next to the chair on which she had sat, and stared senselessly in front of me. About a minute passed; suddenly I started all over: right in front of me, on the table, I saw... in a word, I saw a crumpled blue five-rouble note, the very one I had pressed into her hand a minute before. It was that note; it could not have been any other; there was no other in the house. So she had managed to throw it from her hand onto the table at the moment when I sprang into the other corner.

Well? I might have expected her to do that. Might have expected it? No. I was such an egoist, I respected people so little in reality, that I could not even imagine that she would do it. That I could not bear. A moment later, like a madman, I rushed to dress, threw on whatever I could in my haste, and ran out headlong after her. She could not have gone two hundred paces when I ran out into the street.

It was quiet; the snow was falling thickly, almost straight down, laying a cushion on the pavement and the empty street. There were no passers-by; not a sound could be heard. The street lamps flickered gloomily and uselessly. I ran some two hundred paces to the crossroads and stopped.

“Where did she go? And why am I running after her? Why? To fall down before her, to sob with remorse, to kiss her feet, to beg for forgiveness! I did want that; my whole breast was being torn to pieces, and never, never shall I remember that moment with indifference. But why?” I thought. “Won’t I hate her, perhaps tomorrow, precisely because I kissed her feet today? Will I give her happiness? Haven’t I learned again today, for the hundredth time, what I am worth? Won’t I torment her to death?”

I stood in the snow, peering into the murky haze, and thought about this.

“And won’t it be better, won’t it be better,” I fantasised afterwards at home, drowning the living pain in my heart with fantasies, “won’t it be better if she carries away the insult with her now for ever? An insult: why, that is purification; it is the most stinging and painful consciousness! Tomorrow I would have soiled her soul with myself and worn out her heart. But now the insult will never die in her; and however vile the filth that awaits her, the insult will raise her up and purify her... by hatred... hm... perhaps by forgiveness too... But will it all make things any easier for her?”

And really: here I am now asking an idle question of my own: which is better, cheap happiness or exalted suffering? Well, which is better?

So I dreamed as I sat at home that evening, barely alive with the pain in my soul. Never had I endured so much suffering and remorse; but could there have been the slightest doubt, when I ran out of the flat, that I would turn back home halfway? I never met Liza again, and heard nothing of her. I will add, too, that for a long time I remained pleased with my phrase about the usefulness of insult and hatred, although I nearly fell ill from misery at the time.

Even now, after so many years, all this comes back to me somehow too painfully. Much comes back to me painfully now, but... hadn’t I better end my “Notes” here? I think I made a mistake in beginning to write them. At least I have been ashamed all the while I was writing this story: so it is no longer literature but a corrective punishment. For to tell long stories, for example, about how I failed in my life through moral corruption in a corner, through the lack of proper surroundings, through being cut off from living life, and through vain spite in the underground: by God, it is not interesting. A novel needs a hero, and here all the traits of an anti-hero have been collected on purpose; and above all, all this will make a most unpleasant impression, because we have all lost touch with life, we are all lame, every one of us, more or less. We have lost touch with it so much that we sometimes feel a kind of loathing for real “living life”, and so we cannot bear to be reminded of it. Why, we have come to the point of almost regarding real “living life” as hard work, almost as a job, and we all agree in private that it is better in books. And why do we sometimes fuss about, why do we carry on, what are we asking for? We don’t know ourselves. It would be worse for us if our whimsical requests were granted. Go on, try: give us, for example, a little more independence, untie the hands of any one of us, widen our sphere of activity, relax the supervision, and we... why, I assure you, we would at once ask to be taken back under supervision. I know you will perhaps be angry with me for this, shout and stamp your feet: “Speak for yourself alone and your miseries in the underground, but don’t you dare say ‘all of us’!” Excuse me, gentlemen; I am not justifying myself with this “all of us”. As for me in particular, I have only carried to an extreme in my life what you have not dared to carry even halfway; and you have taken your cowardice for good sense, and found comfort in that, deceiving yourselves. So that perhaps I am even more “alive” than you. Look more closely! Why, we don’t even know where living life lives now, or what it is, or what it is called. Leave us alone, without books, and we shall at once get confused and lost; we shall not know what to join, what to hold on to, what to love and what to hate, what to respect and what to despise. We find it a burden even to be human beings, human beings with our own real bodies and blood; we are ashamed of it, we think it a disgrace, and we try to be some sort of never-existing generalised humans. We are stillborn, and for a long time now we have been born not of living fathers, and we like this more and more. We are acquiring a taste for it. Soon we shall invent a way of being born somehow from an idea. But enough; I don’t want to write any more “from the Underground”...

However, the “notes” of this paradoxalist do not end here. He could not resist, and went on. But it seems to us too that this is a good place to stop.
