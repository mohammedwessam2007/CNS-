## CHAPTER III

“Pyotr Petrovich!” she cried. “You at least protect me! Make this stupid creature understand that she dare not treat a gentlewoman in misfortune like this, that there is a law for it... I’ll go to the Governor-General himself... She will answer for it... Remembering my father’s hospitality, protect the orphans.”

“Allow me, madam... Allow me, allow me, madam,” Pyotr Petrovich waved her off. “Your papa, as you know, I never had the honour of knowing... allow me, madam!” (someone laughed loudly) “and I have no intention of taking part in your incessant quarrels with Amalia Ivanovna, madam... I have come on my own business... and I wish to have an explanation, immediately, with your stepdaughter, Sofya... Ivanovna... I believe that is right, madam? Allow me to pass, madam...”

And Pyotr Petrovich, edging round Katerina Ivanovna, made his way to the opposite corner, where Sonya was.

Katerina Ivanovna stayed where she was, as if thunderstruck. She could not understand how Pyotr Petrovich could disown her papa’s hospitality. Having once invented this hospitality, she now believed in it devoutly herself. She was struck too by Pyotr Petrovich’s businesslike, dry tone, full even of a kind of contemptuous threat. And everyone grew somehow quieter little by little at his appearance. Besides the fact that this “businesslike and serious” man was far too sharply out of harmony with the whole company, it was evident too that he had come about something important, that probably some extraordinary reason could have drawn him into such company, and that, therefore, something was about to happen, something was coming. Raskolnikov, who was standing beside Sonya, moved aside to let him pass; Pyotr Petrovich seemed not to notice him at all. A minute later Lebezyatnikov also appeared on the threshold; he did not come into the room, but stopped there too, with a kind of special curiosity, almost with surprise; he listened, but for a long time seemed unable to understand something.

“Excuse me if I am perhaps interrupting, but the matter is rather important, madam,” Pyotr Petrovich remarked to the room in general, not addressing anyone in particular. “I am even glad that there is an audience. Amalia Ivanovna, I most humbly request you, as landlady of the flat, to pay attention to the conversation I am about to have with Sofya Ivanovna. Sofya Ivanovna,” he went on, turning straight to Sonya, who was extremely surprised and already frightened in advance, “immediately after your visit, a hundred-rouble government banknote belonging to me disappeared from my table, in the room of my friend, Andrei Semyonovich Lebezyatnikov. If in any way whatever you know and will show us where it is now, then I assure you on my word of honour, and I call everyone to witness, that the matter will end there. Otherwise I shall be forced to resort to very serious measures, and then... you will have only yourself to blame, madam!”

Complete silence reigned in the room. Even the crying children fell quiet. Sonya stood deathly pale, looking at Luzhin, and could not answer anything. She seemed not yet to understand. Several seconds passed.

“Well, madam, what then, madam?” asked Luzhin, looking at her intently.

“I don’t know... I don’t know anything...” Sonya said at last in a weak voice.

“No? You don’t know?” Luzhin asked again, and was silent for a few more seconds. “Think, mademoiselle,” he began sternly, but still as if admonishing her, “consider; I am willing to give you more time to reflect. Kindly see, madam: if I were not so sure, then of course, with my experience, I would not have risked accusing you so directly; for a direct and public accusation of this kind, if false or even only mistaken, I am, in a certain sense, myself answerable. I know that, madam. This morning I changed, for my own needs, several five-per-cent bonds for a sum of, nominally, three thousand roubles. The account is written down in my pocketbook. On coming home, I (Andrei Semyonovich is witness to it) began counting the money, and having counted two thousand three hundred roubles, I put them in my pocketbook and the pocketbook in the side pocket of my frock coat. About five hundred roubles were left on the table, in banknotes, and among them three notes of a hundred roubles each. At that moment you arrived (at my invitation), and all the time you were with me afterwards you were in extreme confusion, so that three times, in the middle of the conversation, you even stood up and hurried for some reason to leave, although our conversation was not yet finished. Andrei Semyonovich can testify to all this. Probably you yourself, mademoiselle, will not refuse to confirm and declare that I invited you, through Andrei Semyonovich, solely in order to talk with you about the orphaned and helpless position of your relative, Katerina Ivanovna (whose memorial dinner I could not attend), and about how useful it would be to arrange something for her benefit, a kind of subscription, a lottery or the like. You thanked me and even shed tears (I am telling everything as it was, in the first place to remind you, and in the second to show you that not the slightest detail has been erased from my memory). Then I took a ten-rouble note from the table and gave it to you, in my own name, for the interests of your relative and by way of first relief. Andrei Semyonovich saw all this. Then I saw you to the door, all in the same confusion on your part, after which, remaining alone with Andrei Semyonovich and talking with him for about ten minutes, Andrei Semyonovich went out, and I turned again to the table with the money lying on it, intending to count it and put it aside separately, as I had planned before. To my surprise, one hundred-rouble note was missing among the rest. Kindly judge for yourselves: I cannot possibly suspect Andrei Semyonovich, madam; I am ashamed even of the supposition. Nor could I have made a mistake in counting, because a minute before your arrival, having finished all my accounts, I found the total correct. You will agree yourselves that, remembering your confusion, your haste to leave, and the fact that you kept your hands on the table for some time; taking into consideration, finally, your social position and the habits associated with it, I was, so to speak, with horror, and even against my will, forced to settle on a suspicion: a cruel one, of course, but a just one, madam! I will add, and repeat, that in spite of all my evident certainty, I understand that there is nonetheless some risk for me in my present accusation. But, as you see, I have not let it pass; I have risen up, and I will tell you why: solely, madam, solely on account of your blackest ingratitude! What? I invite you in the interests of your poorest relative, I give you my modest alms of ten roubles, and you, right there, right then, repay me for all this with such an act! No, madam, that is not good, madam! A lesson is needed, madam. Think it over; what’s more, as your true friend, I beg you (for you can have no better friend at this moment), come to your senses! Otherwise I shall be relentless! Well, madam, then?”

“I didn’t take anything from you,” Sonya whispered in horror. “You gave me ten roubles; here, take them.” Sonya took her handkerchief out of her pocket, found the knot, untied it, took out the ten-rouble note and held out her hand to Luzhin.

“And you still won’t admit to the other hundred roubles?” he said reproachfully and insistently, not accepting the note.

Sonya looked round. Everyone was looking at her with such terrible, stern, mocking, hateful faces. She glanced at Raskolnikov... he was standing by the wall, his arms crossed, looking at her with a fiery look.

“Oh Lord!” broke from Sonya.

“Amalia Ivanovna, it will be necessary to inform the police, and so I most humbly ask you to send for the caretaker for the time being,” Luzhin said quietly and even gently.

“Gott der Barmherzige! I knew it zat she vas stealing!” Amalia Ivanovna clasped her hands.

“You knew it?” Luzhin took it up. “Then you already had some grounds before for concluding so. I ask you, most worthy Amalia Ivanovna, to remember your words, uttered, moreover, before witnesses.”

A loud hubbub suddenly arose on all sides. Everyone began to stir.

“Wha-a-at!” Katerina Ivanovna cried out suddenly, coming to herself, and, as if breaking loose, rushed at Luzhin. “What! You accuse her of theft? Sonya? Ah, scoundrels, scoundrels!” And rushing to Sonya, she embraced her with her withered arms as in a vice.

“Sonya! How dared you take ten roubles from him! Oh, you silly girl! Give them here! Give me those ten roubles at once, there!”

And snatching the note from Sonya, Katerina Ivanovna crumpled it in her hands and flung it back-handed straight into Luzhin’s face. The little ball hit him in the eye and bounced onto the floor. Amalia Ivanovna rushed to pick up the money. Pyotr Petrovich lost his temper.

“Restrain this madwoman!” he shouted.

At that moment several more faces appeared in the doorway beside Lebezyatnikov, among whom both of the visiting ladies were peeping in.

“What! Madwoman? I am a madwoman? You fo-ol!” shrieked Katerina Ivanovna. “You are a fool yourself, you pettifogger, you low man! Sonya, Sonya take money from him! Sonya a thief! Why, she would give you money herself, you fool!” And Katerina Ivanovna laughed hysterically. “Have you ever seen such a fool?” she rushed in all directions, pointing Luzhin out to everyone. “What! And you too?” she caught sight of the landlady. “And you join in too, you sausage-seller, confirming that she ‘vas stealing’, you vile Prussian chicken leg in a crinoline! Oh, you! Oh, you! Why, she didn’t even leave the room, and when she came back from you, you scoundrel, she sat down right here beside Rodion Romanovich!.. Search her! If she didn’t go anywhere, then the money must be on her! Search, then, search, search! Only if you don’t find it, then excuse me, my dear, you will answer for it! I’ll run to the sovereign, to the sovereign, to the merciful tsar himself, I’ll throw myself at his feet, at once, this very day! I am an orphan! They will let me in! You think they won’t? You’re lying, I’ll get there! I’ll get the-ere! Were you counting on her being meek? Was that what you relied on? But I, brother, am no meek one! You’ll come to grief! Search, then! Search, search, go on, search!!”

And Katerina Ivanovna, in a frenzy, was tugging at Luzhin, dragging him towards Sonya.

“I am ready, madam, and I answer for it... but calm yourself, madam, calm yourself! I see all too well that you are no meek one!.. This... this... how is this to be done, madam?” muttered Luzhin. “This ought to be done in the presence of the police, madam... though, however, even now there are more than enough witnesses... I am ready, madam... But in any case it is awkward for a man... on account of sex... If with the help of Amalia Ivanovna... though, however, things are not done like this... How is this to be done, madam?”

“Whoever you like! Let whoever wants to search her!” shouted Katerina Ivanovna. “Sonya, turn out your pockets for them! There, there! Look, you monster, here, it’s empty, my handkerchief was here, the pocket is empty, you see! Here is the other pocket, here, here! You see! You see!”

And Katerina Ivanovna did not so much turn out as pull out both pockets, one after the other. But from the second, right-hand pocket a piece of paper suddenly jumped out and, describing a parabola in the air, fell at Luzhin’s feet. Everyone saw it; many cried out. Pyotr Petrovich bent down, picked up the paper from the floor with two fingers, held it up for everyone to see and unfolded it. It was a hundred-rouble banknote, folded in eight. Pyotr Petrovich moved his hand round in a circle, showing everyone the note.

“Thief! Out of ze rooms! Police, police!” howled Amalia Ivanovna. “Zey must be chased to Siberia! Out!”

Exclamations flew from all sides. Raskolnikov was silent, not taking his eyes off Sonya, now and then, but quickly, moving them to Luzhin. Sonya stood on the same spot as if unconscious: she was hardly even surprised. Suddenly colour flooded her whole face; she cried out and hid her face in her hands.

“No, it wasn’t me! I didn’t take it! I don’t know!” she cried with a heartrending wail, and rushed to Katerina Ivanovna. Katerina Ivanovna seized her and pressed her tightly to herself, as if wishing to shield her from everyone with her breast.

“Sonya! Sonya! I don’t believe it! You see, I don’t believe it!” cried Katerina Ivanovna (in spite of all the evidence), shaking her in her arms like a child, kissing her countless times, catching her hands and, simply fastening on them, kissing them. “That you would take it! What stupid people these are! Oh Lord! You are stupid, stupid,” she cried, turning to everyone. “Why, you don’t know yet, you don’t know what a heart this is, what a girl this is! She take it, she! Why, she would take off her last dress, sell it, go barefoot, and give it to you if you needed it, that is what she is like! She even got the yellow ticket because my children were dying of hunger; she sold herself for us!.. Ah, my late husband, my late husband! Ah, my late husband! Do you see? Do you see? Here is your memorial dinner! Lord! But protect her, why are you all standing there! Rodion Romanovich! Why don’t you stand up for her? Do you believe it too? You are not worth her little finger, all of you, all, all, all! Lord! Protect her, at last!”

The weeping of the poor, consumptive, orphaned Katerina Ivanovna seemed to produce a strong effect on the public. There was so much that was pitiful, so much suffering, in this withered consumptive face distorted with pain, in these parched lips caked with blood, in this hoarsely shouting voice, in this sobbing like a child’s crying, in this trusting, childlike and at the same time desperate plea to protect her, that, it seemed, everyone pitied the unhappy woman. At any rate Pyotr Petrovich at once pitied her.

“Madam! Madam!” he exclaimed in an impressive voice. “This fact does not concern you! No one would venture to accuse you of intent or complicity, the more so since it was you who discovered it, by turning out the pocket: so you suspected nothing. I am very, very ready to feel sorry if, so to speak, poverty drove Sofya Semyonovna to it too; but why, mademoiselle, did you not want to confess? Were you afraid of the shame? The first step? Perhaps you lost your head? It is understandable, madam; very understandable, madam... But all the same, why go in for such things! Gentlemen!” he turned to all present. “Gentlemen! Feeling sorry and, so to speak, sympathising, I am perhaps prepared to forgive, even now, in spite of the personal insults I have received. Let this present shame, mademoiselle, serve as a lesson to you for the future,” he turned to Sonya, “and I will let the rest go and, so be it, I drop the matter. Enough!”

Pyotr Petrovich gave a sidelong look at Raskolnikov. Their eyes met. Raskolnikov’s burning look was ready to reduce him to ashes. Meanwhile Katerina Ivanovna seemed not to hear anything more: she was embracing and kissing Sonya like a madwoman. The children too had clasped Sonya from all sides with their little arms, and Polechka, who did not quite understand, however, what the matter was, seemed to have drowned completely in tears, breaking down in sobs and hiding her pretty little face, swollen with crying, on Sonya’s shoulder.

“How base!” a loud voice rang out suddenly in the doorway.

Pyotr Petrovich looked round quickly.

“What baseness!” repeated Lebezyatnikov, looking him intently in the eyes.

Pyotr Petrovich even seemed to start. Everyone noticed it. (They remembered it afterwards.) Lebezyatnikov stepped into the room.

“And you dared to put me down as a witness?” he said, going up to Pyotr Petrovich.

“What does this mean, Andrei Semyonovich? What are you talking about?” muttered Luzhin.

“It means that you are... a slanderer; that is what my words mean!” Lebezyatnikov said hotly, looking at him sternly with his short-sighted little eyes. He was terribly angry. Raskolnikov fastened his eyes on him, as if catching up and weighing every word. Again silence reigned. Pyotr Petrovich was almost at a loss, especially in the first moment.

“If you are saying this to me...” he began, stammering. “But what is the matter with you? Are you in your right mind?”

“I am in my right mind, sir, but you are... a swindler! Ah, how base this is! I have been listening to everything; I waited on purpose so as to understand everything, because, I confess, even now it is not quite logical... But why you did all this, I don’t understand.”

“But what have I done! Will you stop talking in your absurd riddles! Or perhaps you have been drinking?”

“It is you, you low man, who may drink, not I! I never drink vodka at all, because it is not in my convictions! Imagine, he, he himself, with his own hands, gave this hundred-rouble note to Sofya Semyonovna; I saw it, I am a witness, I will take an oath! He did, he did!” repeated Lebezyatnikov, turning to each and everyone.

“Have you gone mad or not, you milksop?” shrieked Luzhin. “She is here herself before you, in person; she herself, here, just now, in front of everyone, confirmed that apart from ten roubles she received nothing from me. How could I have given it to her, after that?”

“I saw it, I saw it!” Lebezyatnikov shouted and insisted. “And though it is against my convictions, I am ready this very hour to take any oath you like in court, because I saw how you slipped it to her quietly! Only I, fool that I am, thought you slipped it to her out of charity! In the doorway, saying goodbye to her, when she turned round and you were pressing her hand with one hand, with the other, the left, you put the paper quietly into her pocket. I saw it! I saw it!”

Luzhin turned pale.

“What lies you are telling!” he cried insolently. “And how could you, standing by the window, make out the paper? You imagined it... with your short-sighted eyes. You are raving!”

“No, I did not imagine it! And though I was standing far off, I saw everything, everything, and though from the window it is really hard to make out a paper (you are right about that), I knew for certain, owing to a special circumstance, that it was precisely a hundred-rouble note, because when you began giving Sofya Semyonovna the ten-rouble note (I saw it myself), you took a hundred-rouble note from the table at the same time (I saw that, because I was standing close by then, and because a certain thought came to me at once, I did not forget that you had a note in your hand). You folded it and kept it squeezed in your hand all the time. Then I forgot again, but when you began to get up, you moved it from your right hand to your left and nearly dropped it; then I remembered again, because the same thought came to me again, namely that you wanted to do her a kindness without my knowing. You can imagine how I began to watch, and so I saw how you managed to slip it into her pocket. I saw it, I saw it, I will take an oath!”

Lebezyatnikov was almost choking. Various exclamations began to ring out from all sides, mostly expressing surprise; but there were also exclamations that took on a threatening tone. Everyone pressed in around Pyotr Petrovich. Katerina Ivanovna rushed to Lebezyatnikov.

“Andrei Semyonovich! I was wrong about you! Protect her! You alone are for her! She is an orphan; God has sent you! Andrei Semyonovich, my dear, my good man!”

And Katerina Ivanovna, hardly knowing what she was doing, threw herself on her knees before him.

“Rubbish!” howled Luzhin, enraged to fury. “It’s rubbish you keep grinding out, sir. ‘I forgot, I remembered, I forgot’: what is that! So I planted it on her on purpose? Why? For what purpose? What have I in common with this...”

“Why? That is just what I don’t understand myself, but that I am telling a true fact, that is certain! I am so far from mistaken, you vile, criminal man, that I remember precisely how a question came into my head at once about it, just at the moment when I was thanking you and shaking your hand. Why exactly did you put it into her pocket stealthily? That is, why exactly stealthily? Was it only because you wanted to hide it from me, knowing that I hold opposite convictions and reject private charity, which cures nothing radically? Well, and I decided that you really were ashamed before me to hand out such sums, and besides, perhaps, I thought, he wants to give her a surprise, to astonish her when she finds a whole hundred roubles in her pocket. (Because some philanthropists are very fond of spreading out their good deeds like that; I know.) Then it also occurred to me that you wanted to test her, that is, whether she would come to thank you on finding it. Then, that you wanted to avoid gratitude and so that, well, how does the saying go: so that the right hand, or something, should not know... in a word, something like that... Well, all sorts of thoughts came into my head then, so that I resolved to think it all over later, but all the same I considered it indelicate to reveal to you that I knew the secret. But then, however, another question came into my head at once: that Sofya Semyonovna, before she noticed, might, for all one knows, lose the money; that is why I decided to come here, call her out and let her know that a hundred roubles had been put in her pocket. But on the way I first called at the room of the Kobylyatnikov ladies, to take them The General Conclusion of the Positive Method and especially to recommend Piderit’s article (and also Wagner’s, by the way); then I come here, and look what a story is going on! Well, could I, could I have had all these thoughts and reasonings if I had not really seen that you put a hundred roubles into her pocket?”

When Andrei Semyonovich had finished his long-winded reasoning, with such a logical deduction at the end of his speech, he was terribly tired, and sweat was even running down his face. Alas, he could not even express himself properly in Russian (though he knew no other language), so that he was all somehow exhausted at once, and even seemed to have grown thinner after his lawyer’s feat. Nevertheless his speech produced an extraordinary effect. He spoke with such passion, with such conviction, that evidently everyone believed him. Pyotr Petrovich felt that things were going badly.

“What business is it of mine that some stupid questions came into your head?” he cried. “That is no proof, sir! You could have dreamed it all in your sleep, that’s all, sir! And I tell you that you are lying, sir! Lying and slandering out of some spite against me, and precisely out of pique because I would not agree to your free-thinking and godless social proposals, that’s what it is, sir!”

But this dodge did Pyotr Petrovich no good. On the contrary, a murmur arose on all sides.

“Ah, so that’s where you’re heading!” shouted Lebezyatnikov. “You’re lying! Call the police, and I will take an oath! Only one thing I can’t understand: why he risked such a base act! Oh, pitiful, vile man!”

“I can explain why he risked such an act, and, if necessary, I will take an oath myself!” Raskolnikov said at last in a firm voice, and stepped forward.

He was apparently firm and calm. It somehow became clear to everyone, at a single look at him, that he really knew what the matter was, and that things had come to the denouement.

“Now I have made everything completely clear to myself,” Raskolnikov went on, turning straight to Lebezyatnikov. “From the very beginning of this business I had begun to suspect that there was some vile trick here; I began to suspect it because of certain special circumstances, known only to me, which I will now explain to everyone: the whole matter lies in them! And you, Andrei Semyonovich, with your precious testimony, have finally made everything clear to me. I ask everyone, everyone, to listen: this gentleman” (he pointed to Luzhin) “recently sought the hand of a certain girl, and precisely of my sister, Avdotya Romanovna Raskolnikova. But on arriving in Petersburg, two days ago, at our first meeting, he quarrelled with me, and I drove him out of my room, of which there are two witnesses. This man is very spiteful... Two days ago I did not yet know that he was lodging here, in your rooms, Andrei Semyonovich, and that, therefore, on the very day we quarrelled, that is, two days ago, he witnessed how I gave, as a friend of the late Mr Marmeladov, some money to his wife Katerina Ivanovna for the funeral. He at once wrote a note to my mother and informed her that I had given all the money not to Katerina Ivanovna but to Sofya Semyonovna, and in doing so he referred in the vilest terms to... to Sofya Semyonovna’s character, that is, he hinted at the character of my relations with Sofya Semyonovna. All this, as you understand, with the aim of setting me at odds with my mother and sister, by suggesting to them that I am squandering, for ignoble purposes, their last money, with which they help me. Last night, before my mother and sister, and in his presence, I re-established the truth, proving that I had given the money to Katerina Ivanovna for the funeral, and not to Sofya Semyonovna, and that two days ago I was not even acquainted with Sofya Semyonovna and had not even seen her face. At the same time I added that he, Pyotr Petrovich Luzhin, with all his merits, was not worth Sofya Semyonovna’s little finger, of whom he speaks so ill. And to his question whether I would seat Sofya Semyonovna beside my sister, I answered that I had already done so, that same day. Infuriated that my mother and sister would not quarrel with me on the strength of his slanders, he began, word by word, to say unforgivably insolent things to them. A final break took place, and he was turned out of the house. All this happened last night. Now I ask for special attention: imagine that if he had now succeeded in proving that Sofya Semyonovna was a thief, then, in the first place, he would have proved to my sister and mother that he had been almost right in his suspicions; that he had justly been angry at my putting my sister and Sofya Semyonovna on the same level; that, in attacking me, he had therefore been defending and safeguarding the honour of my sister, his bride. In a word, by all this he could even have set me at odds with my family again, and of course he hoped to get back into their good graces. Not to mention that he was taking revenge on me personally, because he has grounds for supposing that the honour and happiness of Sofya Semyonovna are very dear to me. That is his whole calculation! That is how I understand this business! That is the whole reason, and there can be no other!”

So, or almost so, Raskolnikov ended his speech, which was often interrupted by exclamations from the audience, who nevertheless listened very attentively. But in spite of all the interruptions, he spoke sharply, calmly, precisely, clearly, firmly. His sharp voice, his tone of conviction and his stern face produced an extraordinary effect on everyone.

“Yes, yes, that’s it!” Lebezyatnikov confirmed in rapture. “It must be so, because he asked me precisely, as soon as Sofya Semyonovna came into our room, ‘are you here? Had I seen you among Katerina Ivanovna’s guests?’ He called me over to the window for that and asked me there quietly. So he absolutely needed you to be here! That’s it, that’s all it is!”

Luzhin was silent and smiled contemptuously. He was very pale, however. He seemed to be considering how to wriggle out of it. Perhaps he would gladly have dropped everything and left, but at the present moment that was almost impossible; it would have meant openly admitting the justice of the accusations brought against him and that he really had slandered Sofya Semyonovna. Besides, the audience, already tipsy enough as it was, was too agitated. The commissariat clerk, though he did not understand everything, shouted loudest of all and proposed certain measures very unpleasant for Luzhin. But there were sober people too; people had come and gathered from all the rooms. All three little Poles were terribly heated and kept shouting at him: “Pan lajdak! Mister scoundrel!”, and muttering some threats in Polish besides. Sonya listened intently, but she too seemed not to understand everything, as if she were waking from a faint. She only did not take her eyes off Raskolnikov, feeling that all her protection lay in him. Katerina Ivanovna was breathing with difficulty and hoarsely, and seemed to be in terrible exhaustion. Most foolish of all stood Amalia Ivanovna, with her mouth open, understanding absolutely nothing. She only saw that Pyotr Petrovich had somehow been caught. Raskolnikov asked to speak again, but they no longer let him finish: everyone was shouting and pressing around Luzhin with curses and threats. But Pyotr Petrovich did not lose his nerve. Seeing that the case of the accusation against Sonya was completely lost, he resorted straight to insolence.

“Allow me, gentlemen, allow me; don’t crowd, let me pass!” he said, making his way through the crowd. “And do me the favour of not threatening; I assure you that nothing will come of it, you will do nothing; I am not one of the timid sort, sir; on the contrary, it is you, gentlemen, who will answer for having covered up a criminal case by violence. The thief has been more than exposed, and I shall prosecute, sir. In court they are not so blind and... not so drunk, sir, and they will not believe two notorious atheists, agitators and free-thinkers, who accuse me out of personal revenge, which they themselves, in their stupidity, admit... Yes, sir, allow me, sir!”

“See that not a whiff of you is left in my room at once; kindly move out, and everything between us is over! And when I think that I was jumping out of my skin, explaining things to him... for two whole weeks!..”

“But I told you myself earlier, Andrei Semyonovich, that I was moving out, when you were still trying to keep me; now I will only add that you are a fool, sir. I wish you a cure for your mind and your short-sighted eyes. Allow me, gentlemen, sir!”

He pushed his way through; but the commissariat clerk did not want to let him go so easily, with mere curses: he seized a glass from the table, swung his arm and hurled it at Pyotr Petrovich; but the glass flew straight at Amalia Ivanovna. She shrieked, and the commissariat clerk, losing his balance from the swing, fell heavily under the table. Pyotr Petrovich went to his own room, and within half an hour he was no longer in the house. Sonya, timid by nature, had known before that it was easier to ruin her than anyone else, and that anyone could offend her almost with impunity. But all the same, up to this very moment, it had seemed to her that disaster could somehow be avoided: by caution, by meekness, by submissiveness before each and everyone. Her disillusionment was too heavy. She could, of course, bear everything with patience and almost without complaint, even this. But in the first moment it was too heavy. In spite of her triumph and her vindication, when the first fright and the first stupor had passed, when she understood and grasped everything clearly, a feeling of helplessness and injury wrung her heart tormentingly. She became hysterical. At last, unable to bear it, she rushed out of the room and ran home. This was almost immediately after Luzhin’s departure. Amalia Ivanovna, when the glass hit her amid the loud laughter of those present, also could not bear paying for someone else’s feast. With a shriek, like a madwoman, she rushed at Katerina Ivanovna, considering her to blame for everything:

“Out of ze rooms! At vonce! March!” And with these words she began seizing everything of Katerina Ivanovna’s that came to hand and throwing it on the floor. Almost crushed as it was, nearly fainting, gasping, pale, Katerina Ivanovna sprang up from the bed (on which she had fallen in exhaustion) and rushed at Amalia Ivanovna. But the struggle was too unequal; the landlady pushed her away like a feather.

“What! Not enough that they have slandered us so godlessly, this creature is at me too! What! On the day of my husband’s funeral I am turned out of my lodging, after my hospitality, into the street, with orphans! But where am I to go!” the poor woman wailed, sobbing and gasping. “Lord!” she cried suddenly, her eyes flashing, “can it be that there is no justice! Whom are you to protect, if not us, orphans? But we shall see! There is judgement and truth in the world, there is, I shall find it! At once, wait, you godless creature! Polechka, stay with the children, I’ll come back. Wait for me, even in the street! We shall see whether there is truth in the world!”

And throwing over her head that same green drap-de-dames shawl which the late Marmeladov had mentioned in his story, Katerina Ivanovna pushed her way through the disorderly drunken crowd of lodgers, still crowding the room, and ran out into the street wailing and in tears, with the vague purpose of finding justice somewhere, now, at once, and at whatever cost. Polechka, in fright, huddled with the children in a corner on the trunk, where, embracing the two little ones and trembling all over, she began to wait for her mother to come back. Amalia Ivanovna rushed about the room, shrieking, lamenting, flinging everything that came her way on the floor, and raging. The lodgers bawled at random, every one in his own key: some were finishing saying what they could about the incident; others were quarrelling and swearing; some struck up songs...

“And now it is time for me!” thought Raskolnikov. “Well, Sofya Semyonovna, let us see what you will say now!”

And he set off for Sonya’s lodging.
