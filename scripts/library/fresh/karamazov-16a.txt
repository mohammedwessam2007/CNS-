## Book Six, Chapter III · From the Talks and Teachings of Elder Zosima · (e) Something of the Russian Monk and His Possible Significance

Fathers and teachers, what is a monk? In the enlightened world this word is spoken nowadays by some with mockery, and by some even as a term of abuse. And the further it goes, the more so. True, oh true, there are many parasites in the monastic life too, gluttons, voluptuaries and insolent vagabonds. Educated people of the world point to this: “You are idlers and useless members of society,” they say; “you live on other people’s labour; you are shameless beggars.” And yet how many in the monastic life are humble and meek, thirsting for solitude and for fervent prayer in silence. These are pointed to less, and even passed over in silence altogether; and how surprised people would be if I said that from these meek ones, who thirst for solitary prayer, the salvation of the Russian land will come forth perhaps once more! For truly they are made ready in silence “for the day and the hour, the month and the year”. Meanwhile, in their solitude, they keep the image of Christ beautiful and undistorted, in the purity of God’s truth, from the most ancient fathers, apostles and martyrs, and when the time comes they will reveal it to the shaken truth of the world. That is a great thought. This star will shine forth from the East.

That is what I think of the monk; and is it false, is it arrogant? Look at the worldly, and at the whole world that exalts itself above the people of God: has not the image of God and his truth been distorted in it? They have science; but in science there is only what is subject to the senses. The spiritual world, the higher half of man’s being, is rejected altogether, driven out with a kind of triumph, even with hatred. The world has proclaimed freedom, especially of late, and what do we see in this freedom of theirs? Nothing but slavery and suicide! For the world says: “You have needs, so satisfy them, for you have the same rights as the most distinguished and the richest. Do not be afraid to satisfy them; multiply them even.” That is the world’s teaching today. In that they see freedom. And what comes of this right to multiply needs? Among the rich, isolation and spiritual suicide; among the poor, envy and murder; for they have been given the rights, but not yet shown the means of satisfying the needs. They assure us that the more the world goes on, the more it is united, that it is forming itself into a brotherly communion, because it shortens distances and sends thoughts through the air. Alas, do not believe in such a union of people. Taking freedom to mean the multiplying and the speedy satisfaction of needs, they distort their own nature, for they breed in themselves many senseless and foolish desires, habits and the most absurd inventions. They live only for envy of one another, for the pleasures of the flesh and for vanity. To have dinners, carriages, horses, rank and slavish servants is now thought such a necessity that people sacrifice even life, honour and love of mankind to satisfy it, and even kill themselves if they cannot satisfy it. Among those who are not rich we see the same; and among the poor the unsatisfied needs and the envy are drowned for the present in drink. But soon, instead of wine, they will drink their fill of blood; that is where they are being led. I ask you: is such a man free? I knew one “fighter for an idea” who told me himself that when they deprived him of tobacco in prison he was so tormented by the deprivation that he very nearly went and betrayed his “idea” just to be given some tobacco. And this sort of man says: “I am going to fight for mankind.” Well, how far will such a man go, and what is he capable of? A quick deed, perhaps, but he will not endure long. And no wonder that instead of freedom they have fallen into slavery, and instead of serving brotherly love and human unity they have fallen, on the contrary, into separation and isolation, as my mysterious visitor and teacher told me in my youth. And so in the world the idea of serving mankind, of the brotherhood and wholeness of people, is dying out more and more, and indeed this idea is now met even with mockery; for how is a man to give up his habits, where is this slave to go, if he is so used to satisfying the countless needs he has invented for himself? He is in isolation, and what is the whole to him? And they have reached the point where they have piled up more things, but there is less joy.

The monastic way is a different matter. People even laugh at obedience, fasting and prayer, and yet in them alone lies the way to real, true freedom: I cut away from myself superfluous and unnecessary needs, I humble my proud and self-loving will and scourge it with obedience, and by this, with God’s help, I attain freedom of spirit, and with it spiritual gladness! Which of them is more capable of raising up a great idea and going to serve it: the isolated rich man, or this man freed from the tyranny of things and habits? The monk is reproached for his solitude: “You have shut yourself away to save yourself within the monastery walls, and you have forgotten brotherly service to mankind.” But we shall see who will be more zealous in brotherly love. For the isolation is not with us but with them, only they do not see it. From us, from of old, came men who acted for the people; why should there not be such men now? The same humble and meek men of fasting and silence will rise up and go to the great work. The salvation of Russia is from the people. And the Russian monastery has from of old been with the people. If the people are in isolation, then we too are in isolation. The people believe as we do, and an unbelieving man of action will do nothing in Russia, even if he is sincere at heart and a genius in mind. Remember this. The people will meet the atheist and overcome him, and Russia will become one and Orthodox. Take care of the people, then, and guard their heart. Bring them up in quietness. That is your monastic labour, for this people is a God-bearer.

## (f) Something of Masters and Servants, and Whether It Is Possible for Masters and Servants to Become Brothers in Spirit

God knows there is sin among the people too. And the flame of corruption is spreading visibly, hourly; it comes from above. Isolation is coming upon the people too: kulaks and bloodsuckers of the commune are appearing; already the merchant wants more and more honours, strives to show himself educated without having the least education, and to this end basely scorns ancient custom and is even ashamed of the faith of his fathers. He visits princes, though he is only a spoilt peasant himself. The people are festering with drink and cannot give it up. And how much cruelty there is to their families, to their wives, even to their children; all from drink. I have seen in factories children even ten years old: sickly, stunted, bent and already depraved. A stifling room, a clattering machine, work all God’s day, depraved words and drink, drink; and is that what the soul of so small a child needs? He needs sunshine, children’s games, a bright example everywhere, and at least a drop of love for him. Let there be no more of this, monks; let there be no torturing of children; rise up and preach it quickly, quickly. But God will save Russia, for though the simple man is corrupt and can no longer deny himself his foul sin, yet he knows that his foul sin is cursed by God and that he does wrong in sinning. So our people still believe unwearyingly in righteousness; they acknowledge God; they weep with tender feeling. It is not so with the upper classes. They, following science, want to arrange things justly by their reason alone, but without Christ now, unlike before, and they have already proclaimed that there is no crime, no sin. And that is right, by their way of thinking: for if you have no God, what crime can there be? In Europe the people are already rising against the rich by force, and the people’s leaders everywhere are leading them to blood and teaching them that their wrath is just. But “cursed be their anger, for it was fierce.” And the Lord will save Russia, as he has saved her many times before. Salvation will come from the people, from their faith and their humility. Fathers and teachers, guard the people’s faith; and this is no dream. All my life I have been struck by the true and seemly dignity of our great people; I have seen it myself; I can bear witness myself; I have seen it and wondered at it; I have seen it in spite of the stench of sins and the beggarly look of our people. They are not servile, and this after two centuries of slavery. They are free in look and bearing, but without any insolence. And they are not vindictive and not envious. “You are noble, you are rich, you are clever and gifted; very well, God bless you. I honour you, but I know that I too am a man. By honouring you without envy I show you my human dignity.” Truly, if they do not say this (for they do not yet know how to say it), that is how they act; I have seen it myself, I have experienced it myself; and would you believe it, the poorer and lower our Russian man is, the more this seemly truth is to be seen in him; for the rich among them, the kulaks and bloodsuckers, are already corrupted in great numbers, and much, much of that has come from our own negligence and lack of care! But God will save his people, for Russia is great in her humility. I dream of seeing our future, and I seem to see it clearly already: for it will come to pass that even the most corrupt of our rich men will end by being ashamed of his riches before the poor, and the poor man, seeing this humility, will understand and give way to him with joy, and will answer his seemly shame with kindness. Believe that it will end so: things are going that way. Equality is only in man’s spiritual dignity, and this will be understood only among us. If there were brothers, there would be brotherhood; but before there is brotherhood they will never share things out. We keep the image of Christ, and it will shine forth like a precious diamond to the whole world… So be it, so be it!

Fathers and teachers, a touching thing happened to me once. In my wanderings I met, in the provincial town of K., my former orderly Afanasy; eight years had passed since I had parted from him. He caught sight of me by chance in the market, recognised me, ran up to me, and, God, how glad he was! He simply flew at me: “Father, master, is it you? Can I really be seeing you?” He took me home with him. He had left the service by then, married, and already had two baby children. He and his wife made their living by petty trade in the market, from a stall. His little room was poor but clean and joyful. He sat me down, put on the samovar, sent for his wife, as if I had made a holiday for him by turning up there. He brought the children to me: “Bless them, Father.” “Is it for me to bless them?” I answer him. “I am a simple, humble monk. I will pray to God for them; and for you, Afanasy Pavlovich, I pray to God always, every day, from that very day, for everything came from you,” I tell him. And I explained it to him as best I could. And what of the man? He looks at me and still cannot picture to himself that I, his former master, an officer, am now before him in such a guise and such clothes: he even began to cry. “Why are you crying?” I say to him. “You unforgettable man, rather rejoice in your soul for me, my dear, for my path is joyful and bright.” He did not say much, but kept sighing and shaking his head at me tenderly. “Where is your fortune, then?” he asks. I answer him: “I gave it to the monastery, and we live in common.” After tea I began to take leave of them, and suddenly he brought me out a half-rouble, an offering for the monastery; and another half-rouble, I see, he is hurriedly pushing into my hand: “That is for you,” he says, “a wanderer, a traveller; it may be of use to you, Father.” I accepted his half-rouble, bowed to him and to his wife, and went away gladdened, and thought on the way: “Here we both are now, he at home and I on the road, sighing, no doubt, and smiling joyfully in the gladness of our hearts, nodding our heads and remembering how God brought us to meet.” And I never saw him again after that. I was his master and he my servant; and now, when we had kissed each other lovingly and in spiritual tenderness, a great human union had come about between us. I have thought about this a great deal, and now I think this: is it so inconceivable that this great and simple-hearted union might in its time come about everywhere among our Russian people? I believe that it will, and that the time is near.

And about servants I will add the following. As a youth I used to be very angry with servants: “The cook served it too hot; my orderly didn’t brush my clothes.” But then the thought of my dear brother, which I had heard from him in my childhood, suddenly lit me up: “Am I worth it, am I worth it at all, that another should serve me, and that I should order him about because of his poverty and ignorance?” And I wondered then how late the simplest thoughts, plain to the eye, appear in our minds. It is impossible to be without servants in the world, but act so that your servant is freer in spirit than if he were not a servant. And why can I not be a servant to my servant, and in such a way that he even sees it, and without any pride on my part or any disbelief on his? Why should my servant not be like my own kin, so that I take him at last into my family and rejoice in it? Even now this can be done, and it will serve as the foundation for the future, already magnificent, union of men, when a man will not seek servants for himself and will not want to turn people like himself into servants, as now, but, on the contrary, will want with all his strength to become himself the servant of all, according to the Gospel. And is it a dream, that in the end man will find his joys only in deeds of enlightenment and mercy, and not in cruel joys, as now, in gluttony, fornication, vanity, boasting and the envious lording of one over another? I believe firmly that it is not, and that the time is near. They laugh and ask: when will this time come, and does it look as if it will come? But I think that with Christ we shall bring about this great thing. And how many ideas have there been on earth, in human history, which were unthinkable even ten years before and which suddenly appeared when their mysterious time had come, and swept over the whole earth? So it will be with us, and our people will shine forth to the world, and all people will say: “The stone which the builders rejected is become the head of the corner.” And the mockers themselves should be asked: if ours is a dream, then when will you put up your building and arrange things justly by your reason alone, without Christ? If they themselves declare that they, on the contrary, are moving towards union, truly only the most simple-hearted of them believe it, so that one may even wonder at such simple-heartedness. Truly they have more of dreamy fancy than we. They mean to arrange things justly, but, having rejected Christ, they will end by drenching the world in blood, for blood calls for blood, and he who draws the sword shall perish by the sword. And were it not for Christ’s promise, they would destroy one another even to the last two men on earth. And even those last two would not manage, in their pride, to restrain each other, so that the last would destroy the last but one and then himself. And it would come to pass, were it not for Christ’s promise that for the sake of the meek and humble this thing shall be cut short. In those days, still in my officer’s uniform, after my duel, I began to speak about servants in society, and everyone, I remember, wondered at me: “What,” they said, “are we to sit our servant on the sofa and bring him tea?” And I answered them then: “Why not, if only sometimes?” Everyone laughed then. Their question was frivolous, and my answer unclear; but I think there was some truth in it too.

## (g) Of Prayer, of Love, and of Contact with Other Worlds

Young man, do not forget prayer. Every time you pray, if your prayer is sincere, a new feeling will flash through it, and with it a new thought you did not know before, which will give you fresh courage; and you will understand that prayer is an education. Remember this too: every day, and whenever you can, repeat to yourself: “Lord, have mercy on all who have stood before thee this day.” For at every hour and every moment thousands of people leave their life on this earth, and their souls stand before the Lord; and how many of them have parted from the earth in isolation, unknown to anyone, in sadness and anguish that no one will grieve for them or even know of them at all, whether they lived or not. And there, perhaps from the other end of the earth, your prayer for his rest will rise up to the Lord, though you did not know him at all, nor he you. How moving it is for his soul, standing in fear before the Lord, to feel at that moment that there is someone praying for him too, that there is still a human being left on earth who loves him. And God will look more mercifully on you both, for if you have pitied him so much, how much more will he pity him, he who is infinitely more merciful and loving than you. And he will forgive him for your sake.

Brothers, do not be afraid of men’s sin; love man even in his sin, for that is already a likeness of divine love and is the height of love on earth. Love all God’s creation, the whole of it and every grain of sand. Love every leaf, every ray of God’s light. Love the animals, love the plants, love everything. If you love everything, you will perceive the mystery of God in things. Once you perceive it, you will begin to understand it more and more every day, unwearyingly. And you will come at last to love the whole world with an all-embracing, universal love. Love the animals: God has given them the beginnings of thought and an untroubled joy. Do not trouble it, do not torment them, do not take away their joy, do not go against God’s thought. Man, do not exalt yourself above the animals: they are sinless, and you, with your greatness, defile the earth by appearing on it and leave the trail of your defilement behind you; alas, almost every one of us! Love children especially, for they too are sinless, like the angels, and live to soften our hearts, to purify our hearts, and as a kind of guidance for us. Woe to him who offends a child. Father Anfim taught me to love children: dear, silent man that he was on our wanderings, he would buy them little gingerbreads and sweets with the coppers we were given, and hand them out; he could not pass children without his soul being stirred; such was the man.

Before some thought you will stand bewildered, especially at the sight of men’s sin, and you will ask yourself: “Shall I take it by force, or by humble love?” Always decide: “I will take it by humble love.” If you decide so once for all, you may be able to conquer the whole world. Loving humility is a terrible force, the strongest of all, and there is nothing like it. Every day and hour, every minute, walk round yourself and watch yourself, so that your image may be seemly. You pass by a little child; you pass by spiteful, with a foul word, with an angry soul; perhaps you did not even notice the child, but he saw you, and your image, unlovely and ungodly, may remain in his defenceless little heart. You did not know it, but perhaps by that you have already sown a bad seed in him, and it may grow, and all because you did not take care before the child, because you did not nurture in yourself a careful, active love. Brothers, love is a teacher, but one must know how to acquire it, for it is acquired with difficulty, it is bought dearly, by long work and over a long time; for one must love not only for a chance moment but for all time. Anyone can love by chance; even a villain can love. My young brother asked the birds to forgive him: it seems senseless, but it is the truth, for everything is like an ocean; everything flows and touches; touch it in one place and it echoes at the other end of the world. Let it be madness to ask the birds’ forgiveness; still, it would be easier for the birds, and for the child, and for every animal around you, if you yourself were more seemly than you are now, if only by one drop. Everything is like an ocean, I tell you. Then you would pray to the birds too, tormented by an all-embracing love, as if in some rapture, and pray that they too should forgive you your sin. Treasure this rapture, however senseless it may seem to people.

My friends, ask God for gladness. Be glad as children, as the birds of heaven. And let not men’s sin trouble you in your work; do not be afraid that it will wear your work away and not let it be done. Do not say: “Sin is strong, wickedness is strong, the foul surroundings are strong, and we are alone and powerless; the foul surroundings will wear us down and not let the good work be done.” Flee from this despondency, children! There is only one salvation for you: take yourself and make yourself answerable for all men’s sin. Friend, that is indeed the truth, for as soon as you make yourself sincerely answerable for everything and for everyone, you will see at once that it really is so, and that you are guilty for all and for everything. But throwing your own laziness and your own powerlessness onto others, you will end by sharing in Satan’s pride and murmuring against God. Of Satan’s pride I think this: it is hard for us on earth even to comprehend it, and so how easy it is to fall into error and share in it, and while thinking that we are doing something great and beautiful. Indeed, many of the strongest feelings and movements of our nature we cannot comprehend while we are on earth. Do not be led astray by that either, and do not think that it can serve to justify you in anything, for the eternal judge will ask of you what you could comprehend, not what you could not; you will be convinced of that yourself, for then you will see everything rightly and will no longer argue. On earth, indeed, we seem to wander, and if the precious image of Christ were not before us, we should perish and go utterly astray, like the human race before the flood. Much on earth is hidden from us, but in return we have been given a secret, hidden sense of our living bond with another world, a higher and heavenly world; and the roots of our thoughts and feelings are not here but in other worlds. That is why the philosophers say that the essence of things cannot be comprehended on earth. God took seeds from other worlds and sowed them on this earth and grew his garden, and everything that could come up came up; but what has grown lives and is alive only through the feeling of its contact with mysterious other worlds. If that feeling grows weak or is destroyed in you, then what has grown in you dies too. Then you will become indifferent to life and even come to hate it. That is what I think.

## (h) Can One Be the Judge of One’s Fellow Men? Of Faith to the End

Remember especially that you cannot be anyone’s judge. For there can be no judge of a criminal on earth until the judge himself knows that he too is just such a criminal as the one standing before him, and that he perhaps is more to blame than anyone for the crime of the one before him. When he understands that, then he will be able to be a judge. However mad it may seem, it is the truth. For if I myself were righteous, perhaps there would be no criminal standing before me. If you can take upon yourself the crime of the criminal who stands before you and whom your heart is judging, then take it at once and suffer for him yourself, and let him go without reproach. And even if the law itself has made you his judge, act in that spirit then too, as far as you possibly can, for he will go away and condemn himself more bitterly than your judgement would. And if he goes away unfeeling after your kiss, laughing at you, do not be led astray by that either: it means his time has not yet come, but it will come in its time; and if it does not come, no matter: if not he, then another in his place will understand, and suffer, and condemn and accuse himself, and justice will be fulfilled. Believe this, believe it without doubt, for in this very thing lies all the hope and all the faith of the saints.

Work unceasingly. If you remember in the night, as you lie down to sleep, “I have not done what I ought to have done,” get up at once and do it. If the people around you are spiteful and unfeeling and will not listen to you, fall down before them and beg their forgiveness, for truly you too are to blame that they do not want to listen to you. And if you can no longer speak with the embittered, serve them in silence and in lowliness, never losing hope. And if everyone abandons you and even drives you out by force, then, left alone, fall on the earth and kiss it, water it with your tears, and the earth will bear fruit from your tears, even though no one has seen or heard you in your solitude. Believe to the end, even if it should happen that everyone on earth were led astray and you alone remained faithful: offer your sacrifice even then, and praise God, you who alone remain. And if two of you such come together, there is already the whole world, a world of living love; embrace each other in tenderness and praise the Lord: for, if only in the two of you, his truth has been fulfilled.

If you yourself sin and are sorrowful even unto death for your sins, or for your sudden sin, then rejoice for another, rejoice for the righteous one, rejoice that if you have sinned, he is righteous and has not sinned.

But if the wickedness of men rouses in you an indignation and a grief that cannot be overcome, even to the point of wanting revenge on the wicked, then fear that feeling most of all; go at once and seek torments for yourself, as if you yourself were guilty of that wickedness of men. Take those torments and endure them, and your heart will be appeased, and you will understand that you too are guilty, for you might have shone a light for the wicked, even as the one sinless man, and you did not. If you had shone, your light would have lit the way for others too, and he who did the wicked deed would perhaps not have done it in your light. And even if you did shine but see that people are not saved even by your light, then remain firm and do not doubt the power of the heavenly light; believe that if they have not been saved now, they will be saved later. And if they are not saved later, their sons will be saved, for your light will not die, even though you have died. The righteous man departs, but his light remains. People are always saved after the death of him who saves them. The human race does not accept its prophets and kills them, but people love their martyrs and honour those whom they have tortured to death. And you are working for the whole, acting for the future. Never seek a reward, for great is your reward on this earth without it: your spiritual joy, which only the righteous find. Do not fear the great or the mighty, but be wise and always seemly. Know the measure, know the times; learn this. And when you are alone, pray. Love to throw yourself down on the earth and kiss it. Kiss the earth and love it unceasingly, insatiably; love all men, love everything; seek this rapture and ecstasy. Water the earth with the tears of your joy and love those tears of yours. And do not be ashamed of this ecstasy; treasure it, for it is a gift of God, a great one, and it is not given to many, but to the chosen.

## (i) Of Hell and Hellfire: A Mystical Reflection

Fathers and teachers, I ask myself: “What is hell?” And I reason thus: “The suffering of being no longer able to love.” Once, in infinite existence, unmeasured by time or space, a certain spiritual being was given, through his appearance on earth, the ability to say to himself: “I am, and I love.” Once, only once, he was given a moment of active, living love, and for that earthly life was given him, and with it times and seasons; and what then? This fortunate being rejected the priceless gift, did not value it, did not love it, looked at it mockingly and remained unfeeling. Such a one, having already left the earth, sees Abraham’s bosom and talks with Abraham, as we are shown in the parable of the rich man and Lazarus, and beholds paradise, and can go up to the Lord; but he is tormented precisely by this, that he will go up to the Lord, he who did not love, and come into contact with those who loved, he who scorned their love. For he sees clearly and says to himself: “Now I have knowledge, and though I have come to thirst to love, there will be no great deed in my love now, nor any sacrifice, for earthly life is over, and Abraham will not come with even a drop of living water (that is, with the gift of earthly life again, the former, active life) to cool the flame of thirst for spiritual love with which I burn now, having scorned it on earth; life is no more, and there shall be time no longer! Even if I would gladly give my life for others, I cannot now, for the life that could have been offered as a sacrifice to love has passed, and now there is a gulf between that life and this existence.” They speak of a material fire in hell. I do not search into that mystery, and I fear it; but I think that if there were a material fire, they would truly be glad of it, for, so I fancy, in material torment the still more terrible spiritual torment would be forgotten for a moment at least. And it is impossible to take that spiritual torment from them, for the torment is not outside them but within. And if it were possible to take it away, then, I think, they would be made even more bitterly unhappy by it. For though the righteous in paradise might forgive them, beholding their torments, and call them to themselves, loving them infinitely, by that very thing they would only multiply their torments, for they would rouse in them still more strongly the flame of thirst for an answering, active and grateful love which is no longer possible. In the timidity of my heart I think, however, that the very awareness of this impossibility would at last serve to relieve them, for, accepting the love of the righteous with no possibility of repaying it, in this submission and in the act of this humility they will find at last a kind of likeness of that active love which they scorned on earth, and a kind of action resembling it… I am sorry, my brothers and friends, that I cannot say this clearly. But woe to those who have destroyed themselves on earth, woe to the suicides! I think there can be none more unhappy than they. It is a sin, they tell us, to pray to God for them, and the church outwardly seems to reject them; but I think in the secret of my soul that one may pray for them too. Christ will not be angry at love. For such as these I have prayed inwardly all my life, I confess it to you, fathers and teachers, and I pray for them every day now.

Oh, there are some who remain proud and fierce even in hell, in spite of their certain knowledge and their contemplation of irrefutable truth; there are terrible ones who have given themselves wholly to Satan and his proud spirit. For them hell is voluntary and insatiable; they are martyrs of their own will. For they have cursed themselves, cursing God and life. They feed on their own spiteful pride, as if a starving man in the desert began to suck the blood out of his own body. But they are insatiable for ever and ever, and they reject forgiveness; they curse God who calls them. They cannot behold the living God without hatred, and they demand that there be no God of life, that God destroy himself and all his creation. And they will burn in the fire of their own wrath for ever, and thirst for death and non-existence. But they will not receive death.

Here Alexei Fyodorovich Karamazov’s manuscript ends. I repeat: it is incomplete and fragmentary. The biographical notes, for instance, cover only the elder’s early youth. And from his teachings and opinions, things said evidently at different times and from different promptings have been brought together as if into a single whole. What the elder actually said in these last hours of his life is not exactly determined; only an idea is given of the spirit and character of this conversation too, if one compares it with what Alexei Fyodorovich’s manuscript gives from his earlier teachings. The elder’s death came truly quite unexpectedly. For though all who had gathered round him on that last evening understood perfectly that his death was near, still it was impossible to imagine that it would come so suddenly; on the contrary, his friends, as I have already remarked, seeing him that night so cheerful, as it seemed, and so talkative, were even convinced that there had been a marked improvement in his health, if only for a short time. Even five minutes before his death, as they told afterwards with surprise, nothing could yet be foreseen. He suddenly felt, it seemed, a very sharp pain in his chest, turned pale and pressed his hands tightly to his heart. Everyone rose from their places then and rushed to him; but he, though suffering, still looking at them with a smile, quietly sank from his armchair to the floor and knelt, then bowed his face to the ground, stretched out his arms, and, as if in joyful rapture, kissing the earth and praying (as he himself had taught), quietly and joyfully gave up his soul to God. The news of his death spread at once through the hermitage and reached the monastery. Those closest to the newly departed, and those whose office it was, began to prepare his body according to the ancient rite, and all the brethren gathered in the cathedral church. And even before dawn, as rumour had it afterwards, the news of the newly departed reached the town. By morning almost the whole town was talking of the event, and a multitude of townspeople streamed to the monastery. But of this we shall speak in the next book; for now we will only add in advance that before the day was out something happened so unexpected for everyone, and, by the impression it made in the monastery and in the town, so strange, disturbing and confusing, that even now, after so many years, the most vivid memory is preserved in our town of that day, so disturbing for many…
