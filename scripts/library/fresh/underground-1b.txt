## II

I want now to tell you, gentlemen, whether you want to hear it or not, why I could not even become an insect. I tell you solemnly that I wanted to become an insect many times. But I was not even worthy of that. I swear to you, gentlemen, that to be too conscious is an illness, a real, thorough illness. For everyday human life ordinary human consciousness would be more than enough: that is, half or a quarter of the portion that falls to the lot of a cultivated man of our unhappy nineteenth century, especially one who has the double misfortune of living in Petersburg, the most abstract and premeditated city on the whole globe. (There are premeditated cities and unpremeditated ones.) It would be quite enough, for example, to have the consciousness that all so-called spontaneous people and men of action live by. I bet you think I am writing all this to show off, to make witty remarks at the expense of men of action, and that I am rattling my sword in bad taste, like my officer. But, gentlemen, who can be vain of his own illnesses, and show off with them too?

But what am I saying? Everyone does that; people are vain precisely of their illnesses, and I, perhaps, more than anyone. Let’s not argue; my objection is absurd. But all the same I am firmly convinced that not only too much consciousness, but any consciousness at all, is an illness. I insist on it. Let us leave that for a moment too. Tell me this: why did it happen, as if on purpose, that at the very moments, yes, at the very same moments when I was most capable of being conscious of all the refinements of “the beautiful and the sublime”, as we used to say, I happened not merely to be conscious of, but to do, such ugly things, such things as... well, in a word, things that perhaps everyone does, but which, as if on purpose, came to me precisely when I was most conscious that they ought not to be done at all? The more conscious I was of goodness and of all that “beautiful and sublime”, the deeper I sank into my mire and the more capable I was of getting stuck in it altogether. But the main thing was that all this seemed not to be accidental in me, but as if it had to be so. As if it were my most normal condition, and not at all an illness or a vice, so that in the end I lost even the wish to fight against this vice. It ended with my almost believing (and perhaps I really did believe) that this might well be my normal condition.

But at first, at the beginning, what agonies I went through in that struggle! I did not believe that this happened to others, and so all my life I kept it to myself as a secret. I was ashamed (perhaps I am ashamed even now); I got to the point of feeling a kind of secret, abnormal, mean little pleasure in going home to my corner on some most disgusting Petersburg night and being acutely conscious that today again I had done something vile, that what was done could never be undone, and inwardly, secretly, gnawing, gnawing at myself for it, sawing at myself and sucking at myself until the bitterness turned at last into a kind of shameful, accursed sweetness, and finally into a decided, serious pleasure! Yes, into pleasure, into pleasure! I insist on it. I have started talking precisely because I keep wanting to know for certain: do other people have such pleasures?

I will explain to you: the pleasure here came precisely from too vivid a consciousness of your own humiliation; from feeling yourself that you had reached the last wall; that it is vile, but that it cannot be otherwise; that there is no way out for you, that you will never become a different person; that even if there were still time and faith enough to change into something different, you probably would not want to change yourself; and if you did want to, you still would not do anything, because in reality there is perhaps nothing to change into. And the main thing, and the end of it all, is that all this happens according to the normal and fundamental laws of heightened consciousness, and by the inertia that follows directly from those laws, and consequently not only can you not change yourself, but you simply cannot do anything at all. It follows, for example, from heightened consciousness that you are right to be a scoundrel, as if it were any consolation to a scoundrel to feel that he really is a scoundrel. But enough... Ah, I have piled up a lot of words, but what have I explained?... What explains the pleasure here? But I will explain myself! I will see it through to the end! That is why I took up my pen...

I, for example, am terribly vain. I am suspicious and touchy, like a hunchback or a dwarf; but really, there have been moments when, if someone had happened to slap my face, I might even have been glad of it. I say it seriously: I would probably have managed to find a kind of pleasure even in that; the pleasure of despair, of course, but it is in despair that the most burning pleasures occur, especially when you are very strongly conscious that your position is hopeless. And with a slap in the face, why, you are simply crushed by the consciousness of what a pulp you have been ground into. The main thing is that, however you look at it, it always turns out that I am the first to blame for everything, and, what is most humiliating, to blame without being guilty, and, so to speak, by the laws of nature. To blame, in the first place, because I am cleverer than everyone around me. (I have always considered myself cleverer than everyone around me, and sometimes, would you believe it, I have even been ashamed of it. At any rate, all my life I have somehow looked away and could never look people straight in the eye.) To blame, finally, because if there were any magnanimity in me, it would only give me more torment from the consciousness of how useless it all is. For I would certainly not have been able to do anything with my magnanimity: neither to forgive, because perhaps the offender struck me by the laws of nature, and one cannot forgive the laws of nature; nor to forget, because though they are the laws of nature, it still hurts. Finally, even if I wanted to be not magnanimous at all, but, on the contrary, wished to take revenge on the offender, I could not take revenge on anyone for anything, because I would certainly never bring myself to do anything, even if I could. Why would I not bring myself to? About that I want to say a few words separately.
