## Chapter XXII

The ball had only just begun when Kitty and her mother went up the great staircase, flooded with light and lined with flowers and with footmen in powder and red coats. From the rooms came a steady murmur of movement, as in a beehive, and while they were setting their hair right before a mirror on the landing among the trees, the carefully distinct sounds of the orchestra’s violins could be heard from the ballroom, beginning the first waltz. A little old man in civilian dress, who was setting his grey side-locks straight before another mirror and giving off a smell of scent, ran into them on the stairs and stood aside, evidently admiring Kitty, whom he did not know. A beardless youth, one of those society youths whom the old Prince Shcherbatsky called puppies, in an exceedingly low-cut waistcoat, straightening his white tie as he went, bowed to them, ran past, came back, and asked Kitty for the quadrille. The first quadrille had already been given to Vronsky; she had to give this youth the second. An officer, buttoning his glove, stood aside at the door, and, stroking his moustache, admired the rosy Kitty.

Although her dress, her hair and all the preparations for the ball had cost Kitty great trouble and thought, she now, in her elaborate tulle dress over a pink slip, entered the ball as freely and simply as if all these rosettes and laces, all the details of her toilette, had not cost her or her household a moment’s attention, as if she had been born in this tulle and lace, with this high coiffure and the rose and two leaves on top of it.

When the old princess, before they went into the ballroom, wanted to set straight the twisted ribbon of her sash, Kitty drew back slightly. She felt that everything on her must be right and graceful of itself, and that nothing needed setting straight.

Kitty was having one of her happy days. Her dress was not tight anywhere; the lace bertha did not slip down anywhere; the rosettes were not crushed or torn off; the pink slippers with their high curved heels did not pinch but gladdened her feet. The thick plaits of fair hair stayed on her little head as if they were her own. All three buttons had done up without tearing on the long glove that wrapped her arm without changing its shape. The black velvet ribbon of her locket encircled her neck with particular tenderness. This velvet ribbon was a delight, and at home, looking at her neck in the mirror, Kitty had felt that this velvet was speaking. About everything else there might still be some doubt, but the velvet was a delight. Kitty smiled here too at the ball, glancing at it in a mirror. In her bare shoulders and arms Kitty felt a cold, marble feeling, a sensation she especially loved. Her eyes shone, and her red lips could not help smiling from the awareness of her attractiveness. She had hardly entered the ballroom and reached the throng of ladies, all tulle, ribbons, lace and flowers, waiting to be asked to dance (Kitty never stood long in that throng), when she was already asked for a waltz, and asked by the best partner, the chief partner in the ballroom hierarchy, the famous director of balls, the master of ceremonies, a married, handsome and well-built man, Yegorushka Korsunsky. He had only just left Countess Banina, with whom he had danced the first round of the waltz, and, looking over his domain, that is, the few couples who had started dancing, he saw Kitty coming in and ran up to her with that special free-and-easy amble peculiar to directors of balls, and, bowing, without even asking whether she wished it, lifted his arm to put it round her slender waist. She looked round for someone to give her fan to, and the hostess, smiling at her, took it.

“How good of you to come in time,” he said, putting his arm round her waist. “What a habit people have of coming late.”

She laid her left hand, bent, on his shoulder, and her little feet in pink slippers moved quickly, lightly and evenly in time to the music over the slippery parquet.

“One rests, waltzing with you,” he said to her, setting off in the first unhurried steps of the waltz. “Delightful, such lightness, précision,” he said to her what he said to almost all his good acquaintances.

She smiled at his praise and went on looking about the room over his shoulder. She was not a girl coming out for the first time, for whom at a ball all the faces merge into one magical impression; nor was she a girl dragged around to balls, to whom all the faces of a ball are so familiar that they bore her; she was midway between these two: she was excited, and at the same time so much in possession of herself that she could observe. In the left corner of the room, she saw, the flower of society had gathered. There was Lidi, Korsunsky’s wife, a beauty bare beyond what was possible; there was the hostess; there Krivin’s bald head shone, he who was always where the flower of society was; the young men looked that way, not daring to go up; and there her eyes found Stiva, and then she saw Anna’s lovely figure and head in a black velvet dress. And he was there. Kitty had not seen him since the evening when she refused Levin. With her long-sighted eyes Kitty knew him at once, and even noticed that he was looking at her.

“Well, another round? You are not tired?” said Korsunsky, a little out of breath.

“No, thank you.”

“Where shall I take you?”

“Madame Karenina is here, I think… take me to her.”

“Wherever you wish.”

And Korsunsky waltzed, slackening his step, straight towards the throng in the left corner of the room, saying, “Pardon, mesdames, pardon, pardon, mesdames,” and, tacking through the sea of lace, tulle and ribbons without catching on a single feather, he turned his partner sharply, so that her slender legs in their openwork stockings were shown, and her train spread out like a fan and covered Krivin’s knees. Korsunsky bowed, straightened his open shirt-front and gave her his arm to take her to Anna Arkadyevna. Kitty, flushed, took her train off Krivin’s knees and, a little giddy, looked round, searching for Anna. Anna was standing surrounded by ladies and men, talking. Anna was not in lilac, as Kitty had so wanted, but in a black, low-cut velvet dress that showed her full shoulders and bosom, shaped as if from old ivory, and her rounded arms with their slender, tiny wrists. The whole dress was trimmed with Venetian lace. On her head, in her black hair, her own without any addition, was a little wreath of pansies, and another like it on the black ribbon of her sash among the white lace. Her coiffure was not noticeable. All that was noticeable, and that adorned her, were those wilful short ringlets of curly hair that always escaped at the nape of her neck and at her temples. On her finely shaped, strong neck was a string of pearls.

Kitty had seen Anna every day; she was in love with her, and had pictured her without fail in lilac. But now, seeing her in black, she felt that she had not understood all her charm. She now saw her as entirely new and unexpected. Now she understood that Anna could not have been in lilac, and that her charm lay precisely in this: that she always stood out from her dress, that her dress could never be seen on her. And the black dress with its rich lace could not be seen on her; it was only a frame, and only she could be seen, simple, natural, elegant, and at the same time gay and animated.

She was standing, as always, holding herself extremely straight, and, when Kitty came up to the group, she was talking to the master of the house, her head turned slightly towards him.

“No, I will not throw a stone,” she was answering him about something, “though I don’t understand it,” she went on, shrugging her shoulders; and at once, with a tender smile of patronage, she turned to Kitty. Glancing over her dress with a quick feminine look, she made a movement of her head, barely noticeable but understood by Kitty, approving her dress and her beauty. “You come into the ballroom dancing,” she added.

“This is one of my most faithful helpers,” said Korsunsky, bowing to Anna Arkadyevna, whom he had not yet seen. “The princess helps to make a ball gay and beautiful. Anna Arkadyevna, a round of the waltz,” he said, bending forward.

“Do you two know each other?” asked the host.

“Whom do we not know? My wife and I are like white wolves; everyone knows us,” answered Korsunsky. “A round of the waltz, Anna Arkadyevna.”

“I don’t dance when it is possible not to dance,” she said.

“But tonight it is impossible,” answered Korsunsky.

At that moment Vronsky came up.

“Well, if it is impossible not to dance tonight, then let us go,” she said, not noticing Vronsky’s bow, and quickly put her hand on Korsunsky’s shoulder.

“Why is she displeased with him?” thought Kitty, noticing that Anna had deliberately not answered Vronsky’s bow. Vronsky came up to Kitty, reminding her of the first quadrille and regretting that all this time he had not had the pleasure of seeing her. Kitty listened to him, looking admiringly at Anna waltzing. She was waiting for him to ask her to waltz, but he did not, and she looked at him in surprise. He blushed and hurriedly asked her to waltz; but no sooner had he put his arm round her slender waist and taken the first step than the music suddenly stopped. Kitty looked at his face, which was so near her; and long afterwards, for several years, that look full of love which she gave him then, and to which he did not answer, cut her heart with tormenting shame.

“Pardon, pardon! A waltz, a waltz!” Korsunsky shouted from the other side of the room, and, catching up the first young lady who came to hand, began dancing himself.

## Chapter XXIII

Vronsky and Kitty went round the room several times in the waltz. After the waltz Kitty went to her mother, and had hardly time to say a few words to Countess Nordston before Vronsky came for her for the first quadrille. Nothing significant was said during the quadrille; there was a broken conversation, now about the Korsunskys, husband and wife, whom he described very amusingly as sweet children of forty, now about the public theatre that was being planned; and only once did the conversation touch her to the quick, when he asked about Levin, whether he was here, and added that he had liked him very much. But Kitty had not expected more from the quadrille. She was waiting with a sinking heart for the mazurka. It seemed to her that in the mazurka everything must be decided. That he had not asked her for the mazurka during the quadrille did not trouble her. She was sure that she would dance the mazurka with him, as at earlier balls, and she had refused five men for the mazurka, saying that she was engaged. The whole ball, up to the last quadrille, was for Kitty a magic dream of joyful colours, sounds and movements. She did not dance only when she felt too tired and asked for a rest. But, dancing the last quadrille with one of the dull young men whom it was impossible to refuse, she happened to be vis-à-vis with Vronsky and Anna. She had not come near Anna since the beginning of the evening, and here suddenly she saw her again as entirely new and unexpected. She saw in her the feature so familiar to herself, of excitement at success. She saw that Anna was drunk with the wine of the admiration she was arousing. She knew that feeling and knew its signs, and saw them in Anna: saw the quivering, flashing brightness in her eyes, and the smile of happiness and excitement that curved her lips in spite of herself, and the distinct grace, sureness and lightness of her movements.

“Who is it?” she asked herself. “All of them, or one?” And, not helping the tormented young man with whom she was dancing in the conversation, whose thread he had dropped and could not pick up, and outwardly submitting to the loud, cheerful, commanding shouts of Korsunsky, who threw everyone now into the grand rond, now into the chaîne, she watched, and her heart contracted more and more. “No, it is not the admiration of the crowd that has made her drunk, but the rapture of one. And that one? Can it be he?” Every time he spoke to Anna, a joyful brightness flashed in her eyes, and a smile of happiness curved her red lips. She seemed to be making an effort over herself not to show these signs of joy, but they came out on her face of themselves. “But what of him?” Kitty looked at him and was horrified. What Kitty had seen so clearly in the mirror of Anna’s face, she saw in his. What had become of his always calm, firm manner and the carelessly calm expression of his face? No, now, every time he turned to her, he bent his head a little, as though wishing to fall down before her, and in his look there was only an expression of submission and fear. “I do not want to offend,” his look seemed to say each time, “but I want to save myself, and I don’t know how.” There was an expression on his face she had never seen before.

They talked about common acquaintances, carried on the most trifling conversation, but it seemed to Kitty that every word they said was deciding their fate and hers. And the strange thing was that though they really were talking about how ridiculous Ivan Ivanovich was with his French, and about how a better match could have been found for the Eletskaya girl, yet these words had a meaning for them, and they felt it, just as Kitty did. The whole ball, the whole world, everything was covered with mist in Kitty’s soul. Only the strict school of upbringing she had been through held her up and made her do what was required of her, that is, dance, answer questions, talk, even smile. But before the mazurka began, when they were already setting out the chairs and some couples were moving from the small rooms into the ballroom, a moment of despair and horror came over Kitty. She had refused five men, and now she was not dancing the mazurka. There was not even any hope of being asked, precisely because she had had too great a success in society, and it could not enter anyone’s head that she had not been asked before now. She ought to tell her mother she was ill and go home, but she had not the strength for that. She felt crushed.

She went to the far end of the little drawing-room and sank into an armchair. The airy skirt of her dress rose like a cloud round her slender waist; one bare, thin, tender girlish arm, hanging down helplessly, sank in the folds of her pink tunic; in the other she held her fan and with quick, short movements fanned her hot face. But in spite of this look of a butterfly that has just clung to a blade of grass and is about to flutter up and spread its rainbow wings, a terrible despair wrung her heart. “But perhaps I am mistaken; perhaps it was not so?”

And again she went over everything she had seen.

“Kitty, what does this mean?” said Countess Nordston, who had come up to her noiselessly over the carpet. “I don’t understand it.”

Kitty’s lower lip trembled; she got up quickly.

“Kitty, you are not dancing the mazurka?”

“No, no,” said Kitty in a voice trembling with tears.

“He asked her for the mazurka in front of me,” said Countess Nordston, knowing that Kitty would understand who he and she were. “She said: aren’t you dancing with Princess Shcherbatskaya?”

“Oh, it’s all the same to me!” answered Kitty.

No one but she herself understood her position; no one knew that the day before she had refused a man whom she perhaps loved, and refused him because she believed in another.

Countess Nordston found Korsunsky, with whom she was dancing the mazurka, and told him to ask Kitty.

Kitty danced in the first couple, and, luckily for her, she did not have to talk, because Korsunsky was running about all the time, directing his domain. Vronsky and Anna were sitting almost opposite her. She saw them with her long-sighted eyes, she saw them close to when they met in the figures, and the more she saw of them, the more convinced she became that her unhappiness had come to pass. She saw that they felt themselves alone in this crowded room. And on Vronsky’s face, always so firm and independent, she saw that expression which had struck her, of bewilderment and submission, like the expression of an intelligent dog when it has done wrong.

Anna smiled, and the smile passed to him. She grew thoughtful, and he became serious. Some supernatural force drew Kitty’s eyes to Anna’s face. She was lovely in her simple black dress; lovely were her full arms with their bracelets; lovely her firm neck with its string of pearls; lovely the curling hair of her disordered coiffure; lovely the graceful, light movements of her small feet and hands; lovely that beautiful face in its animation; but there was something terrible and cruel in her loveliness.

Kitty admired her still more than before, and suffered more and more. Kitty felt crushed, and her face expressed it. When Vronsky saw her, meeting her in the mazurka, he did not recognize her at once, she had changed so.

“A splendid ball!” he said to her, for the sake of saying something.

“Yes,” she answered.

In the middle of the mazurka, repeating a complicated figure newly invented by Korsunsky, Anna came out into the middle of the circle, took two partners, and called a lady and Kitty to her. Kitty looked at her in fright as she came up. Anna looked at her with half-closed eyes and smiled, pressing her hand. But, noticing that Kitty’s face answered her smile only with an expression of despair and surprise, she turned away from her and began talking gaily to the other lady.

“Yes, there is something terrible, devilish and lovely in her,” Kitty said to herself.

Anna did not want to stay for supper, but the host began to press her.

“Come now, Anna Arkadyevna,” Korsunsky began, drawing her bare arm under the sleeve of his tailcoat. “What an idea I have for the cotillion! Un bijou!”

And he moved little by little, trying to draw her along. The host smiled approvingly.

“No, I shall not stay,” answered Anna, smiling; but in spite of the smile, both Korsunsky and the host understood from the decided tone in which she answered that she would not stay.

“No, as it is I have danced more in Moscow at your one ball than all winter in Petersburg,” said Anna, glancing round at Vronsky, who was standing beside her. “I must rest before the journey.”

“And you are really going tomorrow?” asked Vronsky.

“Yes, I think so,” answered Anna, as though surprised at the boldness of his question; but the irrepressible quivering brightness of her eyes and of her smile scorched him as she said it.

Anna Arkadyevna did not stay for supper, and left.
