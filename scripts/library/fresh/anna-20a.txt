## Part Four · Chapter XVII

Going back over the impressions of the conversations held during and after dinner, without meaning to, Alexei Alexandrovich returned to his lonely hotel room. Darya Alexandrovna’s words about forgiveness had produced nothing in him but vexation. Whether or not the Christian rule applied to his case was a question too difficult to be spoken of lightly, and Alexei Alexandrovich had long since answered it in the negative. Of all that had been said, what had sunk deepest into his imagination were the words of the foolish, good-natured Turovtsyn: he acted like a fine fellow; challenged him to a duel and killed him. Everyone had evidently sympathized with this, though out of politeness they had not said so.

“However, that matter is settled; there is no point in thinking about it,” Alexei Alexandrovich said to himself. And, thinking only of his coming departure and the business of the inspection, he went into his room and asked the porter who showed him up where his servant was; the porter said the servant had just gone out. Alexei Alexandrovich ordered tea, sat down at the table, and, taking up the railway guide, began working out the route of his journey.

“Two telegrams,” said the servant, coming back into the room. “Forgive me, your excellency, I had only just gone out.”

Alexei Alexandrovich took the telegrams and opened them. The first telegram was the news of Stremov’s appointment to the very post Karenin had wanted. Alexei Alexandrovich threw the dispatch down and, flushing, got up and began pacing the room. “Quos vult perdere dementat,” he said, meaning by quos the persons who had helped to bring about this appointment. What vexed him was not that he had not received the post, that he had evidently been passed over; but it was incomprehensible, astonishing to him, that they did not see that the windbag and phrase-maker Stremov was less capable of it than anyone else. How could they not see that they were ruining themselves, their prestige, by this appointment!

“Something else of the same kind,” he said to himself bitterly, opening the second dispatch. The telegram was from his wife. Her signature in blue pencil, “Anna”, was the first thing that caught his eye. “I am dying; I beg, I implore you to come. I shall die more peacefully with your forgiveness,” he read. He smiled contemptuously and threw the telegram down. That this was deceit and cunning there could be, as it seemed to him in the first minute, no doubt whatever.

“There is no deceit at which she would stop. She is due to give birth. Perhaps it is the illness of childbirth. But what is their aim? To legitimize the child, to compromise me and prevent the divorce,” he thought. “But something is said there: I am dying…” He read the telegram again; and suddenly the direct meaning of what was said in it struck him. “And if it is true?” he said to himself. “If it is true that in the moment of suffering and the nearness of death she sincerely repents, and I, taking it for deceit, refuse to go? That would not only be cruel, and everyone would condemn me, but it would be stupid on my part.”

“Pyotr, stop the carriage. I am going to Petersburg,” he said to his servant.

Alexei Alexandrovich decided that he would go to Petersburg and see his wife. If her illness was a deceit, he would say nothing and go away again. If she was really ill and dying and wished to see him before her death, he would forgive her if he found her alive, and pay her the last duty if he arrived too late.

All the way he thought no more about what he ought to do.

With the feeling of weariness and uncleanness that a night in a railway carriage produces, Alexei Alexandrovich drove in the early fog of Petersburg along the deserted Nevsky Prospect and looked straight ahead, not thinking of what awaited him. He could not think of it, because, picturing to himself what would happen, he could not drive away the supposition that her death would at once untie all the difficulty of his position. Bakers, shuttered shops, night cabmen, yard-keepers sweeping the pavements flashed before his eyes, and he watched it all, trying to stifle in himself the thought of what awaited him and what he dared not wish for and yet did wish for. He drove up to the porch. A cab and a carriage with a sleeping coachman were standing at the entrance. Going into the hall, Alexei Alexandrovich, as it were, fetched a decision out of a far corner of his brain and consulted it. It said: “If it is deceit, then calm contempt, and go away. If it is true, then observe the proprieties.”

The porter opened the door even before Alexei Alexandrovich rang. The porter, Petrov, otherwise Kapitonych, looked strange in an old frock-coat, without a tie and in slippers.

“How is your mistress?”

“She was safely delivered yesterday.”

Alexei Alexandrovich stopped and turned pale. He understood clearly now with what force he had wished for her death.

“And her health?”

Korney, in his morning apron, ran down the stairs.

“Very bad,” he answered. “There was a consultation of doctors yesterday, and the doctor is here now.”

“Take my things,” said Alexei Alexandrovich, and, feeling a certain relief at the news that there was still hope of death, he went into the hall.

On the hall-stand hung a military overcoat. Alexei Alexandrovich noticed it and asked:

“Who is here?”

“The doctor, the midwife and Count Vronsky.”

Alexei Alexandrovich went through into the inner rooms.

There was no one in the drawing-room; at the sound of his steps the midwife, in a cap with lilac ribbons, came out of Anna’s boudoir.

She came up to Alexei Alexandrovich and, with the familiarity that the nearness of death brings, took him by the hand and led him towards the bedroom.

“Thank God you have come! She talks of nothing but you, you,” she said.

“Bring the ice quickly!” said the doctor’s commanding voice from the bedroom.

Alexei Alexandrovich went into her boudoir. By her table, sideways to its back, on a low chair, sat Vronsky, with his face in his hands, weeping. He jumped up at the doctor’s voice, took his hands from his face, and saw Alexei Alexandrovich. Seeing the husband, he was so confused that he sat down again, drawing his head down into his shoulders as though wishing to vanish somewhere; but he made an effort over himself, got up and said:

“She is dying. The doctors say there is no hope. I am entirely in your power, but let me stay here… however, I am at your will, I…”

Alexei Alexandrovich, seeing Vronsky’s tears, felt a rush of that inner turmoil which the sight of other people’s suffering produced in him, and, turning his face away, without hearing him out, went hurriedly to the door. From the bedroom came the sound of Anna’s voice saying something. Her voice was gay, animated, with extraordinarily distinct intonations. Alexei Alexandrovich went into the bedroom and up to the bed. She was lying with her face turned towards him. Her cheeks glowed with colour, her eyes shone, her small white hands, coming out of the cuffs of her jacket, were playing with a corner of the blanket, twisting it. She seemed not only healthy and fresh, but in the best of spirits. She was speaking quickly, resonantly, with extraordinarily correct and deeply felt intonations.

“Because Alexei, I am speaking of Alexei Alexandrovich (what a strange, terrible fate, that both are Alexei, isn’t it?), Alexei would not refuse me. I would forget, he would forgive… But why doesn’t he come? He is good; he doesn’t know himself how good he is. Oh, my God, what anguish! Give me some water, quickly, quickly! Oh, that will be bad for her, for my little girl! Well, all right, give her to a wet-nurse. Well, I agree, it is even better. He will come; it will hurt him to see her. Take her away.”

“Anna Arkadyevna, he has come. Here he is!” said the midwife, trying to draw her attention to Alexei Alexandrovich.

“Oh, what nonsense!” Anna went on, not seeing her husband. “But give her to me, give me my little girl! He hasn’t come yet. You say he won’t forgive because you don’t know him. No one knew him. Only I did, and even for me it became hard. His eyes, you must know, Seryozha has exactly the same eyes, and that is why I can’t look at them. Has Seryozha been given his dinner? I know everyone will forget. He would not forget. Seryozha must be moved into the corner room, and Mariette asked to sleep with him.”

Suddenly she shrank, fell silent, and in fright, as though expecting a blow, as though defending herself, raised her hands to her face. She had seen her husband.

“No, no,” she began, “I am not afraid of him, I am afraid of death. Alexei, come here. I am in a hurry because I have no time; I have only a little while left to live; soon the fever will begin, and I shall understand nothing any more. Now I understand, I understand everything, I see everything.”

Alexei Alexandrovich’s wrinkled face took on an expression of suffering; he took her hand and wanted to say something, but could not get it out; his lower lip trembled, but he was still struggling with his emotion and only glanced at her now and then. And every time he glanced at her, he saw her eyes, which looked at him with such touched and rapturous tenderness as he had never seen in them.

“Wait, you don’t know… Wait, wait…” She stopped, as though collecting her thoughts. “Yes,” she began. “Yes, yes, yes. This is what I wanted to say. Don’t be surprised at me. I am still the same… But there is another woman in me; I am afraid of her: she fell in love with that man, and I wanted to hate you and could not forget the woman who was there before. That woman is not I. Now I am my real self, all of me. I am dying now, I know I shall die; ask him. Even now I feel them, here they are, weights of a hundred pounds on my hands, on my feet, on my fingers. My fingers are like this, enormous! But it will soon be over… There is one thing I need: forgive me, forgive me completely! I am terrible, but my nurse used to tell me: the holy martyr, what was her name? she was worse. And I shall go to Rome; there are wildernesses there, and then I shall be in no one’s way; I shall only take Seryozha and the little girl… No, you cannot forgive! I know it can’t be forgiven! No, no, go away, you are too good!” She held his hand with one burning hand and pushed him away with the other.

Alexei Alexandrovich’s inner turmoil kept growing, and had now reached such a degree that he ceased to struggle against it; he suddenly felt that what he had considered inner turmoil was, on the contrary, a blissful state of soul, which had suddenly given him a new happiness he had never known. He was not thinking that the Christian law which he had all his life wanted to follow commanded him to forgive and love his enemies; but a joyful feeling of love and forgiveness towards his enemies filled his soul. He knelt, and, laying his head in the crook of her arm, which burned him like fire through the jacket, he sobbed like a child. She put her arm round his balding head, moved towards him, and raised her eyes upwards with defiant pride.

“There he is, I knew it. Now goodbye, everyone, goodbye!… They have come again; why don’t they go?… Oh, take these fur coats off me!”

The doctor took her hands away, carefully laid her on the pillow, and covered her shoulders. She lay back submissively and looked in front of her with a radiant gaze.

“Remember one thing: that all I needed was forgiveness, and I want nothing more… Why doesn’t he come?” she said, turning towards the door, to Vronsky. “Come here, come here! Give him your hand.”

Vronsky came to the edge of the bed and, seeing her, again covered his face with his hands.

“Uncover your face, look at him. He is a saint,” she said. “Uncover it, uncover your face!” she said angrily. “Alexei Alexandrovich, uncover his face! I want to see him.”

Alexei Alexandrovich took Vronsky’s hands and drew them away from his face, terrible with the expression of suffering and shame that was on it.

“Give him your hand. Forgive him.”

Alexei Alexandrovich gave him his hand, not holding back the tears that poured from his eyes.

“Thank God, thank God,” she said, “now everything is ready. Only let me stretch my legs a little. There, like that, that’s lovely. How tastelessly those flowers are done, not a bit like violets,” she said, pointing to the wallpaper. “My God, my God! When will it end? Give me morphine. Doctor! Give me morphine. My God, my God!”

And she began to toss about on the bed.

The doctor and the other doctors said it was puerperal fever, in which ninety-nine cases in a hundred end in death. All day there was fever, delirium and unconsciousness. By midnight the patient lay without feeling and almost without a pulse.

The end was expected every minute.

Vronsky went home, but in the morning came to inquire, and Alexei Alexandrovich, meeting him in the hall, said:

“Stay; perhaps she will ask for you,” and himself took him into his wife’s boudoir.

Towards morning the excitement began again, the liveliness, the rapidity of thought and speech, and again it ended in unconsciousness. On the third day it was the same, and the doctor said there was hope. That day Alexei Alexandrovich went into the boudoir where Vronsky was sitting, and, locking the door, sat down opposite him.

“Alexei Alexandrovich,” said Vronsky, feeling that an explanation was coming, “I cannot speak, I cannot understand. Spare me! However hard it is for you, believe me, it is still more terrible for me.”

He wanted to get up. But Alexei Alexandrovich took him by the hand.

“I ask you to hear me out; it is necessary. I must explain my feelings to you, the feelings that have guided me and will guide me, so that you are not mistaken about me. You know that I had decided on a divorce and had even begun the proceedings. I will not hide from you that, in beginning them, I was undecided; I was tormented; I confess to you that a desire for revenge on you and on her pursued me. When I received the telegram, I came here with the same feelings; I will say more: I wished for her death. But…” He was silent for a while, thinking whether to reveal his feeling to him or not. “But I saw her and forgave. And the happiness of forgiveness revealed my duty to me. I have forgiven completely. I want to turn the other cheek, I want to give my shirt when my coat is taken, and I pray God only that He will not take from me the happiness of forgiveness!” Tears stood in his eyes, and their bright, calm look struck Vronsky. “That is my position. You may trample me in the mud, make me the laughing-stock of the world; I shall not abandon her, and I shall never say a word of reproach to you,” he went on. “My duty is clearly marked out for me: I must be with her, and I shall be. If she wishes to see you, I will let you know; but now, I suppose, it would be better for you to go away.”

He stood up, and a sob cut short his speech. Vronsky stood up too and, bent, not straightened, looked at him from under his brows. He was crushed. He did not understand Alexei Alexandrovich’s feeling, but he felt that it was something higher, and even beyond his reach in his view of the world.

## Chapter XVIII

After his conversation with Alexei Alexandrovich, Vronsky went out onto the porch of the Karenins’ house and stopped, remembering with difficulty where he was and where he had to walk or drive to. He felt ashamed, humiliated, guilty, and deprived of any possibility of washing away his humiliation. He felt knocked out of the rut along which he had walked so proudly and easily until now. All the habits and rules of his life, which had seemed so firm, had suddenly turned out false and inapplicable. The husband, the deceived husband, who until now had seemed a pitiful creature, an accidental and somewhat comic obstacle to his happiness, had suddenly been summoned by her herself and raised to a height that inspired servility; and on that height the husband had appeared not wicked, not false, not ridiculous, but kind, simple and majestic. Vronsky could not help feeling it. The roles had suddenly changed. Vronsky felt the other man’s height and his own humiliation, the other’s rightness and his own wrong. He felt that the husband had been magnanimous even in his grief, while he had been base and petty in his deceit. But this awareness of his baseness before the man he had unjustly despised made up only a small part of his grief. He felt inexpressibly unhappy now because his passion for Anna, which, it had seemed to him, had been cooling lately, had become stronger than it had ever been, now that he knew he had lost her for ever. He had seen all of her during her illness, had come to know her soul, and it seemed to him that he had never loved her until then. And now, when he had come to know her and to love her as he ought to have loved her, he had been humiliated before her and had lost her for ever, leaving her only a shameful memory of himself. Most terrible of all was his ridiculous, shameful position when Alexei Alexandrovich had pulled his hands away from his ashamed face. He stood on the porch of the Karenins’ house like a lost man and did not know what to do.

“Shall I call a cab, sir?” asked the porter.

“Yes, a cab.”

Coming home after three sleepless nights, Vronsky, without undressing, lay face down on the sofa, folding his arms and laying his head on them. His head was heavy. Images, memories and the strangest thoughts followed one another with extraordinary speed and clearness: now it was the medicine he had poured out for the patient and spilled over the spoon; now the midwife’s white hands; now Alexei Alexandrovich’s strange position on the floor before the bed.

“To sleep! To forget!” he said to himself, with the calm confidence of a healthy man that if he is tired and wants to sleep he will fall asleep at once. And indeed, at that very moment his head began to grow confused, and he began to sink into the abyss of oblivion. The waves of the sea of unconscious life were already beginning to close over his head, when suddenly, as though a powerful charge of electricity had been discharged into him, he started so violently that his whole body bounced on the springs of the sofa, and, propping himself on his hands, he sprang up on his knees in fright. His eyes were wide open, as though he had never slept. The heaviness of his head and the weakness of his limbs, which he had felt a minute before, suddenly vanished.

“You may trample me in the mud,” he heard Alexei Alexandrovich’s words, and saw him before him; and he saw Anna’s face with its feverish flush and shining eyes, looking with tenderness and love not at him but at Alexei Alexandrovich; he saw his own figure, foolish and ridiculous as it seemed to him, when Alexei Alexandrovich took his hands away from his face. He stretched out his legs again and threw himself on the sofa in the same position as before and shut his eyes.

“To sleep! To sleep!” he repeated to himself. But with his eyes shut he saw Anna’s face still more clearly, as it had been on the evening before the races, which he remembered so well.

“That is gone and will never be, and she wants to wipe it out of her memory. And I cannot live without it. How are we to be reconciled, how are we to be reconciled?” he said aloud, and without knowing it began repeating these words. This repetition of words held back the rise of new images and memories, which, he felt, were crowding in his head. But the repetition of words did not hold back his imagination for long. Again, one after another, with extraordinary speed, the best moments came before him, and with them the recent humiliation. “Take your hands away,” says Anna’s voice. He takes his hands away and feels the ashamed and foolish expression of his face.

He went on lying there, trying to fall asleep, though he felt there was not the slightest hope of it, and kept repeating in a whisper chance words from some thought, wishing by this to hold back the rise of new images. He listened, and heard, repeated in a strange, mad whisper, the words: “Did not know how to value it, did not know how to make use of it; did not know how to value it, did not know how to make use of it.”

“What is this? Am I going out of my mind?” he said to himself. “Perhaps. Why else do people go out of their minds, why else do they shoot themselves?” he answered himself, and, opening his eyes, he saw with surprise beside his head an embroidered cushion, the work of Varya, his brother’s wife. He touched the tassel of the cushion and tried to think about Varya, about when he had seen her last. But to think of anything else was agony. “No, I must sleep!” He moved the cushion and pressed his head against it, but he had to make an effort to keep his eyes shut. He jumped up and sat down. “That is finished for me,” he said to himself. “I must think out what to do. What is left?” His thought ran quickly over his life outside his love for Anna.

“Ambition? Serpukhovskoy? Society? The court?” He could stop at nothing. All this had had meaning before, but now none of it was there any longer. He got up from the sofa, took off his coat, loosened his belt and, baring his hairy chest so as to breathe more freely, walked about the room. “This is how people go mad,” he repeated, “and this is how they shoot themselves… so as not to be ashamed,” he added slowly.

He went to the door and shut it; then, with a fixed gaze and with his teeth tightly clenched, he went to the table, took the revolver, looked it over, turned it to a loaded chamber, and fell to thinking. For two minutes or so, his head bowed with an expression of a strained effort of thought, he stood motionless with the revolver in his hands and thought. “Of course,” he said to himself, as though a logical, long and clear train of thought had led him to an undoubted conclusion. In reality this “of course”, so convincing to him, was only the consequence of repeating exactly the same circle of memories and images through which he had already gone dozens of times in the course of that hour. They were the same memories of happiness lost for ever, the same idea of the senselessness of everything that lay ahead in life, the same awareness of his humiliation. The sequence of these images and feelings was the same too.

“Of course,” he repeated, when for the third time his thought went round the same enchanted circle of memories and thoughts; and, putting the revolver to the left side of his chest and jerking his whole arm violently, as though suddenly clenching it into a fist, he pulled the trigger. He did not hear the sound of the shot, but a violent blow on his chest knocked him off his feet. He tried to hold on to the edge of the table, dropped the revolver, staggered, and sat down on the floor, looking round him in surprise. He did not recognize his room, looking up from below at the curved legs of the table, at the waste-paper basket and the tiger skin. The quick creaking steps of his servant, coming through the drawing-room, brought him to his senses. He made an effort of thought and understood that he was on the floor, and, seeing blood on the tiger skin and on his hand, understood that he had shot himself.

“Stupid! I missed,” he said, groping with his hand for the revolver. The revolver was beside him; he searched further. Still searching, he stretched out to the other side and, unable to keep his balance, fell over, bleeding.

The elegant servant with side-whiskers, who had more than once complained to his acquaintances of the weakness of his nerves, was so frightened when he saw his master lying on the floor that he left him bleeding and ran for help. An hour later Varya, his brother’s wife, arrived, and with the help of three doctors, whom she had sent for in all directions and who arrived at the same time, put the wounded man to bed, and stayed to look after him.

## Chapter XIX

The mistake Alexei Alexandrovich had made in not considering, as he prepared to meet his wife, the possibility that her repentance would be sincere and that he would forgive her and she would not die: two months after his return from Moscow this mistake presented itself to him in all its force. But the mistake he had made came not only from his not having considered this possibility; it came also from his not having known his own heart until that day of his meeting with his dying wife. At the bedside of his sick wife he had for the first time in his life given himself up to that feeling of tender compassion which other people’s suffering called up in him, and of which he had formerly been ashamed as of a harmful weakness; and pity for her, and remorse at having wished for her death, and above all the very joy of forgiveness, had made him suddenly feel not only the easing of his own sufferings, but a peace of soul he had never known before. He suddenly felt that the very thing that had been the source of his sufferings had become the source of his spiritual joy; what had seemed insoluble while he condemned, reproached and hated had become simple and clear when he forgave and loved.

He had forgiven his wife and pitied her for her sufferings and her repentance. He had forgiven Vronsky and pitied him, especially after the rumours of his desperate act had reached him. He pitied his son too, more than before, and reproached himself now for having paid too little attention to him. But for the newborn little girl he felt a peculiar feeling, not only of pity but of tenderness. At first, from compassion alone, he had taken an interest in this weak newborn girl, who was not his daughter, and who had been neglected during her mother’s illness and would surely have died if he had not looked after her; and he did not notice himself how he came to love her. Several times a day he went into the nursery and sat there for a long time, so that the wet-nurse and the nurse, who were at first timid before him, got used to him. Sometimes for half an hour at a time he would look in silence at the sleeping baby’s saffron-red, downy and wrinkled little face, and watch the movements of the frowning forehead and the plump little hands with their curled-under fingers, rubbing the little eyes and the bridge of the nose with the backs of the palms. At such moments especially Alexei Alexandrovich felt perfectly calm and at one with himself, and saw nothing unusual in his position, nothing that needed to be changed.

But the more time passed, the more clearly he saw that, however natural this position was for him now, he would not be allowed to remain in it. He felt that besides the blessed spiritual force that guided his soul there was another, a coarse force, just as powerful or even more so, which guided his life, and that this force would not give him the humble peace he desired. He felt that everyone was looking at him with questioning surprise, that they did not understand him and expected something of him. In particular he felt the insecurity and unnaturalness of his relations with his wife.

When the softening produced in her by the nearness of death had passed, Alexei Alexandrovich noticed that Anna was afraid of him, found him a burden, and could not look him straight in the eyes. She seemed to want to say something to him and not to dare; and, as though she too felt that their relations could not go on, she seemed to be expecting something from him.

At the end of February it happened that Anna’s newborn daughter, who had also been named Anna, fell ill. Alexei Alexandrovich had been in the nursery in the morning and, having given orders to send for the doctor, had gone to the ministry. When he had finished his work, he came home at about four. Going into the hall, he saw a handsome footman in braided livery and a bearskin cape holding a white cloak of American fur.

“Who is here?” asked Alexei Alexandrovich.

“Princess Elizaveta Fyodorovna Tverskaya,” answered the footman, with a smile, as it seemed to Alexei Alexandrovich.

All through this hard time Alexei Alexandrovich had noticed that his acquaintances in society, especially the women, were taking a particular interest in him and his wife. He had noticed in all these acquaintances a joy at something, barely concealed, the very joy he had seen in the lawyer’s eyes and now in the footman’s. Everyone seemed delighted, as if they were giving someone away in marriage. When they met him they asked after her health with barely concealed joy.

The presence of Princess Tverskaya was unpleasant to Alexei Alexandrovich, both because of the memories connected with her and because he disliked her in general, and he went straight to the nursery. In the first nursery Seryozha, lying with his chest on the table and his feet on a chair, was drawing something and chattering cheerfully. The English governess, who during Anna’s illness had replaced the French one, sitting beside the boy with some fancy knitting, got up hurriedly, curtsied, and pulled at Seryozha.

Alexei Alexandrovich stroked his son’s hair, answered the governess’s question about his wife’s health, and asked what the doctor had said about the baby.

“The doctor said there is nothing dangerous, and prescribed baths, sir.”

“But she is still in pain,” said Alexei Alexandrovich, listening to the baby crying in the next room.

“I think the wet-nurse is no good, sir,” the Englishwoman said decidedly.

“Why do you think so?” he asked, stopping.

“It was the same at Countess Paul’s, sir. The baby was treated, and it turned out the baby was simply hungry: the nurse had no milk, sir.”

Alexei Alexandrovich thought for a moment and, after standing for a few seconds, went in at the other door. The little girl lay throwing her head back, writhing in the wet-nurse’s arms, and would neither take the plump breast offered to her nor be quiet, in spite of the double hushing of the wet-nurse and of the nurse bending over her.

“Still no better?” said Alexei Alexandrovich.

“She is very restless,” the nurse answered in a whisper.

“Miss Edwards says that perhaps the wet-nurse has no milk,” he said.

“I think so too, Alexei Alexandrovich.”

“Then why don’t you say so?”

“Whom am I to tell? Anna Arkadyevna is still unwell,” said the nurse with displeasure.

The nurse was an old servant of the house. And in these simple words of hers Alexei Alexandrovich thought he saw a hint at his position.

The baby screamed still louder, choking and wheezing. The nurse, with a wave of her hand, went up to it, took it from the wet-nurse’s arms, and began rocking it as she walked.

“We must ask the doctor to examine the wet-nurse,” said Alexei Alexandrovich.

The wet-nurse, healthy-looking and smartly dressed, afraid that she would be dismissed, muttered something under her breath and, covering up her large breast, smiled contemptuously at the doubt about her milk. In this smile too Alexei Alexandrovich found a mockery of his position.

“Poor baby!” said the nurse, hushing the child, and went on walking.

Alexei Alexandrovich sat down on a chair and, with a suffering, dejected face, watched the nurse walking up and down.

When the baby, quiet at last, had been put down in her deep cot, and the nurse, after setting the little pillow straight, had moved away, Alexei Alexandrovich got up and, walking on tiptoe with difficulty, went up to the child. For a minute he was silent and looked at the baby with the same dejected face; but suddenly a smile, moving his hair and the skin of his forehead, came over his face, and he went out of the room just as quietly.

In the dining-room he rang and told the servant who came in to send for the doctor again. He was vexed with his wife for not looking after this lovely child, and in this mood of vexation with her he did not want to go to her, nor did he want to see Princess Betsy; but his wife might be surprised that he did not come in to her as usual, and so, making an effort over himself, he went to the bedroom. As he approached the door over the soft carpet, he could not help overhearing a conversation he did not want to hear.

“If he were not going away, I could understand your refusal, and his too. But your husband ought to be above this,” Betsy was saying.

“It is not for my husband’s sake but for my own that I don’t want it. Don’t say that!” answered Anna’s agitated voice.

“Yes, but you cannot not wish to say goodbye to a man who shot himself because of you…”

“That is just why I don’t want to.”

Alexei Alexandrovich stopped with a frightened and guilty expression and wanted to go back unnoticed. But, thinking that this would be unworthy, he turned round again and, clearing his throat, went towards the bedroom. The voices fell silent, and he went in.

Anna, in a grey dressing-gown, with her black hair cut short and sticking out like a thick brush on her round head, was sitting on a couch. As always at the sight of her husband, the animation of her face suddenly vanished; she lowered her head and glanced uneasily at Betsy. Betsy, dressed in the very latest fashion, in a hat that hovered somewhere above her head like a shade over a lamp, and in a dove-grey dress with sharp diagonal stripes on the bodice going one way and on the skirt going the other, was sitting beside Anna, holding her flat, tall figure straight, and, bending her head, met Alexei Alexandrovich with a mocking smile.

“Ah!” she said, as though surprised. “I am very glad you are at home. You never appear anywhere, and I haven’t seen you since Anna’s illness. I have heard everything: your care of her. Yes, you are a wonderful husband!” she said with a significant and kindly air, as though bestowing on him an order of magnanimity for his conduct towards his wife.

Alexei Alexandrovich bowed coldly and, kissing his wife’s hand, asked after her health.

“Better, I think,” she answered, avoiding his eyes.

“But you seem to have a feverish colour,” he said, stressing the word “feverish”.

“We have been talking too much,” said Betsy. “I feel it is selfishness on my part, and I am going.”

She got up, but Anna, suddenly blushing, quickly caught her hand.

“No, stay, please. I must tell you… no, you,” she said, turning to Alexei Alexandrovich, and a flush spread over her neck and forehead. “I do not want to, and I cannot, keep anything hidden from you,” she said.

Alexei Alexandrovich cracked his fingers and lowered his head.

“Betsy was saying that Count Vronsky wished to call on us, to say goodbye before he leaves for Tashkent.” She was not looking at her husband, and was evidently hurrying to get it all out, hard as it was for her. “I said that I cannot receive him.”

“You said, my dear, that it would depend on Alexei Alexandrovich,” Betsy corrected her.

“No, no, I cannot receive him, and it would be of no… ” She suddenly stopped and looked questioningly at her husband (he was not looking at her). “In a word, I don’t want to…”

Alexei Alexandrovich moved closer and was about to take her hand.

Her first impulse was to snatch her hand away from his moist hand, with its big swollen veins, which was seeking hers; but, evidently making an effort over herself, she pressed his hand.

“I thank you very much for your trust, but…” he said, feeling with confusion and vexation that what he could easily and clearly decide by himself he could not discuss in front of Princess Tverskaya, who seemed to him the embodiment of that coarse force which was to guide his life in the eyes of the world and which prevented him from giving himself up to his feeling of love and forgiveness. He stopped, looking at Princess Tverskaya.

“Well, goodbye, my lovely,” said Betsy, getting up. She kissed Anna and went out. Alexei Alexandrovich saw her out.

“Alexei Alexandrovich! I know you for a truly magnanimous man,” said Betsy, stopping in the little drawing-room and pressing his hand once more, particularly firmly. “I am an outsider, but I love her so much and respect you so much that I allow myself to give you some advice. Receive him. Alexei is honour itself, and he is going to Tashkent.”

“I thank you, Princess, for your sympathy and your advice. But the question of whether my wife can or cannot receive anyone she will decide herself.”

He said this from habit, raising his eyebrows with dignity, and at once thought that, whatever the words, there could be no dignity in his position. And he saw it from the restrained, malicious and mocking smile with which Betsy looked at him after his phrase.

## Chapter XX

Alexei Alexandrovich bowed to Betsy in the ballroom and went to his wife. She was lying down, but, hearing his steps, she hurriedly sat up in her former position and looked at him in fright. He saw that she had been crying.

“I am very grateful for your trust in me,” he repeated meekly, in Russian, the phrase he had said in French in front of Betsy, and sat down beside her. When he spoke Russian and said “thou” to her, this “thou” irritated Anna uncontrollably. “And very grateful for your decision. I too suppose that, since he is leaving, there is no need at all for Count Vronsky to come here. However…”

“But I have said so already, so why repeat it?” Anna suddenly interrupted him with an irritation she had not managed to hold back. “No need at all,” she thought, “for a man to come and say goodbye to the woman he loves, for whom he wanted to die and ruined himself, and who cannot live without him. No need at all!” She pressed her lips together and lowered her shining eyes to his hands with their swollen veins, which were slowly rubbing each other.

“Let us never speak of it,” she added more calmly.

“I left it to you to decide this question, and I am very glad to see…” Alexei Alexandrovich began.

“That my wish agrees with yours,” she finished quickly, irritated that he spoke so slowly when she knew beforehand everything he would say.

“Yes,” he confirmed, “and Princess Tverskaya interferes quite inappropriately in the most difficult family matters. She in particular…”

“I don’t believe anything they say about her,” Anna said quickly. “I know she loves me sincerely.”

Alexei Alexandrovich sighed and was silent. She was playing anxiously with the tassels of her dressing-gown, glancing at him with that tormenting feeling of physical aversion for him, for which she reproached herself but which she could not overcome. She wished now for only one thing: to be rid of his hateful presence.

“I have just sent for the doctor,” said Alexei Alexandrovich.

“I am well; why should I need a doctor?”

“No, the little one is crying, and they say the wet-nurse has too little milk.”

“Why didn’t you let me nurse her when I begged to? All the same” (Alexei Alexandrovich understood what that “all the same” meant), “she is a baby, and they will kill her.” She rang and ordered the baby to be brought. “I asked to nurse her, they wouldn’t let me, and now I am the one reproached.”

“I am not reproaching…”

“Yes, you are reproaching me! My God! Why didn’t I die!” And she burst into sobs. “Forgive me, I am irritable, I am unfair,” she said, coming to herself. “But go away…”

“No, this cannot go on like this,” Alexei Alexandrovich said to himself decidedly, as he left his wife.

Never before had the impossibility of his position in the eyes of the world, and his wife’s hatred of him, and in general the power of that coarse, mysterious force which, against the mood of his soul, guided his life and demanded the fulfilment of its will and a change in his relations with his wife, presented themselves to him with such clarity as today. He saw clearly that the whole world and his wife demanded something of him, but what exactly he could not understand. He felt that because of this a feeling of anger was rising in his soul, destroying his peace and all the merit of his great deed. He considered that it would be better for Anna to break off her relations with Vronsky; but if they all found that this was impossible, he was ready even to allow these relations again, if only the children were not disgraced, if only he was not deprived of them and his position was not changed. Bad as this was, it was still better than a rupture, in which she would be put in a hopeless, shameful position, and he himself would be deprived of everything he loved. But he felt himself powerless; he knew beforehand that everyone was against him and that he would not be allowed to do what seemed to him now so natural and good, but would be made to do what was bad, but seemed to them the proper thing.

## Chapter XXI

Betsy had not yet had time to leave the ballroom when Stepan Arkadyich, who had just come from Eliseev’s, where fresh oysters had arrived, met her in the doorway.

“Ah! Princess! What a pleasant meeting!” he began. “And I have just been to your house.”

“A meeting for one minute, because I am leaving,” said Betsy, smiling and putting on her glove.

“Wait, Princess, before you put on your glove, let me kiss your little hand. There is nothing I am so grateful for in the return of old fashions as the kissing of hands.” He kissed Betsy’s hand. “When shall we meet?”

“You don’t deserve it,” answered Betsy, smiling.

“Oh yes, I deserve it very much, because I have become the most serious of men. I arrange not only my own family affairs but other people’s,” he said with a significant expression.

“Ah, I am very glad!” answered Betsy, understanding at once that he was speaking of Anna. And, going back into the ballroom, they stood in a corner. “He is killing her,” said Betsy in a significant whisper. “It is impossible, impossible…”

“I am glad you think so,” said Stepan Arkadyich, shaking his head with a serious and painfully sympathetic expression. “That is what I have come to Petersburg for.”

“The whole town is talking about it,” she said. “It is an impossible situation. She is wasting away. He does not understand that she is one of those women who cannot trifle with their feelings. One of two things: either he should take her away, act energetically, or give her a divorce. But this is stifling her.”

“Yes, yes… exactly…” said Oblonsky, sighing. “That is what I have come for. That is, not exactly for that… I have been made a chamberlain, and of course I had to give thanks. But the main thing is to settle this.”

“Well, God help you!” said Betsy.

Having seen Princess Betsy to the hall, kissed her hand once more above the glove, where the pulse beats, and told her yet more such improper nonsense that she no longer knew whether to be angry or to laugh, Stepan Arkadyich went to his sister. He found her in tears.

In spite of the mood, bubbling over with gaiety, in which he found himself, Stepan Arkadyich at once naturally passed into the sympathetic, poetically excited tone that suited her mood. He asked how she was and how she had spent the morning.

“Very, very badly. The day and the morning, and all the days past and to come,” she said.

“It seems to me you are giving way to gloom. You must shake yourself, you must look life straight in the face. I know it is hard, but…”

“I have heard that women love men even for their vices,” Anna began suddenly, “but I hate him for his virtues. I cannot live with him. You must understand, the sight of him has a physical effect on me, I am beside myself. I cannot, I cannot live with him. What am I to do? I was unhappy and thought it was impossible to be more unhappy; I could not imagine it. Would you believe that, knowing he is a kind, excellent man, that I am not worth his little finger, I still hate him? I hate him for his magnanimity. And nothing is left for me except…”

She wanted to say death, but Stepan Arkadyich did not let her finish.

“You are ill and irritable,” he said. “Believe me, you are exaggerating terribly. There is nothing so dreadful here.”

And Stepan Arkadyich smiled. No one else in Stepan Arkadyich’s place, dealing with such despair, would have allowed himself to smile (the smile would have seemed coarse); but in his smile there was so much kindness and almost feminine tenderness that his smile did not offend but softened and soothed. His quiet, soothing words and smiles had a softening, soothing effect, like oil of almonds. And Anna soon felt it.

“No, Stiva,” she said. “I am lost, lost! Worse than lost. I am not lost yet; I cannot say that everything is over; on the contrary, I feel that it is not over. I am like a string drawn tight that must snap. But it is not over yet… and it will end terribly.”

“Never mind; the string can be let down gently. There is no situation without a way out.”

“I have thought and thought. Only one…”

Again he understood from her frightened look that this one way out, in her opinion, was death, and he did not let her finish.

“Not at all,” he said. “Let me. You cannot see your position as I can. Let me tell you my opinion frankly.” Again he smiled cautiously his almond-oil smile. “I shall begin from the beginning: you married a man twenty years older than yourself. You married without love, or not knowing love. That was a mistake, let us say.”

“A terrible mistake!” said Anna.

“But I repeat: it is an accomplished fact. Then you had, let us say, the misfortune to love someone who was not your husband. That is a misfortune; but it too is an accomplished fact. And your husband has recognized it and forgiven it.” He stopped after each sentence, waiting for her objection, but she made no answer. “That is so. Now the question is: can you go on living with your husband? Do you wish it? Does he wish it?”

“I don’t know anything, anything.”

“But you said yourself that you cannot bear him.”

“No, I didn’t say so. I take it back. I know nothing and understand nothing.”

“Yes, but allow me…”

“You cannot understand. I feel that I am flying head first into some abyss, but I must not save myself. And I can’t.”

“Never mind, we shall spread something underneath and catch you. I understand you; I understand that you cannot take it on yourself to express your wish, your feeling.”

“I don’t wish for anything, anything… only for it all to end.”

“But he sees this and knows it. And do you suppose he finds it less of a burden than you do? You are tormented, he is tormented, and what can come of it? Whereas a divorce unties everything,” Stepan Arkadyich brought out, not without effort, his main idea, and looked at her significantly.

She made no answer and shook her cropped head in denial. But from the expression of her face, which suddenly lit up with its former beauty, he saw that she did not wish for it only because it seemed to her an impossible happiness.

“I am terribly sorry for you both! And how happy I would be if I could arrange it!” said Stepan Arkadyich, smiling more boldly now. “Don’t speak, don’t say anything! If only God lets me say it as I feel it. I am going to him.”

Anna looked at him with thoughtful, shining eyes and said nothing.

## Chapter XXII

Stepan Arkadyich, with the somewhat solemn face with which he took the president’s chair at his office, went into Alexei Alexandrovich’s study. Alexei Alexandrovich, his hands behind his back, was pacing the room and thinking about the same thing Stepan Arkadyich had been talking about with his wife.

“I’m not disturbing you?” said Stepan Arkadyich, suddenly feeling, at the sight of his brother-in-law, a sense of embarrassment unusual with him. To hide his embarrassment, he took out a cigarette-case he had just bought, which opened in a new way, and, sniffing the leather, took out a cigarette.

“No. Do you want something?” Alexei Alexandrovich answered reluctantly.

“Yes, I wanted… I need to h… yes, I need to have a talk,” said Stepan Arkadyich, feeling with surprise an unaccustomed timidity.

The feeling was so unexpected and strange that Stepan Arkadyich did not believe it was the voice of conscience telling him that what he meant to do was wrong. Stepan Arkadyich made an effort over himself and overcame the timidity that had come over him.

“I hope you believe in my love for my sister and in my sincere attachment and respect for you,” he said, blushing.

Alexei Alexandrovich stopped and made no answer, but his face struck Stepan Arkadyich by its expression of a submissive victim.

“I meant… I wanted to talk about my sister and about the position you are both in,” said Stepan Arkadyich, still struggling with his unaccustomed shyness.

Alexei Alexandrovich smiled sadly, looked at his brother-in-law, and, without answering, went to the table, took from it a letter he had begun, and handed it to his brother-in-law.

“I think of nothing else. And this is what I have begun to write, supposing that I can say it better in writing and that my presence irritates her,” he said, handing him the letter.

Stepan Arkadyich took the letter, looked with perplexed surprise at the dull eyes fixed motionless on him, and began to read.

“I see that my presence is a burden to you. However hard it has been for me to become convinced of it, I see that it is so and cannot be otherwise. I do not blame you, and God is my witness that, on seeing you during your illness, I resolved with all my soul to forget everything that had been between us and to begin a new life. I do not repent, and I shall never repent, of what I did; but I wished for one thing, your good, the good of your soul, and now I see that I have not achieved it. Tell me yourself what will give you true happiness and peace of soul. I give myself up entirely to your will and to your sense of justice.”

Stepan Arkadyich handed back the letter and went on looking at his brother-in-law with the same perplexity, not knowing what to say. This silence was so awkward for them both that Stepan Arkadyich’s lips twitched painfully while he was silent, not taking his eyes off Karenin’s face.

“That is what I wanted to tell her,” said Alexei Alexandrovich, turning away.

“Yes, yes…” said Stepan Arkadyich, unable to answer, as tears were rising in his throat. “Yes, yes. I understand you,” he brought out at last.

“I wish to know what she wants,” said Alexei Alexandrovich.

“I am afraid she does not understand her position herself. She is no judge,” said Stepan Arkadyich, recovering himself. “She is crushed, simply crushed by your magnanimity. If she reads this letter she will not have the strength to say anything; she will only hang her head lower.”

“Yes, but what then, in that case? How am I to explain… how am I to learn her wish?”

“If you will allow me to give my opinion, I think it is for you to point out directly the measures you consider necessary to put an end to this position.”

“So you find that it must be ended?” Alexei Alexandrovich interrupted him. “But how?” he added, making an unaccustomed gesture with his hands before his eyes. “I see no possible way out.”

“There is a way out of every position,” said Stepan Arkadyich, getting up and growing animated. “There was a time when you wanted to break off… If you are convinced now that you cannot make each other happy…”

“Happiness may be understood in different ways. But suppose I agree to everything, I want nothing. What way out is there from our position?”

“If you want to know my opinion,” said Stepan Arkadyich, with the same softening, almond-tender smile with which he had spoken to Anna. The kindly smile was so persuasive that Alexei Alexandrovich, feeling his weakness and submitting to it, was involuntarily ready to believe what Stepan Arkadyich would say. “She will never say it. But one thing is possible, one thing she may wish for,” Stepan Arkadyich went on: “it is the ending of your relations and of all the memories connected with them. In my opinion, in your position a clarification of new mutual relations is necessary. And these relations can be established only by the freedom of both sides.”

“Divorce,” Alexei Alexandrovich interrupted with disgust.

“Yes, I suppose, divorce. Yes, divorce,” Stepan Arkadyich repeated, blushing. “It is in every respect the most reasonable way out for a husband and wife whose relations are like yours. What can be done if a husband and wife have found that life together is impossible for them? That can always happen.” Alexei Alexandrovich sighed heavily and closed his eyes. “There is only one consideration here: does one of the two wish to marry again? If not, then it is very simple,” said Stepan Arkadyich, freeing himself more and more from his constraint.

Alexei Alexandrovich, his face wrinkled with emotion, muttered something to himself and made no answer. Everything that seemed so very simple to Stepan Arkadyich, Alexei Alexandrovich had thought over thousands and thousands of times. And all of it seemed to him not only far from very simple, but completely impossible. A divorce, the details of which he already knew, now seemed impossible to him, because his sense of his own dignity and his respect for religion did not allow him to take on himself a charge of fictitious adultery, and still less to allow his wife, forgiven and loved by him, to be exposed and disgraced. Divorce seemed impossible for other, still more important reasons too.

What would become of his son in case of a divorce? To leave him with his mother was impossible. The divorced mother would have her own illegitimate family, in which the position of a stepson and his upbringing would in all probability be bad. To keep him with himself? He knew that would be revenge on his part, and he did not want that. But besides this, divorce seemed most impossible of all to Alexei Alexandrovich because by agreeing to a divorce he would be ruining Anna. A word that Darya Alexandrovna had said in Moscow had sunk into his soul: that in deciding on a divorce he was thinking of himself, and not thinking that by this he would ruin her irrevocably. And, connecting this word with his forgiveness and his attachment to the children, he now understood it in his own way. To agree to a divorce, to give her her freedom, meant in his understanding to deprive himself of the last tie with the life of the children he loved, and her of her last support on the path of goodness, and to cast her into ruin. If she were a divorced wife, he knew that she would join her life with Vronsky’s, and the union would be illegitimate and criminal, because for a wife, by the meaning of the church’s law, there can be no marriage while her husband is alive. “She will join him, and in a year or two either he will abandon her or she will enter into a new liaison,” thought Alexei Alexandrovich. “And I, by agreeing to an unlawful divorce, shall be the cause of her ruin.” He had thought all this over hundreds of times and was convinced that the business of a divorce was not only not very simple, as his brother-in-law said, but completely impossible. He did not believe a single word of Stepan Arkadyich’s, had thousands of refutations for every word, but he listened to him, feeling that in his words was expressed that powerful coarse force which guided his life and to which he would have to submit.

“The only question is how, on what terms, you will agree to a divorce. She wants nothing, she dares not ask; she leaves everything to your magnanimity.”

“My God! My God! What for?” thought Alexei Alexandrovich, remembering the details of a divorce in which the husband took the blame on himself, and, with the same gesture with which Vronsky had covered himself, he covered his face with his hands in shame.

“You are upset, I understand. But if you think it over…”

“And whoever strikes thee on the right cheek, turn to him the other also; and if any man take away thy coat, let him have thy shirt also,” thought Alexei Alexandrovich.

“Yes, yes!” he cried in a shrill voice. “I take the disgrace on myself, I give up even my son, but… but wouldn’t it be better to leave it? However, do what you like…”

And, turning away from his brother-in-law so that he could not see him, he sat down on a chair by the window. He felt bitter, he felt ashamed; but together with this bitterness and shame he felt joy and tenderness before the height of his own humility.

Stepan Arkadyich was touched. He was silent for a while.

“Alexei, believe me, she will appreciate your magnanimity,” he said. “But it seems it was God’s will,” he added, and, having said it, felt that it was stupid, and could hardly keep from smiling at his own stupidity.

Alexei Alexandrovich wanted to answer something, but tears stopped him.

“This is a fatal misfortune, and one must accept it. I accept this misfortune as an accomplished fact, and I am trying to help you and her,” said Stepan Arkadyich.

When Stepan Arkadyich came out of his brother-in-law’s room he was touched, but that did not prevent him from being pleased that he had carried this business through successfully, since he was sure that Alexei Alexandrovich would not go back on his words. With this pleasure there was mixed another: the idea had come to him that, when the business was done, he would put the question to his wife and his close acquaintances: “What is the difference between me and the Emperor? The Emperor grants a divorce, and no one is the better for it; I have arranged a divorce, and three people are the better for it… Or: what is the likeness between me and the Emperor? When… However, I’ll think of a better one,” he said to himself with a smile.
