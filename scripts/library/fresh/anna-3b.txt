## Chapter XV

When the evening was over, Kitty told her mother about her conversation with Levin, and, in spite of all the pity she felt for Levin, the thought that she had received a proposal made her glad. She had no doubt that she had acted as she ought. But in bed she could not get to sleep for a long time. One impression pursued her relentlessly. It was Levin’s face, with his brows drawn together and his kind eyes looking out from under them in gloomy dejection, as he stood listening to her father and glancing at her and at Vronsky. And she felt so sorry for him that tears came into her eyes. But at once she thought of the man for whom she had given him up. She vividly remembered that manly, firm face, that noble calm, and the kindness to everyone that shone in everything; she remembered the love for her of the man she loved, and again her heart was glad, and she lay back on the pillow with a smile of happiness. “It’s a pity, a pity; but what can I do? It is not my fault,” she said to herself; but an inner voice told her something else. Whether she repented of having led Levin on, or of having refused him, she did not know. But her happiness was poisoned by doubts. “Lord have mercy, Lord have mercy, Lord have mercy!” she said to herself, until she fell asleep.

Meanwhile, downstairs, in the prince’s little study, one of those scenes was taking place that so often recur between parents over a favourite daughter.

“What? I’ll tell you what!” shouted the prince, waving his arms and at once wrapping his squirrel-lined dressing-gown round him again. “That you have no pride, no dignity, that you are disgracing, ruining our daughter with this matchmaking, this mean, idiotic matchmaking!”

“But, for the love of God, Prince, what have I done?” said the princess, almost in tears.

Happy and pleased after her talk with her daughter, she had come to the prince to say good night as usual, and, though she had not meant to tell him about Levin’s proposal and Kitty’s refusal, she had hinted to her husband that the business with Vronsky seemed to her quite settled, that it would be decided as soon as his mother arrived. And it was then, at these words, that the prince suddenly flared up and began shouting unseemly words.

“What have you done? This is what: first, you are luring a suitor, and all Moscow will talk, and rightly. If you give evening parties, invite everybody, not chosen little suitors. Invite all those young puppies” (so the prince called the young men of Moscow), “hire a pianist, and let them dance; not the way it was tonight, little suitors, and throwing them together. It disgusts me to see it, disgusts me; and you have got what you wanted, you have turned the girl’s head. Levin is a thousand times the better man. And as for that Petersburg dandy, they’re made by machine, they’re all cut to one pattern, and all trash. And even if he were a prince of the blood, my daughter has no need of anyone.”

“But what have I done?”

“Why, this…” the prince cried angrily.

“I know that if one listened to you,” the princess interrupted, “we should never marry our daughter at all. In that case we had better go off to the country.”

“And better we should.”

“But wait. Am I currying favour? I am not currying favour in the least. But a young man, and a very nice one, has fallen in love, and she, it seems…”

“Yes, it seems to you! And what if she really does fall in love, and he no more thinks of marrying than I do?… Oh! I wish my eyes had never seen it!… ‘Ah, spiritualism, ah, Nice, ah, at the ball…’” And the prince, imagining that he was imitating his wife, bobbed a curtsey at every word. “And when we have made Katenka unhappy, when she really takes it into her head…”

“But why do you think so?”

“I don’t think it, I know it; we have eyes for these things, not you women. I see a man who has serious intentions, and that’s Levin; and I see a peacock, like this featherhead, who only wants to amuse himself.”

“Well, once you take a thing into your head…”

“You’ll remember, but too late, as with Dashenka.”

“Well, all right, all right, let’s not talk about it,” the princess stopped him, remembering unhappy Dolly.

“Very well, and good night!”

And, making the sign of the cross over each other and kissing, but feeling that each remained of the same opinion, husband and wife parted.

At first the princess had been firmly convinced that this evening had decided Kitty’s fate and that there could be no doubt of Vronsky’s intentions; but her husband’s words troubled her. And, going back to her room, she too, just like Kitty, in terror before the unknown future, repeated several times in her heart: “Lord have mercy, Lord have mercy, Lord have mercy!”

## Chapter XVI

Vronsky had never known family life. His mother had been, in her youth, a brilliant society woman, who during her marriage, and especially afterwards, had many love affairs known to the whole of society. His father he barely remembered, and he had been brought up in the Corps of Pages.

Leaving school very young, a brilliant officer, he at once fell into the rut of the rich Petersburg military. Though he went now and then into Petersburg society, all his love interests lay outside it.

In Moscow, for the first time, after the luxurious and coarse life of Petersburg, he experienced the charm of intimacy with a sweet and innocent girl of good society, who fell in love with him. It never even entered his head that there could be anything wrong in his relations with Kitty. At balls he danced chiefly with her; he visited the house. He talked to her about what people usually talk about in society, all sorts of nonsense, but nonsense to which he could not help giving a special meaning for her. Although he said nothing to her that he could not have said in front of everyone, he felt that she was becoming more and more dependent on him; and the more he felt it, the more pleasant it was to him, and the more tender his feeling for her became. He did not know that his behaviour towards Kitty had a definite name, that it was luring a girl on without any intention of marrying her, and that such luring is one of the bad actions common among brilliant young men like himself. It seemed to him that he was the first to discover this pleasure, and he enjoyed his discovery.

If he could have heard what her parents were saying that evening, if he could have put himself at the family’s point of view and learned that Kitty would be unhappy if he did not marry her, he would have been very much surprised and would not have believed it. He could not believe that what gave such great and good pleasure to him, and above all to her, could be wrong. Still less could he have believed that he ought to marry.

Marriage had never presented itself to him as a possibility. Not only did he dislike family life, but in a family, and especially in a husband, in accordance with the general view of the bachelor world in which he lived, he saw something alien, hostile, and above all ridiculous. But although Vronsky had no suspicion of what her parents were saying, on leaving the Shcherbatskys’ that evening he felt that the secret spiritual bond that existed between him and Kitty had been strengthened so much that evening that something must be done. But what could and what should be done, he could not think.

“That is what is so delightful,” he thought, as he went home from the Shcherbatskys’, carrying away from them, as always, a pleasant feeling of purity and freshness, which came partly from his not having smoked the whole evening, and with it a new feeling of tenderness at her love for him, “that is what is so delightful, that nothing has been said, either by me or by her, but we understood each other so well in that invisible conversation of looks and tones that today, more clearly than ever, she has told me that she loves me. And how sweetly, simply and, above all, trustingly! I feel better myself, purer. I feel that I have a heart, and that there is a great deal of good in me. Those sweet, loving eyes! When she said: and very much…”

“Well, what of it? Why, nothing. It is good for me, and good for her.” And he began to think where he should finish the evening.

He went over in his imagination the places where he might go. “The club? A game of bezique, champagne with Ignatov? No, I won’t go. The Château des Fleurs; I’ll find Oblonsky there, songs, cancan? No, I’m tired of it. That is just why I love the Shcherbatskys, that I become better myself. I’ll go home.” He went straight to his rooms at Dussot’s hotel, ordered supper, and then, undressing, hardly had time to lay his head on the pillow before he fell into a sound and peaceful sleep, as always.

## Chapter XVII

The next day, at eleven in the morning, Vronsky drove to the station of the Petersburg railway to meet his mother, and the first person he came upon on the steps of the great staircase was Oblonsky, who was expecting his sister by the same train.

“Ah! Your excellency!” cried Oblonsky. “Whom are you meeting?”

“My mother,” answered Vronsky, smiling, as everyone did who met Oblonsky, and shaking his hand, and he went up the stairs with him. “She is due from Petersburg today.”

“And I waited for you till two o’clock. Where did you go after the Shcherbatskys’?”

“Home,” answered Vronsky. “I must confess, I felt so pleasant yesterday after the Shcherbatskys’ that I didn’t want to go anywhere.”

“‘I know a mettled horse by the brand upon his hide, and a youth in love I know by the look in his eyes,’” declaimed Stepan Arkadyich, exactly as he had to Levin before.

Vronsky smiled with an air that said he did not deny it, but at once changed the subject.

“And whom are you meeting?” he asked.

“I? A pretty woman,” said Oblonsky.

“You don’t say!”

“Honi soit qui mal y pense! My sister Anna.”

“Ah, Madame Karenina?” said Vronsky.

“You know her, I expect?”

“I think I do. Or no… Really, I don’t remember,” Vronsky answered absent-mindedly, vaguely picturing to himself at the name Karenina something stiff and dull.

“But Alexei Alexandrovich, my famous brother-in-law, you surely know. The whole world knows him.”

“That is, I know him by reputation and by sight. I know that he is clever, learned, something devout… But you know, that is not my… not in my line,” said Vronsky.

“Yes, he is a very remarkable man; a bit of a conservative, but a splendid man,” observed Stepan Arkadyich, “a splendid man.”

“Well, so much the better for him,” said Vronsky, smiling. “Ah, you’re here,” he said, turning to his mother’s tall old footman, who was standing by the door. “Come in here.”

Lately Vronsky, besides the pleasantness that Stepan Arkadyich had for everyone, had felt attached to him also because in his imagination he was connected with Kitty.

“Well, shall we give a supper for the diva on Sunday?” he said to him, taking his arm with a smile.

“Certainly. I’ll get up a subscription. Ah, did you meet my friend Levin yesterday?” asked Stepan Arkadyich.

“Of course. But he left rather soon.”

“He is a splendid fellow,” Oblonsky went on. “Isn’t he?”

“I don’t know,” answered Vronsky, “why it is that in all Muscovites, excepting, of course, those I am talking to,” he put in jokingly, “there is something sharp. They keep rearing up, getting angry, as though they all wanted to make you feel something…”

“There is that, it’s true, there is…” said Stepan Arkadyich, laughing merrily.

“Will it be long?” said Vronsky, turning to an attendant.

“The train has left the last station,” answered the man.

The approach of the train was more and more marked by the stir of preparations at the station, the running of porters, the appearance of gendarmes and attendants, and the arrival of people coming to meet it. Through the frosty steam one could see workmen in short sheepskin coats and soft felt boots crossing the rails of the curving lines. One could hear the whistle of an engine on the distant rails and the shifting of something heavy.

“No,” said Stepan Arkadyich, who very much wanted to tell Vronsky about Levin’s intentions towards Kitty. “No, you have not judged my Levin rightly. He is a very nervous man, and can be unpleasant, it’s true; but then sometimes he can be very nice. His is such an honest, truthful nature, and a heart of gold. But yesterday there were special reasons,” Stepan Arkadyich went on with a significant smile, quite forgetting the sincere sympathy he had felt for his friend the day before, and now feeling the same sympathy, only for Vronsky. “Yes, there was a reason why he might be either especially happy or especially unhappy.”

Vronsky stopped and asked directly:

“Meaning what? Or did he propose to your belle-sœur yesterday?…”

“Perhaps,” said Stepan Arkadyich. “Something of the sort struck me yesterday. And if he left early and was out of sorts as well, then it’s so… He has been in love so long, and I am very sorry for him.”

“So that’s it!… I think, though, that she can count on a better match,” said Vronsky, and, straightening his chest, he began walking again. “However, I don’t know him,” he added. “Yes, it’s a painful position! That is why most men prefer to have to do with Claras. There, failure only proves that you didn’t have enough money; but here your dignity is on the scales. But here is the train.”

And indeed the engine was already whistling in the distance. A few minutes later the platform trembled, and, puffing steam that the frost beat down to the ground, the engine rolled in, with the lever of its middle wheel slowly and rhythmically bending and stretching, and with the engine-driver, muffled up and covered in frost, bowing as it moved; and behind the tender, more and more slowly and shaking the platform more and more, came the luggage van with a dog whining in it; and at last, quivering before they stopped, the passenger carriages drew in.

A dashing guard, blowing his whistle as he went, jumped off, and after him the impatient passengers began getting down one by one: an officer of the Guards, holding himself straight and looking sternly about him; a fidgety little merchant with a bag, smiling cheerfully; a peasant with a sack over his shoulder.

Vronsky, standing beside Oblonsky, looked at the carriages and the people coming out, and quite forgot his mother. What he had just learned about Kitty excited and delighted him. His chest straightened in spite of him, and his eyes shone. He felt himself a conqueror.

“Countess Vronskaya is in this compartment,” said the dashing guard, coming up to Vronsky.

The guard’s words woke him and made him remember his mother and the meeting with her that lay ahead. In his soul he did not respect his mother and, without admitting it to himself, did not love her; though, by the notions of the circle he lived in, and by his upbringing, he could not imagine any relation to his mother other than one in the highest degree submissive and respectful, and the more outwardly submissive and respectful, the less he respected and loved her in his soul.

## Chapter XVIII

Vronsky followed the guard to the carriage and, at the entrance to the compartment, stopped to make way for a lady who was coming out. With the practised tact of a man of the world, from one glance at this lady’s appearance Vronsky placed her as belonging to the highest society. He begged her pardon and was going on into the carriage, but felt a need to look at her once more: not because she was very beautiful, nor because of the elegance and modest grace that could be seen in her whole figure, but because in the expression of her charming face, as she passed him, there was something especially warm and tender. When he looked round, she too turned her head. Her shining grey eyes, which seemed dark because of their thick lashes, rested in a friendly, attentive way on his face, as if she recognized him, and at once moved to the approaching crowd, as though looking for someone. In that short glance Vronsky had time to notice the restrained animation that played in her face and flitted between her shining eyes and the barely perceptible smile that curved her red lips. It was as though an excess of something so filled her whole being that it showed itself against her will, now in the brightness of her glance, now in her smile. She deliberately put out the light in her eyes, but it shone against her will in the barely perceptible smile.

Vronsky went into the carriage. His mother, a dry old lady with black eyes and ringlets, screwed up her eyes, peering at her son, and smiled slightly with her thin lips. Getting up from the little seat and handing her bag to her maid, she gave her son her small, dry hand and, raising his head from it, kissed him on the face.

“You got my telegram? You’re well? Thank God.”

“Did you have a good journey?” said her son, sitting down beside her and involuntarily listening to a woman’s voice outside the door. He knew it was the voice of the lady he had met at the entrance.

“All the same, I don’t agree with you,” the lady’s voice was saying.

“That’s the Petersburg view, madam.”

“Not the Petersburg view, simply a woman’s,” she answered.

“Well, allow me to kiss your hand.”

“Goodbye, Ivan Petrovich. And do look and see whether my brother is here, and send him to me,” said the lady at the very door, and she came back into the compartment.

“Well, have you found your brother?” said Countess Vronskaya, turning to the lady.

Vronsky remembered now that this was Madame Karenina.

“Your brother is here,” he said, getting up. “Forgive me, I didn’t recognize you; and our acquaintance was so short,” said Vronsky, bowing, “that you surely don’t remember me.”

“Oh, no,” she said, “I would have known you, because your mother and I, I believe, talked of nothing but you the whole way,” she said, allowing at last the animation that was asking to come out to show itself in a smile. “And still my brother isn’t here.”

“Do call him, Alyosha,” said the old countess.

Vronsky went out onto the platform and shouted:

“Oblonsky! Here!”

But Madame Karenina did not wait for her brother; seeing him, she stepped out of the carriage with a light, decided step. And as soon as her brother came up to her, with a movement that struck Vronsky by its decision and grace, she threw her left arm round her brother’s neck, drew him quickly to her and kissed him warmly. Vronsky, not taking his eyes off her, watched and smiled, without knowing why himself. But, remembering that his mother was waiting for him, he went back into the carriage.

“She’s very sweet, isn’t she?” said the countess of Madame Karenina. “Her husband put her in with me, and I was very glad. We talked the whole way. Well, and you, they say… vous filez le parfait amour. Tant mieux, mon cher, tant mieux.”

“I don’t know what you are hinting at, maman,” her son answered coldly. “Well, maman, let us go.”

Madame Karenina came into the carriage again to say goodbye to the countess.

“Well, Countess, you have met your son, and I my brother,” she said gaily. “And all my stories were exhausted; I should have had nothing more to tell.”

“Oh no, my dear,” said the countess, taking her hand, “I could go round the world with you and not be bored. You are one of those sweet women with whom it is pleasant both to talk and to be silent. And please don’t think about your son: one can’t never be parted.”

Madame Karenina stood motionless, holding herself extremely straight, and her eyes were smiling.

“Anna Arkadyevna,” said the countess, explaining to her son, “has a little son of eight, I believe, and she has never been parted from him, and she keeps tormenting herself for having left him.”

“Yes, the countess and I talked all the time, I about my son and she about hers,” said Madame Karenina, and again a smile lit up her face, a warm smile meant for him.

“That must have bored you very much,” he said, catching at once, on the wing, this ball of coquetry that she had thrown him. But she evidently did not want to go on talking in that tone, and she turned to the old countess.

“Thank you so much. I hardly noticed how I spent yesterday. Goodbye, Countess.”

“Goodbye, my dear,” answered the countess. “Let me kiss your pretty little face. I’ll say it straight out, simply, like an old woman: I have grown fond of you.”

Stock phrase as it was, Madame Karenina evidently believed it from her heart and was glad of it. She blushed, bent down slightly, offered her face to the countess’s lips, straightened up again and, with the same smile hovering between her lips and her eyes, gave her hand to Vronsky. He pressed the little hand she gave him and was glad, as of something special, of the energetic pressure with which she firmly and boldly shook his hand. She went out with a quick step that carried her rather full body so strangely lightly.

“Very sweet,” said the old lady.

Her son was thinking the same. He followed her with his eyes until her graceful figure disappeared, and the smile stayed on his face. Through the window he saw her go up to her brother, put her hand on his arm and begin telling him something animatedly, evidently about something that had nothing to do with him, Vronsky; and this seemed to him annoying.

“Well, maman, you are perfectly well?” he repeated, turning to his mother.

“Everything is fine, splendid. Alexandre was very sweet. And Marie has grown very pretty. She is very interesting.”

And again she began telling what interested her most, about the christening of her grandson, for which she had gone to Petersburg, and about the Emperor’s special favour to her elder son.

“Here is Lavrenty,” said Vronsky, looking out of the window. “Now let us go, if you like.”

The old butler who was travelling with the countess appeared in the carriage to say that everything was ready, and the countess got up to go.

“Come along, there are few people now,” said Vronsky.

The maid took the bag and the little dog, the butler and a porter the other bags. Vronsky gave his mother his arm; but just as they were coming out of the carriage, several people with frightened faces suddenly ran past. The station-master ran past too, in his cap of an unusual colour. Evidently something unusual had happened. People were running back from the train.

“What?… What?… Where?… Threw himself!… Crushed!…” could be heard among the passers-by.

Stepan Arkadyich, with his sister on his arm, came back, with frightened faces too, and stopped at the entrance to the carriage, avoiding the crowd.

The ladies went into the carriage, while Vronsky and Stepan Arkadyich followed the crowd to find out the details of the accident.

A watchman, whether he was drunk or too muffled up against the hard frost, had not heard a train being shunted backwards, and he had been crushed.

Even before Vronsky and Oblonsky came back, the ladies had learned these details from the butler.

Oblonsky and Vronsky had both seen the mangled body. Oblonsky was evidently suffering. He was wincing and seemed ready to cry.

“Ah, how horrible! Ah, Anna, if you had seen it! Ah, how horrible!” he kept saying.

Vronsky was silent, and his handsome face was serious but perfectly calm.

“Ah, if you had seen it, Countess,” said Stepan Arkadyich. “And his wife was there… It was dreadful to see her… She threw herself on the body. They say he alone supported an enormous family. How horrible!”

“Can’t something be done for her?” said Madame Karenina in an agitated whisper.

Vronsky looked at her and at once went out of the carriage.

“I’ll be back directly, maman,” he added, turning round in the doorway.

When he came back a few minutes later, Stepan Arkadyich was already talking to the countess about the new singer, and the countess was looking impatiently towards the door, waiting for her son.

“Now let us go,” said Vronsky, coming in. They went out together. Vronsky walked in front with his mother. Behind came Madame Karenina with her brother. At the exit the station-master, who had overtaken him, came up to Vronsky.

“You gave my assistant two hundred roubles. Would you kindly say whom you intend them for?”

“For the widow,” said Vronsky, shrugging his shoulders. “I don’t see what there is to ask.”

“You gave that?” Oblonsky called out behind him, and, pressing his sister’s arm, he added: “Very nice, very nice! Isn’t he a splendid fellow? My respects, Countess.”

And he and his sister stopped, looking for her maid.

When they came out, the Vronskys’ carriage had already driven off. The people coming out were still talking about what had happened.

“What a horrible death!” said some gentleman, passing by. “They say he was cut in two.”

“On the contrary, I think it’s the easiest, it’s instantaneous,” observed another.

“How is it they don’t take measures?” said a third.

Madame Karenina got into the carriage, and Stepan Arkadyich saw with surprise that her lips were trembling and that she could hardly hold back her tears.

“What is the matter, Anna?” he asked, when they had driven a few hundred yards.

“It is a bad omen,” she said.

“What nonsense!” said Stepan Arkadyich. “You have come, that’s the main thing. You can’t imagine how much I am counting on you.”

“And have you known Vronsky long?” she asked.

“Yes. You know, we are hoping he will marry Kitty.”

“Yes?” said Anna quietly. “Well, now let us talk about you,” she added, shaking her head as if she wanted physically to drive away something superfluous that was in her way. “Let us talk about your affairs. I got your letter, and here I am.”

“Yes, all my hope is in you,” said Stepan Arkadyich.

“Well, tell me everything.”

And Stepan Arkadyich began to tell her.

When they reached the house, Oblonsky helped his sister out, sighed, pressed her hand, and drove off to his office.
