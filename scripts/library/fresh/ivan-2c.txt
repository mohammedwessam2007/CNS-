## IX

Late at night his wife came home. She came in on tiptoe, but he heard her: he opened his eyes and hurriedly shut them again. She wanted to send Gerasim away and sit with him herself. He opened his eyes and said:

“No. Go.”

“Are you suffering very much?”

“It makes no difference.”

“Take some opium.”

He agreed and drank it. She went out.

Until about three o’clock he was in a tormenting stupor. It seemed to him that he was being pushed, painfully, into a narrow, deep black sack, and pushed further and further in, and could not be pushed through. And this terrible thing was being done to him with suffering. And he was both afraid and wanted to fall through, and struggled, and helped. And then suddenly he broke loose and fell, and came to. Gerasim was still sitting at the foot of the bed, dozing calmly and patiently. And he was lying with his wasted, stockinged legs raised on Gerasim’s shoulders; the same shaded candle, and the same unceasing pain.

“Go away, Gerasim,” he whispered.

“It’s all right, sir, I’ll stay a bit.”

“No. Go away.”

He took his legs down, lay on his side with his arm under his head, and felt sorry for himself. He waited only until Gerasim had gone into the next room, and then he stopped restraining himself and wept like a child. He wept over his helplessness, his terrible loneliness, the cruelty of people, the cruelty of God, the absence of God. “Why have you done all this? Why have you brought me here? Why, why do you torment me so terribly?...” He did not expect an answer, and he wept because there was no answer and could be none. The pain rose again, but he did not stir or call anyone. He said to himself: “Go on, then, strike me! But what for? What have I done to you, what for?”

Then he grew quiet; he stopped not only weeping but breathing, and became all attention, as if he were listening not to a voice speaking in sounds, but to the voice of his soul, to the current of thoughts rising in him.

“What is it you want?” was the first clear idea he heard that could be put into words. “What do you want? What is it you want?” he repeated to himself. “What? Not to suffer. To live,” he answered.

And again he gave himself up to attention so intense that even the pain did not distract him.

“To live? Live how?” asked the voice of his soul.

“Why, to live as I lived before: well and pleasantly.”

“As you lived before, well and pleasantly?” asked the voice. And he began to go over in his imagination the best moments of his pleasant life. But, strange to say, all these best moments of his pleasant life now seemed quite different from what they had seemed then. All of them, except the first memories of childhood. There, in childhood, there had been something really pleasant, something one could live with if it came back. But the person who had felt that pleasure was no more: it was like a memory of someone else.

As soon as the time began that had produced the present he, Ivan Ilyich, all the joys that had seemed joys then now melted before his eyes and turned into something trivial and often disgusting.

And the further from childhood, the nearer to the present, the more trivial and doubtful the joys were. It began with the School of Law. There, there had still been something truly good: there had been gaiety, there had been friendship, there had been hopes. But in the senior classes these good moments were already rarer. Then, during his first service with the governor, good moments appeared again: these were the memories of love for a woman. Then it all became muddled, and there was even less that was good. Further on, less good still, and the further, the less.

His marriage... so accidental, and the disillusionment, and his wife’s bad breath, and sensuality, pretence! And that deadly work, those worries about money; and so for one year, and two, and ten, and twenty: always the same. And the further, the deader. “As if I had been going steadily downhill while imagining I was going uphill. That is just how it was. In public opinion I was going uphill, and exactly as much as I rose, life was slipping away from under me... And now it is done: die!

“Then what is this? What for? It cannot be. It cannot be that life was so senseless and vile. And if it really was so vile and senseless, then why die, and die in agony? Something is wrong.

“Maybe I did not live as I ought to have?” suddenly came into his head. “But how could that be, when I did everything as it should be done?” he said to himself, and at once drove away from himself this, the only solution to the whole riddle of life and death, as something utterly impossible.

“Then what do you want now? To live? Live how? To live as you live in court, when the usher proclaims, ‘The court is in session!’ The court is in session, the court is in session,” he repeated to himself. “Here is the court! But I am not guilty!” he cried out angrily. “What for?” And he stopped weeping and, turning his face to the wall, began to think about one and the same thing: why, what for, all this horror?

But however much he thought, he found no answer. And when the thought came to him, as it often did, that all this was happening because he had not lived as he should, he at once remembered all the correctness of his life and drove this strange thought away.

## X

Another two weeks went by. Ivan Ilyich no longer got up from the sofa. He did not want to lie in bed, and lay on the sofa. And lying almost all the time with his face to the wall, he suffered alone the same unresolving sufferings and thought alone the same unresolving thought. What is this? Can it be true that it is death? And the inner voice answered: yes, it is true. What are these torments for? And the voice answered: for nothing; just so. Beyond this and besides this there was nothing.

From the very beginning of his illness, from the time Ivan Ilyich first went to the doctor, his life had been divided into two opposite moods, which alternated: now despair and the expectation of an incomprehensible and terrible death, now hope and an observation, full of interest, of the activity of his body. Now there was before his eyes only a kidney or an intestine that had temporarily failed in its duties; now there was only an incomprehensible, terrible death, from which there was no escape.

These two moods had alternated from the very beginning of his illness; but the further the illness went, the more doubtful and fantastic the ideas about the kidney became, and the more real the awareness of approaching death.

He had only to remember what he had been three months before and what he was now; to remember how steadily he had gone downhill, for every possibility of hope to collapse.

In the latter part of that loneliness in which he lay with his face to the back of the sofa, that loneliness in the middle of a populous city and of his many acquaintances and his family, a loneliness than which there could be none more complete anywhere, neither at the bottom of the sea nor in the earth: in the latter part of this terrible loneliness Ivan Ilyich lived only in imagination, in the past. One after another, pictures of his past came to him. It always began with what was nearest in time and went back to the most remote, to childhood, and stopped there. If Ivan Ilyich remembered the stewed prunes he had been offered to eat that day, he remembered the raw, wrinkled French prunes of his childhood, their particular taste and the flow of saliva when he got down to the stone; and alongside this memory of a taste arose a whole series of memories from that time: his nanny, his brother, his toys. “I mustn’t think of that... it’s too painful,” Ivan Ilyich would say to himself, and he would carry himself back into the present. The button on the back of the sofa and the creases in the morocco leather. “Morocco is expensive and doesn’t last; there was a quarrel over it. But there was another morocco, and another quarrel, when we tore Father’s briefcase and were punished, and Mama brought us pies.” And again it stopped at childhood, and again it was painful to Ivan Ilyich, and he tried to drive it away and think of something else.

And again, right there, along with this train of memory, another train of memories went on in his soul: of how his illness had grown and increased. There too, the further back, the more life there was. There was more good in life, and more life itself. And the two merged together. “Just as my torments get worse and worse, so my whole life got worse and worse,” he thought. One bright point there, behind, at the beginning of life, and then everything blacker and blacker, and faster and faster. “Inversely proportional to the square of the distance from death,” thought Ivan Ilyich. And this image of a stone falling downwards with increasing speed sank into his soul. Life, a series of increasing sufferings, flies faster and faster towards the end, the most terrible suffering. “I am falling...” He shuddered, stirred, wanted to resist; but he already knew that he could not resist, and again, with eyes weary of looking but unable not to look at what was in front of them, he stared at the back of the sofa and waited: waited for that terrible fall, the shock and destruction. “It is impossible to resist,” he said to himself. “But if only I could understand what it is for! And even that is impossible. It could be explained if I were to say that I had not lived as I ought. But that is impossible to admit,” he said to himself, remembering all the lawfulness, correctness and decency of his life. “That is something I simply cannot admit,” he said to himself, smiling with his lips, as if someone could see that smile and be deceived by it. “There is no explanation! Torment, death... What for?”

## XI

So two weeks went by. During these weeks an event occurred that Ivan Ilyich and his wife had longed for: Petrishchev made a formal proposal. It happened in the evening. The next day Praskovya Fyodorovna went in to her husband, thinking how to tell him of Fyodor Petrovich’s proposal, but that very night a new change for the worse had taken place in Ivan Ilyich. Praskovya Fyodorovna found him on the same sofa, but in a new position. He lay on his back, groaning and staring in front of him with a fixed look.

She began to talk about his medicines. He moved his eyes to her. She did not finish what she had begun: such hatred, of her in particular, was expressed in that look.

“For Christ’s sake, let me die in peace,” he said.

She was about to go, but at that moment their daughter came in and went up to say good morning. He looked at his daughter as he had looked at his wife, and to her questions about his health said drily that he would soon free them all of himself. Both fell silent, sat for a while, and went out.

“What is it our fault?” Liza said to her mother. “As if we had done it! I’m sorry for Papa, but why should he torment us?”

The doctor came at the usual time. Ivan Ilyich answered him “Yes” and “No”, not taking his angry eyes off him, and at the end said:

“You know very well that nothing will help, so leave me alone.”

“We can ease your sufferings,” said the doctor.

“You can’t even do that; leave me alone.”

The doctor went into the drawing room and told Praskovya Fyodorovna that things were very bad and that there was only one remedy, opium, to ease his sufferings, which must be terrible.

The doctor said that his physical sufferings were terrible, and that was true; but more terrible than his physical sufferings were his moral sufferings, and these were his chief torment.

His moral sufferings consisted in this: that night, looking at Gerasim’s sleepy, good-natured face with its high cheekbones, it had suddenly come into his head: “What if my whole life, my conscious life, really has been *not the right thing*?”

It came into his head that what had seemed to him before an utter impossibility, that he had not lived his life as he should have, might be true. It came into his head that those barely noticeable impulses of his to struggle against what the people in the highest positions considered good, those barely noticeable impulses which he had at once driven away from himself, might have been the real thing, and all the rest might have been not the right thing. His work, and the way he had arranged his life, and his family, and these interests of society and work: all this might have been not the right thing. He tried to defend all this to himself. And suddenly he felt all the weakness of what he was defending. And there was nothing to defend.

“And if this is so,” he said to himself, “and I am leaving life with the awareness that I have ruined everything that was given me, and it is impossible to put right, then what?” He lay on his back and began to go over his whole life in an entirely new way. When in the morning he saw the footman, then his wife, then his daughter, then the doctor, every movement they made, every word they said, confirmed for him the terrible truth that had been revealed to him in the night. In them he saw himself, everything he had lived by, and he saw clearly that it was all not the right thing; that it was all a terrible, enormous deception, covering up both life and death. This awareness increased his physical sufferings, multiplied them tenfold. He groaned and tossed about and tugged at his clothes. It seemed to him that they were choking and crushing him. And for that he hated them.

They gave him a large dose of opium, and he lost consciousness; but at dinner time it began again. He drove everyone away and tossed from place to place.

His wife came to him and said:

“Jean, my dear, do this for me” (for me?). “It can’t do any harm, and it often helps. Really, it’s nothing. Even healthy people often...”

He opened his eyes wide.

“What? Take communion? What for? No need! But still...”

She began to cry.

“Yes, my dear? I’ll call our priest, he is so nice.”

“Fine, very good,” he said.

When the priest came and heard his confession, he softened; he felt as if relieved of his doubts, and so of his sufferings, and a moment of hope came over him. He began to think again about the appendix and the possibility of putting it right. He took communion with tears in his eyes.

When they laid him down after communion, he felt easier for a moment, and hope of life appeared again. He began to think about the operation that had been suggested to him. “To live, I want to live,” he said to himself. His wife came in to congratulate him; she said the usual words and added:

“You feel better, don’t you?”

Without looking at her, he said: “Yes.”

Her clothes, her figure, the expression of her face, the sound of her voice: everything said one thing to him: “Not the right thing. Everything you have lived by and live by is a lie, a deception, hiding life and death from you.” And as soon as he thought this, his hatred rose up, and with his hatred his agonising physical sufferings, and with his sufferings the awareness of inevitable, near destruction. Something new happened: something began to screw and to shoot through him and to squeeze his breath.

The expression of his face when he said “Yes” was terrible. Having said that “Yes”, looking her straight in the face, with a quickness extraordinary for his weakness, he turned over face down and shouted:

“Go away, go away, leave me alone!”

## XII

From that moment began the screaming that did not stop for three days, and that was so terrible that even through two doors one could not hear it without horror. At the moment he answered his wife, he understood that he was lost, that there was no return, that the end had come, the very end, and the doubt was still not resolved, but remained a doubt.

“Oh! Ooh! Oh!” he screamed in various tones. He had begun by screaming “I don’t want to!” and went on screaming on the sound “o”.

For all three days, during which time did not exist for him, he struggled in that black sack into which an invisible, invincible force was pushing him. He struggled as a man condemned to death struggles in the hands of the executioner, knowing he cannot save himself; and with every minute he felt that, in spite of all his efforts to struggle, he was coming nearer and nearer to what terrified him. He felt that his torment lay both in his being pushed into that black hole and, still more, in his not being able to get through it. What prevented him from getting through was his conviction that his life had been a good one. It was this justification of his life that held him fast and would not let him go forward, and tormented him most of all.

Suddenly some force struck him in the chest and in the side, squeezed his breath still harder; he fell through the hole, and there, at the end of the hole, something began to shine. What happened to him was what used to happen to him in a railway carriage, when you think you are going forward but are going backward, and suddenly you recognise the real direction.

“Yes, it was all not the right thing,” he said to himself, “but that doesn’t matter. It is possible, possible to do *the right thing*. But what is the right thing?” he asked himself, and suddenly grew quiet.

This was at the end of the third day, an hour before his death. At that very moment the schoolboy crept quietly in to his father and went up to his bed. The dying man was still screaming desperately and throwing his arms about. His hand fell on the schoolboy’s head. The schoolboy seized it, pressed it to his lips, and began to cry.

At that very moment Ivan Ilyich fell through, saw the light, and it was revealed to him that his life had not been what it should have been, but that this could still be put right. He asked himself: “But what is the right thing?” and grew quiet, listening. Then he felt that someone was kissing his hand. He opened his eyes and looked at his son. He felt sorry for him. His wife came up to him. He looked at her. She was looking at him with a despairing expression, her mouth open and tears she had not wiped away on her nose and cheek. He felt sorry for her.

“Yes, I am tormenting them,” he thought. “They are sorry for me, but it will be better for them when I die.” He wanted to say this, but did not have the strength to get it out. “But why speak? I must act,” he thought. With a look he pointed his son out to his wife and said:

“Take him away... sorry for him... and for you...” He wanted to add “Forgive me,” but said “Forgo...” and, no longer having the strength to correct himself, waved his hand, knowing that the one who needed to would understand.

And suddenly it became clear to him that what had been tormenting him and would not come out was suddenly all coming out at once, from two sides, from ten sides, from all sides. He was sorry for them; he must act so that it would not hurt them. Release them, and release himself from these sufferings. “How good and how simple,” he thought. “And the pain?” he asked himself. “Where has it gone? Now then, where are you, pain?”

He began to listen.

“Yes, there it is. Well, let the pain be.”

“And death? Where is it?”

He searched for his old, habitual fear of death and could not find it. Where is it? What death? There was no fear at all, because there was no death either.

Instead of death there was light.

“So that’s it!” he suddenly said aloud. “What joy!”

For him all this happened in a single instant, and the meaning of that instant did not change. But for those present his agony went on for another two hours. Something rattled in his chest; his emaciated body shuddered. Then the rattling and wheezing grew rarer and rarer.

“It is finished!” someone said over him.

He heard these words and repeated them in his soul. “Death is finished,” he said to himself. “It is no more.”

He drew in a breath, stopped in the middle of it, stretched out, and died.
