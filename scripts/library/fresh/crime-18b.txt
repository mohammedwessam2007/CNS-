## CHAPTER III

He was hurrying to Svidrigailov’s. What he could hope for from this man he did not know himself. But in this man lay hidden some kind of power over him. Having once realised this, he could not rest, and now, besides, the time had come.

On the way one question especially tormented him: had Svidrigailov been to Porfiry’s?

As far as he could judge, and he would have sworn to it, no, he had not! He thought again and again, recalled Porfiry’s whole visit, considered: no, he had not, of course he had not!

But if he had not been yet, would he go to Porfiry or not?

For the time being it seemed to him that he would not. Why? He could not have explained that either, but even if he could have, he would not have racked his brains over it much just now. All this tormented him, and at the same time he somehow had no time for it. Strangely, perhaps no one would have believed it, but he cared somehow weakly and absently about his present, immediate fate. Something else tormented him, something far more important, extraordinary, about himself and no one else, but something different, something essential. Besides, he felt a boundless moral weariness, though his reason worked better this morning than in all these last days.

And was it worth it now, after all that had happened, to try to overcome all these new paltry difficulties? Was it worth it, for instance, to try to intrigue so that Svidrigailov would not go to Porfiry; to study, to find out, to waste time on some Svidrigailov!

Oh, how sick he was of all this!

And yet he was still hurrying to Svidrigailov’s; was he not expecting something new from him, some pointers, a way out? People clutch at straws, after all! Was it not fate, or some instinct, bringing them together? Perhaps it was only weariness, despair; perhaps what he needed was not Svidrigailov but someone else, and Svidrigailov had simply turned up. Sonya? But why would he go to Sonya now? To ask for her tears again? And besides, Sonya was frightening to him. Sonya stood for an inexorable sentence, a decision without change. Here it was either her road or his. Especially at this moment he was not able to see her. No, wouldn’t it be better to try Svidrigailov: what was he? And he could not help admitting inwardly that the man had indeed seemed for a long time to be somehow necessary to him for something.

Well, but what could there be in common between them? Even their villainy could not be the same. This man was very unpleasant besides, obviously extremely depraved, certainly cunning and deceitful, perhaps very wicked. Such stories went around about him. It was true that he had busied himself on behalf of Katerina Ivanovna’s children; but who knew for what purpose, and what it meant? This man always had some intentions and projects.

All these days one more thought had constantly flashed through Raskolnikov’s mind and terribly disturbed him, though he even tried to drive it away, so heavy was it for him! He sometimes thought: Svidrigailov had kept hovering round him, and was hovering round him now; Svidrigailov had learned his secret; Svidrigailov had had designs on Dunya. And what if he still had them? It could almost certainly be said that he had. And what if now, having learned his secret and so gained power over him, he wanted to use it as a weapon against Dunya?

This thought sometimes tormented him even in his sleep, but for the first time it came to him as consciously and vividly as now, when he was going to Svidrigailov’s. This thought alone drove him to gloomy fury. In the first place, everything would change then, even in his own position: he would have to reveal his secret to Dunechka at once. He would perhaps have to give himself up, to keep Dunechka from some incautious step. A letter? This morning Dunya had received some letter! From whom in Petersburg could she receive letters? (Luzhin, perhaps?) It was true that Razumikhin was keeping guard there; but Razumikhin knew nothing. Perhaps he ought to reveal himself to Razumikhin too? Raskolnikov thought of it with loathing.

“In any case I must see Svidrigailov as soon as possible,” he decided finally to himself. “Thank God, here it is not so much the details that are needed as the essence of the matter; but if, if only he is capable of it, if Svidrigailov is plotting anything against Dunya, then...”

Raskolnikov was so tired from all this time, from this whole month, that he could no longer resolve such questions except by one decision: “Then I will kill him,” he thought in cold despair. A heavy feeling gripped his heart; he stopped in the middle of the street and began to look round: what road was he taking, and where had he got to? He was on — Prospect, thirty or forty paces from the Haymarket, which he had passed. The whole second floor of the house on the left was taken up by a tavern. All the windows were wide open; the tavern, judging by the figures moving in the windows, was packed full. In the hall singers were pouring out their songs, a clarinet and a violin were ringing, and a Turkish drum was thundering. Women’s shrieks could be heard. He was about to go back, puzzled why he had turned onto — Prospect, when suddenly, at one of the end windows of the tavern, open like the rest, he saw Svidrigailov sitting right by the window at a tea table, with a pipe in his teeth. This struck him terribly, to the point of horror. Svidrigailov was watching and examining him in silence, and, which also struck Raskolnikov at once, seemed to have been about to get up, so as to slip away quietly before he was noticed. Raskolnikov at once pretended that he had not noticed him either and was looking aside, lost in thought, while he went on watching him out of the corner of his eye. His heart was beating anxiously. So it was: Svidrigailov obviously did not want to be seen. He took the pipe from his lips and was about to hide; but, having got up and pushed back his chair, he probably noticed suddenly that Raskolnikov saw him and was watching him. Something like the scene of their first meeting at Raskolnikov’s, during his sleep, took place between them. A roguish smile appeared on Svidrigailov’s face and grew broader and broader. Each knew that both saw and were watching each other. At last Svidrigailov burst out laughing loudly.

“Well, well! come in, then, if you want to; I’m here!” he shouted from the window.

Raskolnikov went up into the tavern.

He found him in a very small back room with one window, adjoining the big hall, where at twenty little tables, to the shouts of a desperate chorus of singers, merchants, officials and all sorts of people were drinking tea. From somewhere came the click of billiard balls. On the little table before Svidrigailov stood an opened bottle of champagne and a glass half full of wine. In the little room there were also an organ-grinder boy with a small hand organ and a healthy, red-cheeked girl in a tucked-up striped skirt and a Tyrolean hat with ribbons, a singer of about eighteen, who, in spite of the choral song in the other room, was singing to the organ-grinder’s accompaniment, in a rather husky contralto, some lackey’s song...

“Well, that’s enough!” Svidrigailov interrupted her as Raskolnikov came in.

The girl broke off at once and stopped in respectful expectation. She had been singing her rhymed lackey’s ditty with a kind of serious and respectful shade in her face too.

“Hey, Philip, a glass!” shouted Svidrigailov.

“I won’t drink any wine,” said Raskolnikov.

“As you like; it’s not for you. Drink, Katya! Nothing more will be needed today; off you go!” He poured her a whole glass of wine and put down a yellow banknote. Katya drank the glass at one go, as women drink wine, that is, without stopping, in twenty swallows, took the note, kissed Svidrigailov’s hand, which he very seriously allowed her to kiss, and went out of the room, and the boy with the organ trailed after her. They had both been brought in from the street. Svidrigailov had not been living in Petersburg even a week, and already everything around him was on a kind of patriarchal footing. The tavern waiter, Philip, was also already an “acquaintance” and was being obsequious. The door to the hall could be locked; Svidrigailov was at home in this room and perhaps spent whole days in it. The tavern was dirty, wretched, and not even middling.

“I was going to see you and looking for you,” Raskolnikov began, “but why did I suddenly turn onto — Prospect from the Haymarket just now! I never turn this way or come here. I turn right from the Haymarket. And this isn’t the way to your place either. I had only just turned, and here you are! That is strange!”

“Why don’t you say straight out: it’s a miracle!”

“Because perhaps it is only chance.”

“What a twist all these people have!” laughed Svidrigailov. “They won’t admit it, even if inside they believe in a miracle! You say yourself that it is ‘perhaps’ only chance. And what little cowards everyone here is about their own opinion, you can’t imagine, Rodion Romanych! I don’t mean you. You have an opinion of your own and were not afraid to have it. That is how you caught my curiosity.”

“Nothing else?”

“Why, that is enough.”

Svidrigailov was obviously in an excited state, but only a tiny bit; he had drunk only half a glass of wine.

“It seems to me that you came to see me before you knew that I was capable of having what you call an opinion of my own,” Raskolnikov remarked.

“Well, then it was a different matter. Everyone has his own steps. And as for the miracle, I will tell you that you seem to have slept through these last two or three days. I named this tavern to you myself, and there was no miracle in your coming straight here; I explained the whole way to you myself, told you the place where it stands and the hours when I can be found here. Do you remember?”

“I forgot,” answered Raskolnikov in surprise.

“I believe you. I told you twice. The address was stamped on your memory mechanically. And you turned here mechanically, and yet strictly according to the address, without knowing it yourself. Even as I told you, I didn’t expect that you had understood me. You give yourself away far too much, Rodion Romanych. And here is something else: I am convinced that in Petersburg many people talk to themselves as they walk. It is a city of half-mad people. If we had sciences, then doctors, lawyers and philosophers could make most precious studies of Petersburg, each in his own field. Rarely will you find so many gloomy, harsh and strange influences on the soul of man as in Petersburg. The climatic influences alone are worth something! And yet it is the administrative centre of all Russia, and its character must be reflected in everything. But that is not the point now; the point is that I have several times watched you from the side. You come out of the house holding your head up still. After twenty paces you are already lowering it, folding your hands behind your back. You look, and obviously see nothing either before you or at the sides any more. At last you begin moving your lips and talking to yourself, and sometimes you free a hand and declaim, and at last you stop in the middle of the road for a long time. That is very bad, sir. Perhaps someone besides me notices you, and that is not to your advantage. It is all the same to me, essentially, and I won’t cure you, but of course you understand me.”

“And do you know that I am being followed?” asked Raskolnikov, glancing at him searchingly.

“No, I know nothing,” Svidrigailov answered, as if surprised.

“Well, then let us leave me in peace,” Raskolnikov muttered, frowning.

“Very well, let us leave you in peace.”

“Tell me instead: if you come here to drink, and twice named this place yourself for me to come and see you here, why, when I looked up at the window from the street just now, were you hiding and wanting to leave? I noticed that very well.”

“He-he! And why, when I was standing on your threshold that time, were you lying on your sofa with your eyes closed, pretending to be asleep, when you were not asleep at all? I noticed that very well.”

“I may have had... reasons... you know them yourself.”

“And I may have had my reasons, though you won’t learn them.”

Raskolnikov rested his right elbow on the table, propped his chin from below with the fingers of his right hand and stared intently at Svidrigailov. For a minute he examined his face, which had always struck him before too. It was a strange face, as if like a mask: white, ruddy, with ruddy, scarlet lips, with a light fair beard and rather thick fair hair still. The eyes were somehow too blue, and their look somehow too heavy and fixed. There was something terribly unpleasant in this handsome face, which was extremely youthful for his years. Svidrigailov’s clothes were smart, summery, light; he was especially smart in his linen. On his finger was a huge ring with a precious stone.

“Must I really bother with you too?” Raskolnikov said suddenly, coming straight out into the open with convulsive impatience. “Though perhaps you are the most dangerous of men, if you wish to do harm, I don’t want to break myself any more. I will show you right now that I don’t value myself as much as you probably think. Know, then, that I have come to tell you straight out that if you keep your former intention regarding my sister, and if for that purpose you think of making use of anything that has been discovered lately, I will kill you before you get me put in prison. My word is sure: you know that I can keep it. Second, if you want to declare anything to me (because all this time it has seemed to me that you wanted to tell me something), then declare it quickly, because time is precious, and perhaps very soon it will be too late.”

“But where are you hurrying to like this?” asked Svidrigailov, examining him curiously.

“Everyone has his own steps,” Raskolnikov said gloomily and impatiently.

“You invited frankness yourself just now, and at the very first question you refuse to answer,” Svidrigailov remarked with a smile. “You keep thinking I have some purposes, and so you look at me suspiciously. Well, that is perfectly understandable in your position. But however much I wish to become close to you, I still won’t take the trouble to persuade you otherwise. By God, the game isn’t worth the candle, and I didn’t intend to talk to you about anything so very special.”

“Then why did you need me so much? You were hovering round me, weren’t you?”

“Why, simply as a curious subject for observation. I liked you for the fantastic nature of your position, that’s what! Besides, you are the brother of a person who interested me very much, and finally, from that very person, at one time, I heard a terrible lot about you, and often, from which I concluded that you have great influence over her; isn’t that enough? He-he-he! However, I admit that your question is very complex for me, and it is hard for me to answer it. Well, for example, you came to me now not only on business but for something new, didn’t you? Isn’t that so? Isn’t that so?” Svidrigailov insisted with a roguish smile. “Well, imagine after that that I myself, while still on my way here, in the train, was counting on you, that you too would tell me something new, and that I would manage to borrow something from you! That’s how rich we are!”

“Borrow what?”

“What shall I tell you? How do I know what? You see what a wretched little tavern I sit in all the time, and I enjoy it; that is, not exactly enjoy it, but one has to sit somewhere. Well, take that poor Katya; you saw her?.. Well, if I were, for example, at least a glutton, a club gourmet, but look what I can eat!” (He jabbed a finger at the corner, where on a little table, on a tin plate, stood the remains of a dreadful beefsteak with potatoes.) “By the way, have you dined? I had a snack and want no more. Wine, for example, I don’t drink at all. Nothing but champagne, and even of champagne only one glass a whole evening, and even that gives me a headache. I ordered it now to wind myself up, because I am going somewhere, and you see me in a special frame of mind. That is why I hid earlier like a schoolboy, because I thought you would hinder me; but it seems” (he took out his watch) “I can stay with you an hour; it is half past four now. Would you believe it, if only there were something; well, to be a landowner, well, a father, well, an uhlan, a photographer, a journalist... n-nothing, no speciality! Sometimes it’s even boring. Really, I thought you would tell me something new.”

“But who are you, and why have you come here?”

“Who am I? You know: a nobleman; I served two years in the cavalry, then just loafed about here in Petersburg, then married Marfa Petrovna and lived in the country. There is my biography!”

“You are a gambler, I believe?”

“No, what kind of gambler am I. A card sharper is not a gambler.”

“So you were a card sharper?”

“Yes, I was a card sharper.”

“Well, and did you get beaten?”

“It happened. Why?”

“Well, then you could have challenged someone to a duel... and in general it livens one up.”

“I won’t contradict you, and besides, I am no master at philosophising. I confess to you that I came here sooner mostly on account of women.”

“When you had only just buried Marfa Petrovna?”

“Well, yes,” Svidrigailov smiled with disarming frankness. “What of it? You seem to find something wrong in my talking about women like this?”

“That is, do I find anything wrong in depravity, or not?”

“In depravity! Well, so that’s where you’re going! But anyway, in order: first I will answer you about women in general; you know, I am inclined to chatter. Tell me, why should I restrain myself? Why give up women, if I am fond of them at least? At least it is an occupation.”

“So that is all you hope for here, depravity?”

“Well, what of it, depravity then! They’ve got so attached to depravity. But I do like a direct question, at least. In this depravity there is at least something constant, even founded in nature and not subject to fancy, something that stays in the blood like an ever-burning coal, always kindling, which you won’t put out for a long time yet, perhaps not so soon even with the years. You will agree yourself, isn’t it an occupation of its kind?”

“What is there to rejoice in? It is a disease, and a dangerous one.”

“Ah, so that’s where you’re going! I agree that it is a disease, like everything that goes beyond measure (and here one is bound to go beyond measure), but in the first place it is one way with one man and another way with another, and in the second, of course, keep measure in everything, calculation, even a base one, but what is to be done? If it weren’t for this, one would have to shoot oneself, perhaps. I agree that a decent man is obliged to be bored, but still...”

“And could you shoot yourself?”

“Come now!” Svidrigailov parried with disgust. “Do me the favour of not talking about that,” he added hastily, and even without any of the bravado that showed in all his previous words. Even his face seemed to change. “I confess to an unforgivable weakness, but what is to be done: I am afraid of death and don’t like it when people talk about it. Do you know that I am partly a mystic?”

“Ah! Marfa Petrovna’s ghosts! Well, do they keep coming?”

“Never mind them, don’t mention them; there haven’t been any yet in Petersburg; and the devil take them!” he cried with a kind of irritable look. “No, let us rather talk about... though, however... Hm! Eh, there is little time, I can’t stay with you long, and that’s a pity! There would be something to tell.”

“And what is it you have, a woman?”

“Yes, a woman, just one unexpected incident... no, that’s not what I mean.”

“Well, and the vileness of all these surroundings no longer affects you? You have already lost the strength to stop?”

“So you lay claim to strength too? He-he-he! You surprised me just now, Rodion Romanych, though I knew beforehand that it would be so. You talk to me about depravity and aesthetics! You are a Schiller, you are an idealist! All this, of course, is as it should be, and one would have to be surprised if it were otherwise, but still, somehow it is strange in reality... Ah, it’s a pity there is so little time, because you yourself are a most curious subject! And by the way, do you like Schiller? I am terribly fond of him.”

“But what a braggart you are, though!” said Raskolnikov with some disgust.

“Well, by God, no!” answered Svidrigailov, laughing. “But then, I won’t argue; let me be a braggart; but why not brag a little, when it is harmless? I lived seven years in the country at Marfa Petrovna’s, and so, falling now on an intelligent man like you, intelligent and in the highest degree curious, I am simply glad to chat; besides, I have drunk this half glass of wine and it has gone to my head a tiny bit. And above all, there is one circumstance which has wound me up very much, but about which I... will keep silent. Where are you going?” Svidrigailov asked suddenly, in alarm.

Raskolnikov had begun to get up. He felt heavy, and stifled, and somehow awkward at having come here. He had become convinced that Svidrigailov was the emptiest and most worthless villain in the world.

“E-eh! Sit down, stay,” Svidrigailov begged. “And order yourself some tea at least. Well, sit down; well, I won’t talk nonsense, about myself, that is. I’ll tell you something. Well, would you like me to tell you how a woman, to use your style, tried to ‘save’ me? It will even be an answer to your first question, because that person is your sister. May I tell it? And we’ll kill time.”

“Tell it, but I hope that you...”

“Oh, don’t worry! Besides, Avdotya Romanovna can inspire nothing but the deepest respect even in such a nasty and empty man as I am.”
