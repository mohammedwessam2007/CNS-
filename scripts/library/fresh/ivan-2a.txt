## V

So a month went by, and two. Before the New Year his brother-in-law came to their town and stayed with them. Ivan Ilyich was at the court. Praskovya Fyodorovna had gone shopping. Going into his study, he found his brother-in-law there, a healthy, ruddy man, unpacking his suitcase himself. He raised his head at the sound of Ivan Ilyich’s steps and looked at him for a second in silence. That look revealed everything to Ivan Ilyich. His brother-in-law opened his mouth to gasp and stopped himself. That movement confirmed everything.

“Well, have I changed?”

“Yes... there is a change.”

And however much Ivan Ilyich steered the conversation afterwards towards his appearance, his brother-in-law said nothing. Praskovya Fyodorovna came home, and his brother-in-law went to her. Ivan Ilyich locked the door and began to look at himself in the mirror: from the front, then from the side. He took his photograph with his wife and compared it with what he saw in the mirror. The change was enormous. Then he bared his arms to the elbow, looked at them, pulled down his sleeves, sat down on the ottoman, and grew blacker than night.

“Don’t, don’t,” he said to himself; he jumped up, went to the desk, opened a case file and began to read it, but could not. He unlocked the door and went into the reception room. The door to the drawing room was shut. He went up to it on tiptoe and began to listen.

“No, you’re exaggerating,” Praskovya Fyodorovna was saying.

“Exaggerating? You can’t see it: he’s a dead man, look at his eyes. There’s no light in them. But what is wrong with him?”

“Nobody knows. Nikolayev” (that was another doctor) “said something, but I don’t know. Leshchetitsky” (that was the famous doctor) “said the opposite...”

Ivan Ilyich went away, went to his room, lay down and began to think: “The kidney, a floating kidney.” He remembered everything the doctors had told him, how it had come loose and how it floated. And by an effort of imagination he tried to catch this kidney and stop it, fasten it in place: so little was needed, it seemed to him. “No, I’ll go and see Pyotr Ivanovich again.” (That was the friend who had a friend who was a doctor.) He rang, ordered the horse to be harnessed, and got ready to go.

“Where are you going, Jean?” asked his wife, with a particularly sad and unusually kind expression.

This unusual kindness made him angry. He looked at her gloomily.

“I have to see Pyotr Ivanovich.”

He drove to his friend who had a friend who was a doctor. And with him to the doctor. He found him in and talked with him for a long time.

Going through the anatomical and physiological details of what, in the doctor’s opinion, was happening inside him, he understood everything.

There was a little something, a tiny little something in the appendix. It could all be put right. Strengthen the energy of one organ, weaken the activity of another, absorption would take place, and everything would come right. He was a little late for dinner. He dined, talked cheerfully, but for a long time could not bring himself to go to his room to work. At last he went to the study and at once sat down to work. He read case files and worked, but the awareness that he had an important, intimate matter put aside, which he would turn to when he had finished, did not leave him. When he had finished his work, he remembered that this intimate matter was his thoughts about the appendix. But he did not give himself up to them; he went to the drawing room for tea. There were guests; they talked, played the piano and sang; the examining magistrate was there, the desired fiancé for his daughter. Ivan Ilyich spent the evening, as Praskovya Fyodorovna remarked, more cheerfully than anyone, but he did not forget for a minute that he had important thoughts about the appendix put aside. At eleven o’clock he said goodnight and went to his room. Since his illness he had slept alone, in a little room next to the study. He went in, undressed and took up a novel by Zola, but did not read it; he thought. And in his imagination that longed-for correction of the appendix was taking place. Absorption, elimination, regular activity was restored. “Yes, that’s how it is,” he said to himself. “One only has to help nature.” He remembered his medicine, raised himself, took it, and lay on his back, listening to how beneficially the medicine was working and how it was destroying the pain. “Only take it regularly and avoid harmful influences; I already feel somewhat better, much better.” He began to feel his side: it did not hurt to the touch. “Yes, I really don’t feel it; it’s much better already.” He put out the candle and lay on his side... The appendix was correcting itself, absorbing. Suddenly he felt the familiar, old, dull, gnawing pain, stubborn, quiet, serious. In his mouth the same familiar foulness. His heart sank, his head swam. “My God, my God!” he said. “Again, again, and it will never stop.” And suddenly the matter appeared to him from quite another side. “The appendix? The kidney?” he said to himself. “It isn’t a question of the appendix or the kidney, but of life and... death. Yes, there was life, and now it is going, going, and I cannot hold it back. Yes. Why deceive myself? Isn’t it obvious to everyone but me that I am dying, and the only question is how many weeks, how many days... perhaps now. There was light, and now there is darkness. I was here, and now I am going there! Where?” A chill came over him; his breathing stopped. He heard only the beating of his heart.

“I shall not be, so what will there be? There will be nothing. Then where shall I be, when I am no more? Can this be death? No, I don’t want it.” He jumped up, tried to light the candle, fumbled with trembling hands, dropped the candle and candlestick on the floor, and fell back again on the pillow. “What for? It makes no difference,” he said to himself, staring into the darkness with open eyes. “Death. Yes, death. And none of them know, or want to know, and they don’t pity me. They are playing.” (He heard from far off, beyond the door, the swell of a voice and the ritornello of the accompaniment.) “It makes no difference to them, but they will die too. Fools. I go first, and they later; the same will happen to them. And they are enjoying themselves. Brutes!” Rage choked him. And he felt an agonising, unbearable heaviness. It cannot be that everyone is always condemned to this terrible fear. He sat up.

“Something isn’t right; I must calm down; I must think it all through from the beginning.” And so he began to think it through. “Yes, the beginning of the illness. I knocked my side, and I was just the same, that day and the next; it ached a little, then more, then the doctors, then the low spirits, the anguish, the doctors again; and all the while I was going nearer and nearer to the abyss. Less strength. Nearer, nearer. And now I have wasted away; there is no light in my eyes. And it is death, and I am thinking about the appendix. I am thinking about mending my insides, and this is death. Can it really be death?” Again the horror came over him; he panted, bent down, began to look for the matches, and pressed his elbow on the bedside table. It was in his way and hurt him; he grew angry with it, pressed on it harder in annoyance, and knocked it over. And, in despair, gasping for breath, he fell back, expecting death at that very moment.

The guests were leaving at this time. Praskovya Fyodorovna was seeing them off. She heard the crash and came in.

“What is it?”

“Nothing. I knocked it over by accident.”

She went out and brought a candle. He lay there, breathing heavily and very fast, like a man who has run a mile, and stared at her with fixed eyes.

“What is it, Jean?”

“No...thing. I knocked... it... over.” (“What is there to say? She won’t understand,” he thought.)

She certainly did not understand. She picked it up, lit the candle for him and hurried away: she had to see a guest off.

When she came back, he was lying the same way, on his back, looking up.

“What’s the matter? Are you worse?”

“Yes.”

She shook her head and sat for a while. “You know, Jean, I wonder whether we shouldn’t ask Leshchetitsky to come to the house.”

That meant calling in the famous doctor and not sparing the expense. He smiled venomously and said, “No.” She sat a little longer, then came over and kissed him on the forehead.

He hated her with all the strength of his soul while she was kissing him, and made an effort not to push her away.

“Goodnight. God willing, you’ll sleep.”

“Yes.”

## VI

Ivan Ilyich saw that he was dying, and he was in constant despair.

In the depths of his soul Ivan Ilyich knew that he was dying; but not only was he not used to it, he simply did not understand it, could not understand it at all.

The example of a syllogism he had learned in Kiesewetter’s logic: “Caius is a man, men are mortal, therefore Caius is mortal,” had seemed to him all his life to be true only of Caius, and certainly not of himself. That was Caius the man, man in general, and that was perfectly fair; but he was not Caius, and not man in general; he had always been a quite, quite special being, different from all others; he had been Vanya, with Mama and Papa, with Mitya and Volodya, with his toys, the coachman, his nanny, then with Katenka, with all the joys, griefs and raptures of childhood, boyhood and youth. Was it for Caius that there was the smell of that striped leather ball Vanya had loved so much? Was it Caius who had kissed his mother’s hand like that, and was it for Caius that the silk folds of his mother’s dress had rustled like that? Was it he who had rioted over the pies at the School of Law? Had Caius been in love like that? Could Caius preside over a session of the court like that?

And Caius really is mortal, and it is right for him to die; but for me, Vanya, Ivan Ilyich, with all my feelings and thoughts, for me it is a different matter. And it cannot be that I ought to die. That would be too terrible.

That is how he felt.

“If I were to die like Caius, I would have known it, an inner voice would have told me so; but there was nothing of the kind in me; and I and all my friends, we understood that it was not at all the same as with Caius. And now look at this!” he said to himself. “It cannot be. It cannot be, and yet it is. How can this be? How is one to understand it?”

And he could not understand it, and he tried to drive this thought away as false, incorrect and morbid, and to push it out with other, correct and healthy thoughts. But this thought, and not just a thought, but as it were a reality, came back again and stood before him.

And one after another he called up other thoughts in place of this one, in the hope of finding support in them. He tried to return to his former trains of thought, which had once screened the thought of death from him. But, strange to say, everything that had formerly screened, hidden and destroyed the awareness of death could no longer have that effect. Ivan Ilyich spent most of this last period in these attempts to restore the former trains of feeling that had screened death. Now he would say to himself: “I’ll take up my work; after all, I used to live by it.” And he would go to the court, driving away all doubts; he would enter into conversation with his colleagues and sit down, from old habit, absent-mindedly casting a thoughtful glance over the crowd and resting both his wasted hands on the arms of the oak chair, leaning towards a colleague as usual, moving the papers towards him, exchanging whispers; and then, suddenly raising his eyes and sitting up straight, he would pronounce the familiar words and open the proceedings. But suddenly, in the middle, the pain in his side, paying no attention whatever to the stage the case had reached, would begin its sucking work. Ivan Ilyich would listen to it, drive the thought of it away, but it went on with its work, and *it* would come and stand right in front of him and look at him, and he would be stunned, the fire would go out of his eyes, and he would begin to ask himself again: “Is it really the only truth?” And his colleagues and subordinates saw with surprise and regret that he, such a brilliant, subtle judge, was getting confused and making mistakes. He would shake himself, try to pull himself together, somehow bring the session to an end, and return home with the sad awareness that his work as a judge could no longer, as before, hide from him what he wanted hidden; that he could not escape *it* through his work. And what was worst of all was that *it* drew him to itself not so that he would do anything, but only so that he would look at it, straight into its eyes, look at it and, doing nothing, suffer unspeakably.

And to escape from this state, Ivan Ilyich looked for consolations, for other screens; and other screens appeared and for a short time seemed to save him, but at once, not so much collapsed as became transparent, as if *it* penetrated everything, and nothing could screen it.

In this last period it would happen that he went into the drawing room he had furnished: the drawing room where he had fallen, for which (how venomously funny it was to think of it) for the furnishing of which he had sacrificed his life, because he knew that his illness had begun with that bruise. He would go in and see a scratch on the lacquered table, cut by something. He would look for the cause, and find it in the bronze ornament of an album, bent at the edge. He would take up the album, an expensive one he had lovingly put together, and be vexed at the carelessness of his daughter and her friends: here something torn, there the photographs upside down. He would carefully put it in order and bend the ornament back.

Then the idea would come to him of moving this whole *établissement*, this arrangement, with the albums, to another corner, near the flowers. He would call the footman; or his daughter or wife would come to help him; they would disagree, contradict him; he would argue and get angry; but all was well, because he did not remember *it*; it could not be seen.

But then his wife said, as he was moving something himself, “Please, let the servants do it, you’ll hurt yourself again,” and suddenly *it* flashed through the screen, and he saw it. It flashed; he still hoped it would hide again; but involuntarily he listened to his side: the same thing was sitting there, aching just the same, and he could no longer forget, and *it* was plainly looking at him from behind the flowers. What was it all for?

“And it’s true that here, on this curtain, as if storming a fortress, I lost my life. Really? How terrible and how stupid! It cannot be! It cannot be, and yet it is.”

He would go to his study, lie down, and be alone with *it* again, face to face with *it*, and there was nothing to be done with it. Only look at it and grow cold.
