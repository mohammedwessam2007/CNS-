“‘The terrible and intelligent spirit, the spirit of self-destruction and non-being,’ the old man goes on, ‘the great spirit spoke with you in the wilderness, and we are told in the books that he “tempted” you. Is that so? And could anything truer be said than what he told you in his three questions, which you rejected, and which the books call “temptations”? And yet if ever there was a real, thundering miracle on earth, it was on that day, the day of those three temptations. The miracle lay precisely in the appearance of those three questions.

“‘Imagine, just as an experiment, that the three questions of the terrible spirit had been lost from the books without a trace, and had to be restored, thought up and composed anew to be put back in the books, and for this all the wise men of the earth were gathered together: rulers, high priests, scholars, philosophers, poets. And they were given the task: think up, compose three questions, which must not only be equal to the size of the event but must also express, in three words, in only three human sentences, the whole future history of the world and of humanity. Do you think that all the wisdom of the earth, united together, could think up anything even remotely equal in strength and depth to the three questions that were actually put to you then by the powerful and intelligent spirit in the wilderness? From those questions alone, from the miracle of their appearing, one can see that one is dealing not with a passing human mind but with an eternal and absolute one. For in those three questions the whole subsequent history of mankind is, as it were, brought together into one whole and foretold, and three images are revealed in which all the insoluble historical contradictions of human nature on all the earth will meet. It could not be seen so clearly then, because the future was unknown; but now that fifteen centuries have passed, we see that everything in those three questions was so well guessed and foretold, and has come true so completely, that nothing more can be added to them or taken away.

“‘Decide for yourself who was right: you, or the one who questioned you then? Remember the first question. Its meaning, if not its words, was this: “You want to go into the world, and you go empty-handed, with some promise of freedom which men, in their simplicity and their innate unruliness, cannot even understand, which they fear and dread, for nothing has ever been more unbearable for man and for human society than freedom! But do you see these stones in this bare, scorching wilderness? Turn them into bread, and mankind will run after you like a flock, grateful and obedient, though forever trembling that you will withdraw your hand and your bread will stop.” But you did not want to take away man’s freedom, and you rejected the offer, for what kind of freedom is it, you reasoned, if obedience is bought with bread? You replied that man does not live by bread alone. But do you know that in the name of that very earthly bread the spirit of the earth will rise up against you and fight with you and defeat you, and everyone will follow him, crying, “Who is like unto this beast? He has given us fire from heaven!” Do you know that centuries will pass, and mankind will proclaim through the mouth of its wisdom and science that there is no crime, and therefore no sin, but only hungry people? “Feed them first, then ask them to be virtuous!” That is what they will write on the banner they will raise against you, and with which your temple will be destroyed.

“‘In place of your temple a new building will rise; the terrible Tower of Babel will be raised again, and though this one will not be finished either, any more than the first, still you could have spared people this new tower and cut their sufferings short by a thousand years, for it is to us they will come, after tormenting themselves for a thousand years with their tower! They will seek us out again, under the earth, in the catacombs, where we shall be hiding (for we shall be persecuted and tortured again), they will find us and cry out to us: “Feed us, for those who promised us fire from heaven have not given it.” And then we shall finish building their tower, for the one who finishes it is the one who feeds them, and only we shall feed them, in your name, and we shall lie that it is in your name. Oh, never, never will they feed themselves without us! No science will give them bread while they remain free. In the end they will bring their freedom to our feet and say to us: “Make us your slaves, but feed us.” They will understand at last that freedom and enough earthly bread for everyone are unthinkable together, for never, never will they be able to share among themselves! They will be convinced, too, that they can never be free, because they are weak, vicious, worthless and rebellious.

“‘You promised them the bread of heaven, but, I repeat, can it compare in the eyes of the weak, forever vicious and forever ignoble race of men with earthly bread? And if thousands and tens of thousands follow you for the sake of the bread of heaven, what will become of the millions and tens of thousands of millions of creatures who will not have the strength to give up earthly bread for the bread of heaven? Or are only the tens of thousands of the great and strong dear to you, while the remaining millions, numerous as the sand of the sea, who are weak but love you, must serve only as material for the great and strong? No, the weak are dear to us too. They are vicious and rebellious, but in the end they will become obedient. They will marvel at us and think us gods, because we, by standing at their head, have agreed to endure freedom and to rule over them, so terrible will it become for them at last to be free! But we shall say that we obey you and rule in your name. We shall deceive them again, for we shall not let you come near us any more. In that deception our suffering will lie, for we shall have to lie.

“‘That is what the first question in the wilderness meant, and that is what you rejected in the name of freedom, which you put above everything. And yet that question held the great secret of this world. Had you accepted the “loaves”, you would have answered the universal and everlasting anguish of man, both as an individual and as the whole of mankind, which is: “Whom shall I bow down to?” There is no care more constant and more agonising for a man who is left free than to find, as quickly as possible, someone to bow down to. But man seeks to bow down to something that is beyond dispute, so far beyond dispute that all men at once would agree to bow down to it together. For the care of these pitiful creatures is not only to find something that I or someone else can bow down to, but to find something that everyone will believe in and bow down to, and it must be everyone together. This need for a shared worship is the chief torment of every man, and of all mankind, since the beginning of time. For the sake of this shared worship they have killed each other with the sword. They have made gods and called out to each other: “Give up your gods and come and worship ours, or death to you and your gods!” And so it will be until the end of the world, even when the gods have disappeared from the world: they will fall down before idols all the same.

“‘You knew, you could not but know this fundamental secret of human nature, but you rejected the only absolute banner that was offered to you to make everyone bow down to you without dispute: the banner of earthly bread. And you rejected it in the name of freedom and the bread of heaven. And look what you did next. And all again in the name of freedom! I tell you, man has no care more agonising than to find someone to whom he can hand over, as quickly as possible, the gift of freedom with which this unhappy creature is born. But only the one who sets their conscience at rest can take possession of men’s freedom. With bread you were given a banner beyond dispute: give bread, and man will bow down, for nothing is more beyond dispute than bread. But if at the same time someone else takes possession of his conscience, oh, then he will even throw away your bread and follow the one who has seduced his conscience. In that you were right. For the secret of human existence is not just to live, but to have something to live for. Without a firm idea of what he lives for, man will not agree to live; he would rather destroy himself than stay on earth, even if he were surrounded by bread.

“‘That is so. But what happened? Instead of taking possession of men’s freedom, you increased it even more! Did you forget that peace, and even death, are dearer to man than free choice in the knowledge of good and evil? Nothing is more seductive for man than the freedom of his conscience, but nothing is more agonising either. And instead of firm foundations for setting the human conscience at rest once and for all, you chose everything that is exceptional, enigmatic and vague, you chose everything that was beyond men’s strength, and so you acted as though you did not love them at all, and who did this? The one who came to give his life for them! Instead of taking possession of men’s freedom, you multiplied it, and burdened the spiritual kingdom of man with its torments forever. You wanted man’s free love, so that he would follow you freely, enticed and captivated by you. In place of the firm ancient law, man was from now on to decide for himself, with a free heart, what is good and what is evil, having only your image before him as a guide. But did you not think that he would in the end reject and dispute even your image and your truth, if he were weighed down with so terrible a burden as freedom of choice? They will cry out at last that the truth is not in you, for it was impossible to leave them in more confusion and torment than you did, leaving them so many cares and insoluble problems. So you yourself laid the foundation for the destruction of your own kingdom, and you should not blame anyone else for it.

“‘And yet, was that what was offered to you? There are three powers, only three powers on earth, that can conquer and hold captive forever the conscience of these weak rebels, for their own happiness. These powers are miracle, mystery and authority. You rejected all three, and set the example yourself. When the terrible and wise spirit set you on the pinnacle of the temple and said to you, “If you want to know whether you are the Son of God, throw yourself down, for it is written that the angels will catch him and bear him up, and he will not fall and be dashed to pieces, and then you will know whether you are the Son of God, and you will prove how great your faith in your Father is,” you listened, and rejected the offer, and did not give in, and did not throw yourself down. Oh, of course, you acted proudly and magnificently there, like God; but men, that weak, rebellious race, are they gods? Oh, you understood then that if you had taken just one step, made just one movement to throw yourself down, you would at once have tempted the Lord and lost all your faith in him, and been dashed to pieces against the earth you had come to save, and the intelligent spirit who tempted you would have rejoiced. But, I repeat, are there many like you? And could you really have supposed for one moment that men, too, would be able to withstand such a temptation? Is human nature made to reject miracles and, in the most terrible moments of life, the moments of its deepest, most fundamental and agonising questions of the soul, to remain with only the free decision of the heart?

“‘Oh, you knew that your deed would be kept in the books, would reach the depths of time and the furthest ends of the earth, and you hoped that, following you, man too would stay with God without needing a miracle. But you did not know that as soon as man rejects miracles he will reject God as well, for man seeks not so much God as miracles. And since man cannot bear to be without miracles, he will create new miracles of his own, and he will bow down to the miracles of quacks and the witchcraft of old women, even if he is a rebel, a heretic and an atheist a hundred times over. You did not come down from the cross when they shouted to you, mocking and taunting you, “Come down from the cross and we will believe that it is you.” You did not come down because, again, you did not want to enslave man by a miracle, and you longed for faith that was free, not faith based on a miracle. You longed for free love, not the slavish raptures of a slave before a power that has terrified him once and for all. But here too you judged men too highly, for of course they are slaves, though they were created rebels.

“‘Look around and judge. Fifteen centuries have passed: go and look at them. Whom have you raised up to yourself? I swear, man was made weaker and lower than you thought! Can he, can he do what you did? By respecting him so much you acted as if you had ceased to feel compassion for him, because you asked too much of him, and who did this? The one who loved him more than himself! If you had respected him less, you would have asked less of him, and that would have been closer to love, for his burden would have been lighter. He is weak and base. What does it matter that he now rebels everywhere against our power and is proud of rebelling? It is the pride of a child, a schoolboy. They are little children who have rebelled in class and driven out the teacher. But the children’s glee will come to an end, and it will cost them dear. They will tear down the temples and drench the earth in blood. But the foolish children will see at last that, though they are rebels, they are feeble rebels who cannot bear their own rebellion. Shedding their foolish tears, they will admit at last that the one who made them rebels must have meant to mock them. They will say it in despair, and what they say will be blasphemy, which will make them still more unhappy, for human nature cannot bear blasphemy and in the end always takes revenge for it itself.

“‘So: unrest, confusion and unhappiness, that is the lot of men now, after you suffered so much for their freedom! Your great prophet says, in his vision and his allegory, that he saw all those who took part in the first resurrection, and that there were twelve thousand from each tribe. But if there were so many of them, they were not men but gods. They bore your cross, they endured decades in the hungry, naked wilderness, living on locusts and roots, and of course you can point with pride to these children of freedom, of free love, of their free and magnificent sacrifice in your name. But remember that there were only a few thousand of them, and those were gods; and what about the rest? How are the rest, the weak people, to blame for not being able to endure what the mighty endured? How is the weak soul to blame for not being able to hold such terrible gifts? Did you really come only to the chosen, and for the chosen? But if so, there is a mystery here, and we cannot understand it. And if it is a mystery, then we too had the right to preach mystery and to teach them that it is not the free decision of their hearts that matters, and not love, but the mystery, which they must obey blindly, even against their conscience.

“‘That is what we did. We corrected your work and founded it on miracle, mystery and authority. And people rejoiced that they were led like a flock again, and that the terrible gift that had brought them so much suffering was at last lifted from their hearts. Were we right to teach and act like this, tell me? Did we not love mankind, when we so humbly recognised its weakness, lovingly lightened its burden, and allowed its feeble nature even sin, so long as it was with our permission? Why have you come to get in our way now? And why do you look at me so silently and so deeply with your meek eyes? Be angry! I do not want your love, because I do not love you myself.
