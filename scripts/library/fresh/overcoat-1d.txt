The important person, it seems, had not noticed that Akaky Akakievich was already over fifty. So that if he could be called a young man at all, it was only relatively, that is, in relation to someone who was already seventy.

“Do you know whom you are saying this to? Do you understand who is standing before you? Do you understand that, do you understand it? I am asking you.”

Here he stamped his foot, raising his voice to such a powerful note that even someone other than Akaky Akakievich would have been frightened. Akaky Akakievich simply froze, staggered, trembled all over and could not stand at all: if the porters had not run up at once to hold him, he would have flopped onto the floor; he was carried out almost motionless. And the important person, pleased that the effect had exceeded even his expectations, and completely intoxicated by the thought that his word could even deprive a man of his senses, glanced sideways at his friend to see how he was taking it, and saw, not without pleasure, that his friend was in a most uncertain state and was even beginning to feel afraid himself.

How he went down the stairs, how he came out into the street, Akaky Akakievich remembered nothing of that. He could not feel his arms or his legs. Never in his life had he been so badly hauled over the coals by a general, and a strange general at that. He walked through the blizzard that whistled in the streets, with his mouth open, stumbling off the pavements; the wind, as is the Petersburg custom, blew at him from all four sides, from every side street. In a moment it had given him a quinsy in his throat, and he got home unable to say a single word; he swelled up all over and took to his bed. So powerful a proper dressing-down can sometimes be! The very next day he had a high fever. Thanks to the generous assistance of the Petersburg climate, the illness progressed faster than could have been expected, and when the doctor came, he felt his pulse and could find nothing to do but prescribe a poultice, solely so that the patient should not be left without the beneficent help of medicine; and at the same time he announced that in a day and a half it would be certainly all up with him. After which he turned to the landlady and said: “And you, my good woman, don’t waste any time: order him a pine coffin right away, because an oak one will be too dear for him.” Whether Akaky Akakievich heard these words, so fatal for him, and if he heard them, whether they had a shattering effect on him, whether he grieved for his wretched life, none of this is known, because he was all the time in delirium and fever. Visions, each stranger than the last, kept appearing to him: now he saw Petrovich and ordered him to make an overcoat with some kind of traps for thieves, who seemed to him to be constantly under the bed, and he kept calling his landlady to pull a thief out from under the blanket; now he asked why his old dressing gown was hanging in front of him, when he had a new overcoat; now it seemed to him that he was standing before the general, listening to a proper dressing-down, and saying, “I am sorry, Your Excellency!”; now, at last, he even cursed, uttering the most terrible words, so that his old landlady even crossed herself, never in her life having heard anything of the kind from him, all the more since these words came straight after the words “Your Excellency”. After that he talked complete nonsense, so that nothing could be understood; one could only see that his disordered words and thoughts kept turning round one and the same overcoat. At last poor Akaky Akakievich gave up the ghost. Neither his room nor his belongings were sealed up, because, in the first place, there were no heirs, and in the second, very little was left to inherit, namely: a bundle of goose quills, a quire of white government paper, three pairs of socks, two or three buttons torn off his trousers, and the dressing gown already known to the reader. Who got all this, God knows: I confess that even the teller of this story did not take an interest. Akaky Akakievich was carted off and buried. And Petersburg was left without Akaky Akakievich, as if he had never been in it. There vanished and disappeared a being protected by no one, dear to no one, interesting to no one, who had not even attracted the attention of the naturalist, who never fails to stick a pin through an ordinary fly and examine it under the microscope; a being who meekly bore the mockery of the office and went down to his grave without any extraordinary event, but for whom, all the same, though just before the very end of his life, there flashed a bright visitor in the form of an overcoat, which brightened his poor life for a moment, and on whom misfortune afterwards fell just as unbearably as it has fallen on the kings and rulers of the world... A few days after his death a porter was sent to his flat from the department with orders for him to come at once: the chief, it was said, required him. But the porter had to return empty-handed, reporting that he could not come any more, and to the question “Why not?” he expressed himself in these words: “Well, he’s dead; they buried him three days ago.” In this way the department learned of Akaky Akakievich’s death, and the very next day a new clerk was already sitting in his place, much taller, and forming his letters not in such a straight hand, but much more slanting and crooked.

But who could have imagined that this was not yet all about Akaky Akakievich, that he was destined to live noisily for a few days after his death, as if in reward for a life that no one had noticed? But so it happened, and our poor story unexpectedly takes on a fantastic ending.

Rumours suddenly spread through Petersburg that near the Kalinkin Bridge, and much further afield, a dead man had begun to appear at night in the form of a clerk looking for some stolen overcoat, and, on the pretext of the stolen overcoat, pulling every kind of overcoat off every pair of shoulders, regardless of rank or title: cat-fur, beaver, wadded, raccoon, fox and bearskin coats; in a word, every kind of fur and skin that people have ever devised to cover their own. One of the clerks from the department saw the dead man with his own eyes and at once recognised Akaky Akakievich; but this filled him with such fear that he ran off as fast as his legs could carry him, and so could not get a good look; he only saw the other shake his finger at him from a distance. Complaints came in constantly from all sides that backs and shoulders, if only of titular councillors, but no, even of privy councillors themselves, were exposed to severe colds because of the nightly snatching of overcoats. The police were given orders to catch the dead man at all costs, dead or alive, and to punish him in the cruellest way as an example to others; and they very nearly succeeded. Namely, a watchman of some district in Kiryushkin Lane had actually seized the dead man by the collar at the very scene of the crime, as he was attempting to pull a frieze overcoat off some retired musician who in his day had played the flute. Having seized him by the collar, the watchman called out to two of his comrades, whom he told to hold him, while he himself reached into his boot for just one minute to take out his birch-bark snuffbox and refresh his nose, which had been frostbitten six times in his life; but the snuff must have been of a kind that even a dead man could not stand. Before the watchman, having closed his right nostril with his finger, had time to sniff half a handful up his left, the dead man sneezed so violently that he spattered all three of them right in the eyes. While they were raising their fists to rub them, the dead man vanished without a trace, so that they did not even know whether he had really been in their hands. From then on the watchmen became so afraid of dead men that they were even afraid to seize live ones, and only shouted from a distance, “Hey, you, go on your way!”; and the dead clerk began to appear even beyond the Kalinkin Bridge, causing no small fear to all timid people.

But we have completely forgotten a certain important person, who, in truth, was very nearly the cause of the fantastic turn taken by this, however, entirely true story. First of all, justice demands that we say that a certain important person, soon after poor Akaky Akakievich had left, thoroughly dressed down, felt something like regret. Compassion was not foreign to him; many good impulses were accessible to his heart, although his rank very often prevented them from showing. As soon as his visiting friend had left his study, he even began to think about poor Akaky Akakievich. And from then on almost every day he saw before him the pale Akaky Akakievich, who had not been able to stand an official dressing-down. The thought of him troubled him so much that a week later he even decided to send a clerk to him to find out how he was and whether he could in fact be helped somehow; and when he was told that Akaky Akakievich had died suddenly of a fever, he was even struck by it, heard the reproaches of his conscience, and was out of sorts all day. Wishing to distract himself somehow and forget the unpleasant impression, he went to an evening party at a friend’s, where he found decent company and, best of all, almost everyone there was of the same rank, so that he could not be constrained by anything at all. This had an amazing effect on his state of mind. He opened up, became pleasant in conversation, amiable; in a word, he spent the evening very pleasantly. At supper he drank two glasses of champagne, a remedy which, as is well known, works not badly in the matter of cheerfulness. The champagne disposed him to various extravagances, namely: he decided not to go home yet, but to call on a lady he knew, Karolina Ivanovna, a lady, it seems, of German origin, towards whom he felt entirely friendly relations. It must be said that the important person was no longer a young man, a good husband and a respectable father of a family. Two sons, one of whom was already serving in an office, and a pretty sixteen-year-old daughter with a slightly turned-up but charming little nose, came every day to kiss his hand, saying “*Bonjour, papa.*” His wife, still a fresh woman and not at all bad-looking, would first give him her hand to kiss and then, turning it over, kiss his hand. But the important person, though entirely satisfied with domestic family tenderness, found it proper to have a lady friend for friendly relations in another part of the town. This lady friend was not a bit better or younger than his wife; but such puzzles exist in the world, and it is not for us to judge them. And so the important person came down the stairs, got into his sledge and said to the coachman: “To Karolina Ivanovna’s”; and he himself, wrapped very luxuriously in a warm overcoat, remained in that pleasant state than which nothing better can be imagined for a Russian, that is, when you are not thinking of anything yourself, and meanwhile thoughts come into your head of their own accord, each pleasanter than the last, without even giving you the trouble of chasing after them and looking for them. Full of pleasure, he lightly recalled all the merry moments of the evening, all the words that had made the little circle roar with laughter; many of them he even repeated under his breath and found that they were just as funny as before, and so it is no wonder that he laughed heartily himself. Now and then, however, he was bothered by a gusty wind, which, bursting out suddenly from God knows where and for no known reason, cut right into his face, throwing bits of snow into it, flapping his overcoat collar like a sail, or suddenly, with unnatural force, throwing it over his head, and so giving him endless trouble getting out of it. Suddenly the important person felt that someone had seized him very firmly by the collar. Turning round, he saw a short man in an old, worn uniform, and recognised him, not without horror, as Akaky Akakievich. The clerk’s face was white as snow and looked like that of a dead man. But the horror of the important person went beyond all bounds when he saw the dead man’s mouth twist and, breathing on him the terrible smell of the grave, utter these words: “Ah! So here you are at last! At last I have, you know, caught you by the collar! It’s your overcoat I need! You didn’t trouble about mine, and you dressed me down besides; so now give me yours!” The poor important person almost died. However much character he showed in the office and in general before his subordinates, and although everyone who looked at his manly appearance and figure said, “Oh, what a character!”, here, like very many people of heroic appearance, he felt such fear that he even began, not without reason, to be afraid of some sort of fit. He even threw off his overcoat from his shoulders himself as fast as he could, and shouted to the coachman in a voice not his own: “Home, at full speed!” The coachman, hearing the voice that is usually used at decisive moments and is even accompanied by something much more effective, hunched his head into his shoulders just in case, raised his whip, and flew off like an arrow. In a little over six minutes the important person was already at the door of his own house. Pale, terrified and without his overcoat, instead of going to Karolina Ivanovna’s he had come home; he dragged himself somehow to his room and spent the night in very great disorder, so that the next morning at tea his daughter said to him straight out: “You are quite pale today, Papa.” But Papa said nothing and told no one a word about what had happened to him, or where he had been, or where he had wanted to go. This incident made a strong impression on him. He even began to say to his subordinates much less often, “How dare you? Do you understand who is standing before you?”; and if he did say it, it was not until he had first heard what the matter was about. But even more remarkable is that from then on the appearances of the dead clerk stopped entirely: the general’s overcoat must have fitted his shoulders perfectly; at any rate, no more such cases of overcoats being snatched from anyone were heard of anywhere. However, many active and solicitous people would not calm down at all, and kept saying that the dead clerk was still appearing in the outlying parts of the town. And indeed, a watchman in Kolomna saw with his own eyes a ghost appear from behind a house; but being by nature somewhat feeble, so that once an ordinary full-grown piglet, rushing out of some private house, knocked him off his feet, to the great amusement of the cabmen standing around, from whom he demanded a farthing each for snuff for such mockery; and so, being feeble, he did not dare to stop it, but followed it in the darkness until at last the ghost suddenly looked round and, stopping, asked: “What do you want?”, and showed a fist such as you will not find even among the living. The watchman said, “Nothing,” and turned back at once. The ghost, however, was much taller, wore an enormous moustache, and, directing its steps, it seemed, towards the Obukhov Bridge, vanished completely into the darkness of the night.
