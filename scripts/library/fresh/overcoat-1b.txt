“No, it can’t be mended: it’s a wretched garment!”

At these words Akaky Akakievich’s heart sank.

“Why can’t it, Petrovich?” he said, almost in the pleading voice of a child. “It’s only worn a little on the shoulders; surely you have some bits of cloth...”

“Bits of cloth can be found, there’ll be bits,” said Petrovich, “but you can’t sew them on: the stuff is completely rotten; touch it with a needle and it just falls apart.”

“Let it fall apart, and you put a little patch on at once.”

“But there’s nothing to put the patch on; there’s nothing for it to hold on to; it’s too far gone. It’s cloth in name only; if the wind blew, it would fly to pieces.”

“Well, just fasten one on. How can it be like this, really, you know!..”

“No,” said Petrovich decisively, “nothing can be done. It’s in a very bad way. You’d better, when the cold winter weather comes, make yourself some foot-wrappings out of it, because a stocking doesn’t keep you warm. The Germans invented them so as to get more money for themselves” (Petrovich liked to have a dig at the Germans when he had the chance). “And as for the overcoat, it looks as if you’ll have to have a new one made.”

At the word “new” everything went misty before Akaky Akakievich’s eyes, and everything in the room began to swim before him. He saw clearly only the general with the face pasted over with paper on the lid of Petrovich’s snuffbox.

“A new one?” he said, still as if in a dream. “But I haven’t the money for that.”

“Yes, a new one,” said Petrovich with barbaric calm.

“Well, and if I had to have a new one, how much would it, you know...”

“You mean, what would it cost?”

“Yes.”

“You’d have to lay out three fifties and a bit more,” said Petrovich, and pressed his lips together significantly. He was very fond of strong effects; he liked to take someone completely aback somehow and then look sideways to see what face the person would make after such words.

“A hundred and fifty roubles for an overcoat!” cried poor Akaky Akakievich; cried, perhaps, for the first time in his life, for he was always distinguished by the quietness of his voice.

“Yes, sir,” said Petrovich, “and what an overcoat. If you put marten on the collar and add a hood with a silk lining, it’ll come to two hundred.”

“Petrovich, please,” said Akaky Akakievich in a pleading voice, not hearing and not trying to hear the words Petrovich had said and all his effects, “mend it somehow, so that it will serve a little longer at least.”

“Oh, no; that would be killing the work and wasting the money,” said Petrovich; and after these words Akaky Akakievich went out completely crushed.

And after he had gone, Petrovich stood for a long time with his lips pressed significantly together, not starting work, pleased that he had neither lowered himself nor betrayed the art of tailoring.

When he came out into the street, Akaky Akakievich was as if in a dream. “What a business, what a business,” he said to himself. “I really didn’t think it would turn out, you know...” and then, after some silence, he added: “So that’s how it is! So that’s what has come of it at last, and I really never could have supposed it would be like this.” Then followed another long silence, after which he said: “So that’s it! What a truly unexpected, you know... I would never have... such a circumstance!” Having said this, instead of going home he went in exactly the opposite direction, without suspecting it himself. On the way a chimney sweep brushed him with his whole sooty side and blackened his whole shoulder; a whole hatful of lime fell on him from the top of a house that was being built. He noticed none of this, and only later, when he bumped into a watchman who, having set his halberd beside him, was shaking snuff from a horn onto his calloused fist, did he come to himself a little, and only because the watchman said: “Why are you shoving right into my face? Haven’t you got the pavement?” This made him look round and turn towards home. Only here did he begin to collect his thoughts; he saw his position clearly and as it really was, and began to talk to himself, no longer in fragments but sensibly and frankly, as with a prudent friend with whom one can talk over a matter very close to one’s heart. “Well, no,” said Akaky Akakievich, “it’s no use talking to Petrovich now: he’s, you know... his wife must have given him a beating somehow. I’d better go to him on Sunday morning: after Saturday night he’ll be squinting and sleepy, so he’ll need a hair of the dog, and his wife won’t give him any money; and just then I’ll put ten kopecks, you know, into his hand; he’ll be more agreeable, and the overcoat then, you know...” So Akaky Akakievich reasoned with himself, cheered himself up, waited for the next Sunday, and, seeing from a distance that Petrovich’s wife was going out of the house somewhere, went straight to him. Petrovich was indeed squinting badly after Saturday, held his head towards the floor and was quite sleepy; but for all that, as soon as he learned what the matter was, it was as though the devil had nudged him. “It can’t be done,” he said. “Kindly order a new one.” It was then that Akaky Akakievich slipped him the ten kopecks. “Thank you kindly, sir; I’ll take a little something for your health,” said Petrovich, “and don’t you worry about the overcoat: it’s no good for anything. I’ll make you a new overcoat that will be a credit to us; we’ll stand by that.”

Akaky Akakievich tried again to say something about mending, but Petrovich did not hear him out and said: “I’ll make you a new one without fail, you may rely on that; we’ll do our best. We can even do it in the latest fashion: the collar will fasten with silver clasps under appliqué.”
