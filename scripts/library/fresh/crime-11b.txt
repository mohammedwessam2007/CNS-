## PART IV · CHAPTER I

“Can this be the dream going on?” Raskolnikov thought once more. Cautiously and mistrustfully he gazed at the unexpected visitor.

“Svidrigailov? What nonsense! It can’t be!” he said aloud at last, in bewilderment.

The visitor seemed not at all surprised by this exclamation.

“I have called on you for two reasons: in the first place, I wished to make your acquaintance personally, since I have long heard of you from a very curious point of view, and one favourable to you; and in the second, I dream that you will perhaps not refuse to help me in an undertaking that directly concerns the interest of your sister, Avdotya Romanovna. On my own, without a recommendation, she might not let me even into her yard now, because of prejudice; well, but with your help, on the contrary, I count on...”

“You count badly,” Raskolnikov interrupted.

“They arrived only yesterday, may I ask?”

Raskolnikov did not answer.

“Yesterday, I know. I myself arrived only the day before. Well, sir, this is what I will tell you on that score, Rodion Romanovich: I consider it superfluous to justify myself, but allow me too to declare: what is there, in all this, really so especially criminal on my part, that is, judging without prejudices, soberly?”

Raskolnikov went on examining him in silence.

“That in my own house I pursued a defenceless girl and ‘insulted her with my vile proposals’: isn’t that it, sir? (I am running ahead of myself!) But only suppose that I too am a man, et nihil humanum... nothing human, in a word, that I too am capable of being tempted and of falling in love (which, of course, does not happen at our command); then everything is explained in the most natural way. The whole question here is: am I a monster, or a victim myself? Well, and what if a victim? After all, in proposing to the object of my passion that she run away with me to America or to Switzerland, I may have cherished the most respectful feelings, and even thought of arranging our mutual happiness!.. Reason, you know, is the servant of passion; I was ruining myself even more, if you please!..”

“But that is not the point at all,” Raskolnikov interrupted with disgust. “Quite simply, you are repulsive, whether you are right or not; and so people don’t want to have anything to do with you, and they drive you away; so off you go!..”

Svidrigailov suddenly burst out laughing.

“Well, you... you can’t be thrown off!” he said, laughing in the frankest way. “I was thinking of being cunning, but no, you have gone straight to the very real point!”

“But you are still being cunning at this very moment.”

“Well, what of it? What of it?” Svidrigailov repeated, laughing wide open. “It is bonne guerre, fair war, as they say, and the most permissible cunning!.. But all the same you interrupted me; one way or another, I affirm again: there would have been no unpleasantness if it had not been for the incident in the garden. Marfa Petrovna...”

“And Marfa Petrovna, they say, you finished off too?” Raskolnikov interrupted rudely.

“So you have heard about that too? But then, how could you not... Well, as to this question of yours, really, I don’t know what to tell you, although my own conscience is in the highest degree at peace on that score. That is, don’t think that I fear anything of that sort: everything was done in perfect order and with complete exactness: the medical inquest found an apoplexy, brought on by bathing immediately after a heavy dinner with nearly a bottle of wine drunk, and it could have found nothing else... No, sir, this is what I have been thinking to myself for some time, especially on the journey, sitting in the train: did I not contribute to all this... misfortune, somehow, by irritating her morally, or something of that kind? But I concluded that this too was positively impossible.”

Raskolnikov laughed.

“Fancy worrying so much!”

“But what are you laughing at? Consider: I struck her only twice with a little riding whip; there weren’t even any marks... Please don’t think me a cynic; I know exactly how vile it is on my part, and so on; but I also know for certain that Marfa Petrovna was perhaps even glad of this, so to speak, impulse of mine. The story about your sister had been worn out to the last letter. For three days Marfa Petrovna had been forced to sit at home; she had nothing to show herself in the little town with, and besides, she had bored everyone there with that letter of hers (you have heard about the reading of the letter?). And suddenly these two lashes fall as if from heaven! The first thing she did was order the carriage to be harnessed!.. I won’t even mention that women have moments when it is very, very pleasant to be insulted, in spite of all their visible indignation. Everyone has them, these moments; man in general very, very much likes being insulted, have you noticed that? But with women it is especially so. One might even say that they live on it.”

At one point Raskolnikov thought of getting up and leaving and so ending the meeting. But a certain curiosity, and even a kind of calculation, held him back for the moment.

“Are you fond of fighting?” he asked absently.

“No, not very,” Svidrigailov answered calmly. “And Marfa Petrovna and I almost never fought. We lived in great harmony, and she always remained satisfied with me. In all our seven years I used the whip only twice (if one doesn’t count one more, third case, a very ambiguous one, though): the first time two months after our marriage, as soon as we arrived in the country, and then this last case now. And you thought I was such a monster, a reactionary, a serf-owner? He-he... And by the way: do you remember, Rodion Romanovich, how a few years ago, still in the days of beneficent publicity, a certain nobleman was publicly and literarily disgraced among us, I’ve forgotten his name! the one who thrashed a German woman in a railway carriage, remember? It was then, in that same year I think, that ‘The Disgraceful Act of The Age’ happened too (well, ‘Egyptian Nights’, the public reading, remember? Those black eyes! Oh, where are you, golden time of our youth!). Well, sir, here is my opinion: I have no deep sympathy for the gentleman who thrashed the German woman, because really it is... what is there to sympathise with! But at the same time I cannot help declaring that sometimes there are such provoking ‘German women’ that, it seems to me, there is not a single progressive who could fully vouch for himself. Nobody looked at the subject from that point of view at the time, and yet that point is the truly humane one, really it is, sir!”

Having said this, Svidrigailov suddenly laughed again. It was clear to Raskolnikov that this was a man who had firmly resolved on something and kept his own counsel.

“You must have gone several days on end without talking to anyone?” he asked.

“Almost. Why: are you surprised, I suppose, that I am such an easy-going man?”

“No, I am surprised that you are far too easy-going a man.”

“Because I was not offended by the rudeness of your questions? Is that it? Well... why be offended? As you asked, so I answered,” he added with an amazing expression of simple-heartedness. “I am not especially interested in almost anything, by God,” he went on, somehow thoughtfully. “Especially now; I am not occupied with anything at all... However, you are entitled to think that I am currying favour with some purpose, all the more since I have business with your sister, as I declared myself. But I will tell you frankly: I am very bored! Especially these three days, so that I was even glad to see you... Don’t be angry, Rodion Romanovich, but for some reason you yourself seem terribly strange to me. Say what you like, there is something in you; and precisely now, that is, not exactly at this minute, but now in general... Well, well, I won’t, I won’t, don’t frown! I am not such a bear as you think.”

Raskolnikov looked at him gloomily.

“Perhaps you are not a bear at all,” he said. “It even seems to me that you belong to very good society, or at least know how to be a decent man on occasion.”

“But I am not especially interested in anyone’s opinion,” Svidrigailov answered drily and even with a shade of haughtiness, “and so why not be a vulgar fellow for a while, when that suit of clothes is so convenient to wear in our climate and... and especially if one has a natural inclination for it,” he added, laughing again.

“I have heard, though, that you have many acquaintances here. You are what is called ‘not without connections’. Why do you need me, in that case, if not for some purpose?”

“You are right that I have acquaintances,” Svidrigailov took up, not answering the main point. “I have already met some; I have been loafing about for three days now; I recognise people, and they seem to recognise me. Of course, I am decently dressed and reckoned not a poor man; the emancipation of the serfs passed us by, you know: woods and water meadows, the income is not lost; but... I won’t go there; I was sick of it before: I have been walking about for three days and don’t make myself known to anyone... And then the city! I mean, how did it ever get composed among us, tell me, please! A city of clerks and all sorts of seminarists! Really, there was a lot I didn’t notice here before, eight years ago, when I was knocking about here... Now I put my hopes only in anatomy, by God!”

“What anatomy?”

“And as for these clubs, Dussot’s, these points of yours, or perhaps progress too, well, let it go on without us,” he went on, again not noticing the question. “And who wants to be a card sharper?”

“So you were a card sharper too?”

“How could I not be? There was a whole company of us, the most respectable, eight years ago; we passed the time; and all, you know, people with manners, there were poets, there were capitalists. And in general, among us in Russian society, the best manners are found in those who have been beaten, have you noticed that? It is only now in the country that I have let myself go. But all the same they put me in prison for debt then, a little Greek from Nezhin did. That was when Marfa Petrovna turned up, bargained and bought me out for thirty thousand pieces of silver. (I owed seventy thousand in all.) We were joined in lawful wedlock, and she carried me off at once to her place in the country, like some treasure. She was five years older than me, you know. She loved me very much. For seven years I never left the country. And note that all her life she kept a document against me, in someone else’s name, for those thirty thousand, so that if I had thought of rebelling in anything, I would have been in the trap at once! And she would have done it! With women, you know, all this lives together.”

“And if it hadn’t been for the document, would you have run off?”

“I don’t know what to tell you. The document hardly constrained me. I didn’t want to go anywhere, and Marfa Petrovna herself invited me abroad twice, seeing that I was bored. But what of it! I used to go abroad before, and it always made me sick. Not exactly, but the dawn is breaking, the Bay of Naples, the sea, you look, and somehow it is sad. What is most repulsive is that you really are sad about something! No, it’s better at home: here at least you blame others for everything and justify yourself. Perhaps I would go on an expedition to the North Pole now, because j’ai le vin mauvais, I am a bad drunk, and drinking disgusts me, and apart from wine there is nothing left. I have tried. And is it true, they say, that Berg is going up in a huge balloon in the Yusupov Garden on Sunday and inviting fellow passengers for a fee?”

“Why, would you go up?”

“I? No... just so...” muttered Svidrigailov, really as if lost in thought.

“What is he, really?” thought Raskolnikov.

“No, the document didn’t constrain me,” Svidrigailov went on thoughtfully. “It was I myself who never left the country. And it will be about a year now since Marfa Petrovna gave me back that document on my name day, and made me a present of a considerable sum besides. She had capital, you know. ‘You see how I trust you, Arkady Ivanovich’: really, that is how she put it. You don’t believe she put it like that? And you know, I became a decent landowner in the country; I am known in the district. I sent for books too. Marfa Petrovna approved at first, but then she kept being afraid I would study too much.”

“You seem to miss Marfa Petrovna very much?”

“I? Perhaps. Really, perhaps. And by the way, do you believe in ghosts?”

“What ghosts?”

“Ordinary ghosts, what else!”

“And do you believe in them?”

“Well, perhaps not, pour vous plaire, to please you... That is, not exactly not...”

“They appear to you, do they?”

Svidrigailov looked at him somehow strangely.

“Marfa Petrovna is pleased to visit me,” he said, twisting his mouth into a kind of strange smile.

“What do you mean, pleased to visit you?”

“Why, she has come three times already. The first time I saw her was on the very day of the funeral, an hour after the cemetery. That was the day before I left for here. The second time was the day before yesterday, on the journey, at dawn, at Malaya Vishera station; and the third time two hours ago, at the lodging where I am staying, in my room; I was alone.”

“Awake?”

“Completely. All three times awake. She comes, talks for a minute, and goes out by the door; always by the door. You can even seem to hear it.”

“Why did I think that something of this kind was sure to be happening to you!” Raskolnikov said suddenly, and at that very moment was surprised that he had said it. He was in great agitation.

“Re-eally? You thought that?” Svidrigailov asked in surprise. “Can it be? Well, didn’t I say there was some point in common between us, eh?”

“You never said that!” Raskolnikov answered sharply and heatedly.

“Didn’t I?”

“No!”

“I thought I did. Earlier, when I came in and saw that you were lying with your eyes closed and pretending, I said to myself at once: ‘This is the very one!’”

“What does that mean, the very one? What are you talking about?” cried Raskolnikov.

“About what? Really, I don’t know about what...” Svidrigailov muttered openly, as if confused himself.

For a minute they were silent. They stared at each other with all their eyes.

“This is all nonsense!” Raskolnikov cried in vexation. “What does she say to you when she comes?”

“She? Imagine, she talks about the most trifling nonsense, and you may marvel at human beings: that is just what makes me angry. The first time she came in (I was tired, you know: the funeral service, the ‘with the saints give rest’, then the litany, the meal; at last I was left alone in my study, lit a cigar, fell to thinking), she came in at the door: ‘And you, Arkady Ivanovich,’ she says, ‘with all the bustle today, forgot to wind the clock in the dining room.’ And indeed for all seven years I had wound that clock myself every week, and if I forgot, she would always remind me. The next day I was already on my way here. I went into the station at dawn, I had dozed in the night, I was aching, my eyes heavy with sleep; I took some coffee; I look, and Marfa Petrovna suddenly sits down beside me, a pack of cards in her hands: ‘Shall I tell your fortune for the journey, Arkady Ivanovich?’ And she was a past mistress at telling fortunes. Well, I shall never forgive myself for not letting her! I ran away in fright, and the bell was ringing just then, it’s true. Today I am sitting after the most wretched dinner from a cookshop, with a heavy stomach, sitting smoking, and suddenly Marfa Petrovna again, she comes in all dressed up, in a new green silk dress with a very long train: ‘Good day, Arkady Ivanovich! How do you like my dress? Aniska couldn’t make one like it.’ (Aniska is a dressmaker in our village, one of the former serfs, she was apprenticed in Moscow, a pretty little girl.) She stands turning round in front of me. I looked over the dress, then looked attentively into her face: ‘Why do you want,’ I say, ‘to come to me over such trifles, Marfa Petrovna, and trouble yourself?’ ‘Oh my God, my dear, so one can’t even disturb you now!’ To tease her, I say: ‘Marfa Petrovna, I want to get married.’ ‘That would be like you, Arkady Ivanovich; it does you little credit that before you have even buried your wife, you go off at once to get married. And if only you had chosen well, but I know you: neither for her nor for yourself, you’ll only make good people laugh.’ And she took herself off and went out, and her train seemed to rustle. What nonsense, eh?”

“But perhaps you are lying about all of it?” Raskolnikov responded.

“I rarely lie,” answered Svidrigailov, thoughtfully, and as if not noticing the rudeness of the question at all.

“And before this, you had never seen ghosts?”

“N... no, I did, only once in my life, six years ago. I had a house serf, Filka; he had only just been buried when I shouted, forgetting: ‘Filka, my pipe!’ He came in and went straight to the cabinet where I keep my pipes. I sit and think: ‘He is doing it to take revenge on me,’ because just before his death we had a bad quarrel. ‘How dare you,’ I say, ‘come into my presence with a hole in your elbow; get out, you rascal!’ He turned, went out and never came again. I didn’t tell Marfa Petrovna then. I was going to have a requiem served for him, but I felt ashamed.”

“Go and see a doctor.”

“I understand without you that I am unwell, though really I don’t know with what; in my opinion I am surely five times healthier than you. I didn’t ask you that: whether or not you believe that ghosts appear. I asked you: do you believe that ghosts exist?”

“No, I will never believe it!” Raskolnikov cried, even with a kind of anger.

“How do people usually put it?” Svidrigailov muttered, as if to himself, looking aside and bending his head a little. “They say: ‘You are ill, so what appears to you is only non-existent delirium.’ But there is no strict logic in that. I agree that ghosts appear only to the sick; but that only proves that ghosts cannot appear except to the sick, not that they do not exist in themselves.”

“Of course they don’t!” Raskolnikov insisted irritably.

“No? You think so?” Svidrigailov went on, looking at him slowly. “Well, and what if one reasons like this (now help me out here): ‘Ghosts are, so to speak, shreds and fragments of other worlds, their beginnings. A healthy man, of course, has no reason to see them, because a healthy man is the most earthly of men, and therefore must live only this life here, for the sake of completeness and order. Well, but as soon as he falls ill, as soon as the normal earthly order in the organism is disturbed, at once the possibility of another world begins to make itself felt, and the more ill he is, the more contacts there are with the other world, so that when a man dies altogether, he passes straight into the other world.’ I reasoned about this long ago. If you believe in a future life, you can believe this reasoning too.”

“I do not believe in a future life,” said Raskolnikov.

Svidrigailov sat lost in thought.

“And what if there is nothing there but spiders, or something of that sort,” he said suddenly.

“This is a madman,” thought Raskolnikov.

“We keep imagining eternity as an idea that cannot be understood, something huge, huge! But why must it be huge? And suddenly, instead of all that, imagine, there will be one little room there, something like a village bathhouse, black with soot, and spiders in every corner, and that is all eternity. I sometimes imagine it like that, you know.”

“And can it be, can it be that nothing more consoling and more just than that occurs to you!” Raskolnikov cried with a painful feeling.

“More just? And how do we know, perhaps that is just, and you know, I would certainly make it so on purpose!” answered Svidrigailov, smiling vaguely.

A kind of chill suddenly seized Raskolnikov at this hideous answer. Svidrigailov raised his head, looked at him intently and suddenly burst out laughing.

“No, just think of this,” he shouted. “Half an hour ago we had never even seen each other; we count ourselves enemies; there is an unsettled matter between us; we have dropped the matter and gone off into this kind of literature! Well, wasn’t I right in saying that we are berries from the same field?”

“Do me the favour,” Raskolnikov went on irritably, “allow me to ask you to explain yourself quickly and tell me why you have honoured me with your visit... and... and... I am in a hurry, I have no time, I want to go out...”

“By all means, by all means. Your sister, Avdotya Romanovna, is marrying Mr Luzhin, Pyotr Petrovich?”

“Could you not somehow avoid any question about my sister, and not mention her name? I don’t even understand how you dare to pronounce her name in my presence, if you really are Svidrigailov.”

“But I have come precisely to talk about her; how can I not mention her?”

“Very well; speak, but quickly!”

“I am sure that you have already formed your opinion of this Mr Luzhin, a relation of mine through my wife, if you have seen him for even half an hour or heard anything accurate and exact about him. He is no match for Avdotya Romanovna. In my opinion Avdotya Romanovna is sacrificing herself in this affair very generously and recklessly for... for her family. It seemed to me, from everything I had heard about you, that you for your part would be very glad if this marriage could be broken off without damage to anyone’s interests. Now that I know you personally, I am even sure of it.”

“All this is very naive on your part; forgive me, I meant to say: impudent,” said Raskolnikov.

“That is, you mean by this that I am working for my own pocket. Don’t worry, Rodion Romanovich: if I were working for my own advantage, I wouldn’t speak out so directly; I am not a complete fool, after all. On this score I will reveal to you a psychological oddity. Earlier, justifying my love for Avdotya Romanovna, I said that I was a victim myself. Well, then, know that I feel no love now, n-none at all, so that I find it strange myself, because I really did feel something...”

“From idleness and depravity,” Raskolnikov interrupted.

“It’s true, I am a depraved and idle man. But then your sister has so many advantages that even I could not help yielding to a certain impression. But all that is nonsense, as I see myself now.”

“Have you seen it long?”

“I began to notice it even before, but became finally convinced the day before yesterday, almost at the very moment of my arrival in Petersburg. Though even in Moscow I still imagined I was coming to seek Avdotya Romanovna’s hand and to compete with Mr Luzhin.”

“Forgive me for interrupting you; do me the favour: could you not cut it short and go straight to the purpose of your visit? I am in a hurry, I must go out...”

“With the greatest pleasure. Having arrived here and having now decided to undertake a certain... voyage, I wished to make the necessary preliminary arrangements. My children have stayed with their aunt; they are rich, and they have no personal need of me. And what kind of father am I! For myself I took only what Marfa Petrovna gave me a year ago. That is enough for me. Forgive me, I am coming to the point now. Before the voyage, which perhaps will come off, I want to have done with Mr Luzhin too. Not that I can’t stand him very much, but it was through him, all the same, that my quarrel with Marfa Petrovna came about, when I learned that she had cooked up this marriage. I wish now to see Avdotya Romanovna, through your mediation, and perhaps in your presence, to explain to her, in the first place, that not only will she get not the slightest advantage from Mr Luzhin, but there will even certainly be obvious harm. Then, having asked her forgiveness for all these recent unpleasantnesses, I would ask permission to offer her ten thousand roubles and so make easier the break with Mr Luzhin, a break which I am sure she would not be averse to herself, if only the possibility arose.”

“But you really, really are mad!” cried Raskolnikov, not so much angered as surprised. “How dare you talk like that!”

“I knew you would shout; but in the first place, though I am not rich, these ten thousand roubles are free, that is, I have absolutely, absolutely no need of them. If Avdotya Romanovna won’t accept them, I shall perhaps use them still more stupidly. That is one thing. Second: my conscience is perfectly at peace; I make the offer without any calculations. Believe it or not, but later both you and Avdotya Romanovna will find out. The whole point is that I really did cause your much-respected sister some trouble and unpleasantness; and so, feeling sincere repentance, I wish with all my heart, not to buy myself off, not to pay for the unpleasantness, but simply to do something to her advantage, on the grounds that I have not, after all, taken out a privilege to do nothing but evil. If there were even a millionth part of calculation in my offer, I would not make it so directly; and I would not offer only ten thousand, when only five weeks ago I offered her more. Besides, I may very, very soon be marrying a certain girl, and consequently all suspicions of any designs against Avdotya Romanovna must by that very fact be destroyed. In conclusion I will say that in marrying Mr Luzhin, Avdotya Romanovna is taking the very same money, only from another side... But don’t be angry, Rodion Romanovich; reason calmly and coolly.”

As he said this, Svidrigailov was himself extremely cool and calm.

“I ask you to finish,” said Raskolnikov. “In any case this is unforgivably insolent.”

“Not in the least. At that rate a man may do nothing but evil to another man in this world, and on the contrary has no right to do a crumb of good, because of empty accepted formalities. That is absurd. Why, if I, for example, died and left this sum to your sister in my will, surely she wouldn’t refuse to accept it then?”

“Very possibly.”

“Well, that she wouldn’t, sir. But then, no is no, so be it. Only ten thousand is a fine thing, on occasion. In any case, I will ask you to pass on what I have said to Avdotya Romanovna.”

“No, I will not pass it on.”

“In that case, Rodion Romanovich, I shall be forced to seek a personal meeting myself, and so to trouble her.”

“And if I pass it on, you will not seek a personal meeting?”

“Really, I don’t know what to tell you. I would very much like to see her once.”

“Don’t hope for it.”

“A pity. However, you don’t know me. Perhaps we shall become closer.”

“You think we shall become closer?”

“And why not?” said Svidrigailov with a smile, standing up and taking his hat. “It isn’t that I so very much wished to trouble you, and coming here I did not even count on much, though, to be sure, your face struck me this morning...”

“Where did you see me this morning?” Raskolnikov asked uneasily.

“By chance, sir... I keep feeling that there is something in you that suits mine... But don’t worry, I am not a bore; I got along with card sharpers, and I didn’t bore Prince Svirbey, a distant relation of mine and a grandee, and I managed to write about Raphael’s Madonna in Madame Prilukova’s album, and I lived with Marfa Petrovna for seven years without leaving the place, and in the old days I used to spend nights in Vyazemsky’s house on the Haymarket, and perhaps I shall go up in the balloon with Berg.”

“Well, very good, sir. Allow me to ask, will you be setting out on your journey soon?”

“What journey?”

“Why, that ‘voyage’... You said so yourself.”

“The voyage? Ah, yes!.. indeed, I spoke to you about a voyage... Well, that is a wide question... And if only you knew what you are asking about!” he added, and suddenly laughed loudly and shortly. “Perhaps instead of the voyage I shall get married; they are finding me a bride.”

“Here?”

“Yes.”

“When did you find time for that?”

“But I very much wish to see Avdotya Romanovna once. I ask it seriously. Well, goodbye... ah, yes! Here is what I forgot! Tell your sister, Rodion Romanovich, that she is mentioned in Marfa Petrovna’s will for three thousand. That is positively certain. Marfa Petrovna made the arrangement a week before her death, and it was done in my presence. In two or three weeks Avdotya Romanovna can have the money.”

“Are you telling the truth?”

“The truth. Pass it on. Well, sir, your servant. I am staying not very far from you, you know.”

As he went out, Svidrigailov ran into Razumikhin in the doorway.
