“‘And what have I to hide from you? Do I not know whom I am speaking to? Everything I have to say to you, you already know; I can read it in your eyes. And should I hide our secret from you? Perhaps you want to hear it from my own lips. Then listen. We are not with you, but with him: that is our secret! We have not been with you, but with him, for a long time now: eight centuries. Exactly eight centuries ago we took from him what you rejected with indignation, the last gift he offered you when he showed you all the kingdoms of the earth. We took Rome and the sword of Caesar from him, and proclaimed ourselves the only kings of the earth, though even now we have not managed to bring our work to completion. But whose fault is that? Oh, the work is only beginning, but it has begun. We shall have long to wait for its completion, and the earth will suffer much more, but we shall get there, and we shall be Caesars, and then we shall think about the universal happiness of mankind.

“‘And yet you could have taken the sword of Caesar even then. Why did you reject that last gift? If you had accepted that third counsel of the mighty spirit, you would have supplied everything that man seeks on earth: someone to bow down to, someone to entrust his conscience to, and a way for everyone to unite at last in one undisputed, common and harmonious anthill; for the need for universal union is the third and last torment of men. Mankind as a whole has always striven to organise itself universally. There have been many great nations with great histories, but the higher these nations were, the more unhappy they were, for they felt more strongly than others the need for a universal union of mankind. The great conquerors, the Tamerlanes and Genghis Khans, swept like a whirlwind across the earth, striving to conquer the universe, but even they, though without knowing it, expressed the same great need of mankind for universal and general union. Had you accepted the world and the purple of Caesar, you would have founded a universal kingdom and given universal peace. For who should rule over men if not those who rule over their consciences and in whose hands their bread is? So we took the sword of Caesar, and in taking it, of course, we rejected you and followed him.

“‘Oh, there will be centuries more of the lawlessness of free thought, of their science and their cannibalism, for having begun to build their Tower of Babel without us, they will end in cannibalism. But then the beast will crawl to us and lick our feet and spatter them with tears of blood from its eyes. And we shall sit upon the beast and raise the cup, and on it will be written: “Mystery!” And then, and only then, will the kingdom of peace and happiness come for men. You are proud of your chosen ones, but you have only the chosen, while we will give rest to all. And more than that: how many of those chosen ones, of the mighty ones who could have become chosen, have grown tired at last of waiting for you, and have carried and will carry the strength of their spirit and the warmth of their hearts to another field, and will end by raising their free banner against you? But it was you yourself who raised that banner. With us, everyone will be happy and will no longer rebel or destroy one another, as they do everywhere under your freedom.

“‘Oh, we shall convince them that they will only become free when they give up their freedom to us and submit to us. And shall we be right, or shall we be lying? They themselves will be convinced that we are right, for they will remember what horrors of slavery and confusion your freedom brought them to. Freedom, the free mind and science will lead them into such thickets and set them before such marvels and insoluble mysteries that some of them, the unruly and fierce, will destroy themselves; others, unruly but feeble, will destroy one another; and the rest, the feeble and unhappy, will crawl to our feet and cry out to us: “Yes, you were right, you alone possessed his secret, and we are coming back to you; save us from ourselves.” Receiving bread from us, they will see clearly, of course, that we take from them the bread they have made with their own hands in order to give it back to them, without any miracle; they will see that we have not turned stones into bread; but truly they will be more glad to receive it from our hands than of the bread itself! For they will remember only too well that before, without us, the very bread they made turned to stones in their hands, and when they came back to us, the very stones turned to bread in their hands. Only too well, only too well will they appreciate what it means to submit once and for all! And until men understand this, they will be unhappy.

“‘And who did most to bring about this misunderstanding, tell me? Who split the flock and scattered it on unknown paths? But the flock will gather again and submit again, and this time once and for all. Then we shall give them quiet, humble happiness, the happiness of weak creatures such as they were made. Oh, we shall persuade them at last not to be proud, for you raised them up and so taught them to be proud; we shall prove to them that they are weak, that they are only pitiful children, but that a child’s happiness is sweeter than any other. They will grow timid and look to us and huddle close to us in fear, like chicks to a hen. They will marvel at us and be in awe of us, and be proud that we are so mighty and so wise that we could tame such a turbulent flock of a thousand million. They will tremble limply before our anger, their minds will grow timid, their eyes will fill with tears like children’s and women’s; but just as easily, at a sign from us, they will pass to cheerfulness and laughter, bright joy and happy children’s songs. Yes, we shall make them work, but in their hours free from work we shall arrange their lives like a children’s game, with children’s songs, in chorus, with innocent dances. Oh, we shall even allow them sin; they are weak and powerless, and they will love us like children for allowing them to sin. We shall tell them that every sin will be redeemed if it is committed with our permission; we allow them to sin because we love them, and as for the punishment for those sins, very well, we shall take it on ourselves. And we shall take it on ourselves, and they will adore us as benefactors who have borne their sins before God. And they will have no secrets from us. We shall allow or forbid them to live with their wives and mistresses, to have or not to have children, all according to how obedient they are, and they will submit to us gladly and joyfully. The most agonising secrets of their conscience, all, all of them they will bring to us, and we shall decide everything, and they will trust our decision gladly, because it will spare them the great worry and the terrible present torment of deciding personally and freely.

“‘And everyone will be happy, all the millions of creatures, except the hundred thousand who rule over them. For only we, we who keep the secret, only we shall be unhappy. There will be thousands of millions of happy babes, and a hundred thousand sufferers who have taken upon themselves the curse of the knowledge of good and evil. Peacefully they will die, peacefully they will fade away in your name, and beyond the grave they will find only death. But we shall keep the secret, and for their own happiness we shall lure them on with the reward of heaven and eternity. For if there were anything in the other world, it would certainly not be for such as they.

“‘They say and prophesy that you will come and conquer again, come with your chosen ones, your proud and mighty ones; but we shall say that they saved only themselves, while we have saved everyone. They say that the harlot who sits upon the beast and holds the mystery in her hands will be put to shame, that the weak will rise up again, tear her purple robe and strip her “vile” body naked. But then I shall stand up and point out to you the thousands of millions of happy babes who have known no sin. And we, who took their sins upon ourselves for their happiness, we shall stand before you and say: “Judge us if you can and if you dare.” Know that I am not afraid of you. Know that I too was in the wilderness, that I too lived on locusts and roots, that I too blessed the freedom with which you blessed mankind, and I was preparing to stand among your chosen ones, among the mighty and strong, thirsting “to make up the number”. But I came to my senses and would not serve madness. I came back and joined the ranks of those who have corrected your work. I left the proud and came back to the humble, for the happiness of the humble. What I say to you will come to pass, and our kingdom will be built. I tell you again: tomorrow you will see this obedient flock, which at the first sign from me will rush to rake the hot coals up to your fire, on which I shall burn you for coming to get in our way. For if anyone has deserved our fire more than all, it is you. Tomorrow I shall burn you. Dixi: I have spoken.’”

Ivan stopped. He had grown heated as he talked, and talked with passion; when he had finished, he suddenly smiled.

Alyosha, who had listened to him all the while in silence, though towards the end, in extreme agitation, he had many times tried to interrupt his brother’s speech and had visibly held himself back, suddenly burst out as if he had sprung from his seat.

“But... that’s absurd!” he cried, flushing. “Your poem is praise of Jesus, not blame... as you meant it to be. And who will believe you about freedom? Is that, is that the way to understand it? Is that the idea in Orthodoxy?... That’s Rome, and not even the whole of Rome, that isn’t true; those are the worst of Catholicism, the inquisitors, the Jesuits!... And there could never be such a fantastic figure as your Inquisitor. What sins of men taken upon themselves? Who are these keepers of the mystery who have taken some curse upon themselves for the happiness of mankind? When has anyone ever seen them? We know the Jesuits, people speak badly of them, but are they what they are in your poem? They’re not like that at all, not at all... They’re simply a Roman army for a future universal earthly kingdom, with the Pontiff of Rome as emperor at its head... that’s their ideal, but without any mysteries or lofty sadness... The simplest lust for power, for filthy earthly goods, for enslavement... like a future serfdom, with themselves as the landowners... that’s all they are. Perhaps they don’t even believe in God. Your suffering Inquisitor is pure fantasy...”

“Wait, wait,” Ivan laughed, “how worked up you are. Fantasy, you say; very well! Of course it’s fantasy. But allow me: do you really think that the whole Catholic movement of the last centuries is nothing but a lust for power, for filthy earthly goods? Is it Father Paissy who teaches you that?”

“No, no, on the contrary, Father Paissy once said something rather like what you say... but of course not that, not that at all,” Alyosha caught himself.

“A precious piece of information, all the same, in spite of your ‘not that at all’. What I am asking you is why your Jesuits and inquisitors have joined together only for vile material goods. Why can’t there be among them even one sufferer, tormented by great sorrow and loving mankind? Look: suppose that among all those who want nothing but material, filthy goods there was even one, just one, like my old Inquisitor, who had himself eaten roots in the wilderness and raged to overcome his flesh so as to make himself free and perfect, who had loved mankind all his life, and who suddenly saw clearly and understood that it is no great moral bliss to reach perfection of the will if at the same time you are convinced that millions of God’s other creatures have been made only as a mockery, that they will never be able to cope with their freedom, that pitiful rebels will never grow into giants to complete the tower, that it was not for such geese that the great idealist dreamed of his harmony. Having understood all this, he went back and joined... the clever people. Couldn’t that happen?”

“Joined whom? What clever people?” cried Alyosha, almost in a passion. “They have no such intelligence, and no such mysteries and secrets... Unless it’s atheism, that’s their whole secret. Your Inquisitor doesn’t believe in God, that’s his whole secret!”

“And what if that is so! At last you’ve guessed it. Yes, that really is so, that really is the whole secret; but isn’t that suffering, at least for a man like him, who has spent his whole life in heroic struggle in the wilderness and has not been cured of his love for mankind? At the end of his days he becomes clearly convinced that only the counsels of the great and terrible spirit could bring these feeble rebels, ‘these unfinished trial creatures made in mockery’, into any kind of bearable order. And so, convinced of this, he sees that he must follow the guidance of the intelligent spirit, the terrible spirit of death and destruction, and for that accept lies and deceit and lead people consciously to death and destruction, deceiving them all the way so that they do not notice where they are being led, so that at least on the way these pitiful blind creatures may think themselves happy. And note: deceit in the name of the one in whose ideal the old man had so passionately believed all his life! Isn’t that a tragedy? And if even one such man stood at the head of that whole army ‘thirsting for power only for filthy goods’, wouldn’t even one be enough to make a tragedy? More than that: one such man at its head would be enough to give the whole Roman enterprise, with all its armies and Jesuits, its real guiding idea, the highest idea of that enterprise. I tell you frankly that I firmly believe that this single man has never been lacking among those at the head of the movement. Who knows, perhaps there have been such single men even among the Popes of Rome. Who knows, perhaps that cursed old man, who loves mankind so stubbornly and in his own way, exists even now in the form of a whole host of such single old men, and not by chance, but as an alliance, a secret league formed long ago to keep the secret, to keep it from the unhappy and feeble people, in order to make them happy. That must exist, and it must be so. I fancy that even the Freemasons have something of the same kind of mystery at their foundation, and that the Catholics hate the Freemasons so much because they see them as competitors, a splitting of the unity of the idea, when there must be one flock and one shepherd... But in defending my idea I am looking like an author who can’t stand your criticism. Enough of this.”

“Perhaps you are a Freemason yourself!” Alyosha suddenly burst out. “You don’t believe in God,” he added, but now with deep sorrow. It seemed to him, besides, that his brother was looking at him mockingly. “How does your poem end?” he asked suddenly, looking at the ground. “Or is it finished?”

“I meant to end it like this. When the Inquisitor falls silent, he waits for some time for his prisoner to answer him. His silence weighs on him. He saw that the prisoner listened to him all the time intently and quietly, looking him straight in the eyes and evidently not wishing to object to anything. The old man would like him to say something, even something bitter and terrible. But suddenly he goes up to the old man in silence and quietly kisses him on his bloodless, ninety-year-old lips. That is his whole answer. The old man shudders. Something stirs at the corners of his mouth; he goes to the door, opens it and says to him: ‘Go, and do not come again... do not come at all... never, never!’ And he lets him out into the dark streets of the city. The prisoner goes away.”

“And the old man?”

“The kiss glows in his heart, but the old man holds to his idea.”

“And you with him, you too?” Alyosha cried sorrowfully. Ivan laughed.

“But this is nonsense, Alyosha; it’s only the senseless poem of a senseless student who never wrote two lines of verse. Why do you take it so seriously? Surely you don’t think I’m going straight off to the Jesuits now, to join the ranks of the men who are correcting his work? Good Lord, what do I care! I told you: I only want to hold out until I’m thirty, and then, dash the cup to the floor!”

“And the sticky little leaves, and the precious graves, and the blue sky, and the woman you love! How will you live, what will you love them with?” Alyosha cried sorrowfully. “With such a hell in your heart and your head, is it possible? No, you are going away precisely to join them... and if not, you will kill yourself; you won’t be able to bear it!”

“There is a strength that can bear anything,” said Ivan, with a cold smile now.

“What strength?”

“The Karamazov strength... the strength of the Karamazov baseness.”

“To drown in depravity, to crush your soul in corruption, yes, yes?”

“Perhaps that too... only till I’m thirty perhaps I’ll escape it, and then...”

“How will you escape it? With what? With your ideas, it’s impossible.”

“Again, in the Karamazov way.”

“So that ‘everything is permitted’? Everything is permitted, is that it, is that it?”

Ivan frowned and suddenly turned strangely pale.

“Ah, you’ve picked up yesterday’s little phrase that offended Miusov so much... and that brother Dmitri so naively jumped in and repeated?” He smiled crookedly. “Yes, perhaps: ‘everything is permitted’, since the word has been said. I don’t take it back. And Mitya’s version isn’t bad either.”

Alyosha looked at him in silence.

“I thought, brother, as I was leaving, that I had you at least in all the world,” Ivan said suddenly, with unexpected feeling, “and now I see that there is no room for me in your heart either, my dear hermit. I won’t renounce the formula ‘everything is permitted’; well, so, will you renounce me for it? Will you?”

Alyosha stood up, went to him and quietly kissed him on the lips without a word.

“Literary theft!” cried Ivan, suddenly passing into a kind of rapture. “You stole that from my poem! Thank you, though. Get up, Alyosha, let’s go; it’s time, for both of us.”

They went out, but stopped at the steps of the tavern.

“Listen, Alyosha,” said Ivan in a firm voice, “if I really do have it in me to love the sticky little leaves, I shall love them only remembering you. It’s enough for me that you are somewhere here, and I shan’t lose the wish to live. Is that enough for you? Take it as a declaration of love, if you like. And now you go to the right and I go to the left, and enough, do you hear, enough. I mean, even if I didn’t leave tomorrow (I think I certainly shall) and we somehow met again, not another word to me on all these subjects. I earnestly beg you. And about brother Dmitri too, I particularly beg you, don’t even bring him up with me ever again,” he added suddenly with irritation. “Everything has been exhausted, everything has been talked over, hasn’t it? And I in my turn will make you a promise for this: when, at thirty, I want to ‘dash the cup to the floor’, then wherever you are I will come once more to talk it over with you... even from America, you may be sure. I’ll come specially. It will be very interesting to see you by then: what will you be like then? You see, a rather solemn promise. And really, perhaps we are saying goodbye for seven years, or ten. Well, go now to your Pater Seraphicus; he’s dying, after all; if he dies without you, you may be angry with me for keeping you. Goodbye; kiss me once more, like that, and go...”

Ivan suddenly turned and went his way, without looking back again. It was like the way brother Dmitri had left Alyosha the day before, though yesterday had been quite different. This strange little observation flashed like an arrow through Alyosha’s sad mind, sad and sorrowful at that moment. He waited a little, looking after his brother. For some reason he suddenly noticed that brother Ivan walked with a kind of sway, and that his right shoulder, seen from behind, looked lower than his left. He had never noticed that before. But suddenly he too turned and almost ran towards the monastery. It was getting very dark, and he felt almost frightened; something new was growing in him, something he could not have explained. The wind rose again, as it had the day before, and the ancient pines rustled gloomily round him as he entered the hermitage wood. He was almost running. “‘Pater Seraphicus’: where did he get that name? Where from?” flashed through Alyosha’s mind. “Ivan, poor Ivan, and when shall I see you now?... Here is the hermitage, Lord! Yes, yes, it is he, it is Pater Seraphicus; he will save me... from him, and forever!”

Several times later in his life he remembered with great bewilderment how he could, after parting from Ivan, so completely forget about his brother Dmitri, whom that very morning, only a few hours before, he had resolved to find without fail, and not to give up until he had, even if it meant not returning to the monastery that night.
