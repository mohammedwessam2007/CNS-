> Vengeance is mine; I will repay.
> — Romans 12:19, the novel’s epigraph

## Part One · Chapter I

All happy families are alike; each unhappy family is unhappy in its own way.

Everything was in confusion in the Oblonskys’ house. The wife had found out that the husband was having an affair with the French governess who used to live in their house, and she had announced to her husband that she could not go on living under the same roof with him. This state of things had now lasted for three days, and it was felt as a torment by the husband and wife themselves, by every member of the family, and by the whole household. Every member of the family and of the household felt that there was no sense in their living together, and that people who happen to meet at any wayside inn are more bound to one another than they, the family and household of the Oblonskys. The wife did not leave her rooms; the husband had not been home for three days. The children ran all over the house as if lost; the English governess had quarrelled with the housekeeper and written a note to a friend asking her to find her a new place; the cook had walked out of the yard the day before, at the very hour of dinner; the kitchen-maid and the coachman had asked for their wages.

On the third day after the quarrel, Prince Stepan Arkadyich Oblonsky, Stiva, as he was called in society, woke at his usual hour, that is, at eight o’clock in the morning, not in his wife’s bedroom but in his study, on the morocco-leather sofa. He turned his full, well-cared-for body over on the springs of the sofa, as though he wished to go back to sleep for a long while, hugged the pillow tightly from the other side and pressed his cheek to it; but suddenly he jumped up, sat on the sofa and opened his eyes.

“Yes, yes, how did it go?” he thought, remembering his dream. “Yes, how did it go? Yes! Alabin was giving a dinner in Darmstadt; no, not in Darmstadt, but something American. Yes, but Darmstadt was in America there. Yes, Alabin was giving a dinner on glass tables, yes, and the tables were singing Il mio tesoro, and not Il mio tesoro but something better, and there were some little decanters, and they were women too,” he remembered.

Stepan Arkadyich’s eyes shone merrily, and he sat thinking and smiling. “Yes, it was good, very good. There was a great deal more there that was excellent, only you can’t say it in words, and you can’t even put it into thoughts when you are awake.” And, noticing a strip of light that had made its way in at the side of one of the cloth blinds, he cheerfully swung his feet off the sofa, felt about with them for the slippers his wife had embroidered for him (a birthday present the year before), trimmed in gold morocco, and, by an old habit of nine years, without getting up, reached out his hand to the place where his dressing-gown hung in the bedroom. And then he suddenly remembered how and why he was sleeping not in his wife’s bedroom but in the study; the smile vanished from his face, and he wrinkled his forehead.

“Ah, ah, ah! Aaah!” he groaned, remembering everything that had happened. And all the details of the quarrel with his wife rose up again before his imagination, all the hopelessness of his position, and, most tormenting of all, his own guilt.

“Yes! She won’t forgive, and she can’t forgive. And the most terrible thing of all is that I am the cause of it all; I am the cause, and yet I am not to blame. That is where the whole drama lies,” he thought. “Ah, ah, ah!” he kept muttering in despair, remembering the impressions of the quarrel that were hardest for him to bear.

Most unpleasant of all had been that first minute when, coming home from the theatre, cheerful and pleased with himself, with a huge pear for his wife in his hand, he had not found his wife in the drawing-room; to his surprise had not found her in the study either; and at last had seen her in the bedroom, with the unlucky note that had revealed everything in her hand.

She, this Dolly, eternally anxious and fussing and not very clever, as he considered her, was sitting motionless with the note in her hand, and looking at him with an expression of horror, despair and anger.

“What is this? This?” she kept asking, pointing to the note.

And in this memory, as so often happens, what tormented Stepan Arkadyich was not so much the event itself as the way he had answered his wife’s words.

What happened to him at that minute is what happens to people when they are unexpectedly caught in something too shameful. He had not managed to prepare his face for the position he found himself in before his wife after the discovery of his guilt. Instead of taking offence, denying, justifying himself, begging forgiveness, even remaining indifferent (anything would have been better than what he did!), his face quite involuntarily (“reflexes of the brain,” thought Stepan Arkadyich, who was fond of physiology), quite involuntarily smiled all of a sudden its habitual, kindly and therefore foolish smile.

That foolish smile he could not forgive himself. Seeing it, Dolly shuddered as if from a physical pain, burst out, with the hot temper that was hers, into a torrent of cruel words, and ran out of the room. Since then she had refused to see her husband.

“That foolish smile is to blame for everything,” thought Stepan Arkadyich.

“But what is to be done? What is to be done?” he said to himself in despair, and found no answer.

## Chapter II

Stepan Arkadyich was a truthful man in his dealings with himself. He could not deceive himself and persuade himself that he repented of what he had done. He could not now repent of what he had repented of some six years before, when he was first unfaithful to his wife. He could not repent that he, a handsome, amorous man of thirty-four, was not in love with his wife, the mother of five living and two dead children, who was only a year younger than he. He repented only that he had not known how to hide it better from his wife. But he felt the whole weight of his position, and he pitied his wife, the children and himself. Perhaps he would have managed to hide his sins from his wife better if he had expected the news to affect her so. He had never thought the question out clearly, but he had a vague notion that his wife had long suspected him of being unfaithful and was shutting her eyes to it. It even seemed to him that she, a worn-out, ageing woman, no longer pretty and in no way remarkable, simple, merely a kind mother of a family, ought out of a sense of fairness to be indulgent. It turned out to be quite the opposite.

“Oh, it’s terrible! Oh dear, oh dear! Terrible!” Stepan Arkadyich kept repeating to himself, and could think of nothing. “And how good it all was until this, how well we lived! She was contented, happy with the children; I never stood in her way in anything; I let her fuss over the children and the house as she liked. True, it isn’t nice that she was a governess in our own house. It isn’t nice! There is something trivial, something vulgar, in making up to one’s own governess. But what a governess!” (He vividly remembered Mlle Roland’s mischievous black eyes and her smile.) “But after all, while she was in our house I allowed myself nothing. And the worst of it is that she is already… It all has to happen as if on purpose! Oh dear, oh dear! But what, what is to be done?”

There was no answer, except that general answer which life gives to all the most complicated and insoluble questions. That answer is: one must live for the needs of the day, that is, forget oneself. To forget oneself in sleep was no longer possible, at least not until night; it was no longer possible to go back to the music the decanter-women had been singing; so one must forget oneself in the sleep of life.

“We shall see,” Stepan Arkadyich said to himself, and, getting up, he put on his grey dressing-gown with its pale blue silk lining, tied the tassels in a knot, drew a good deep breath into the broad cage of his chest, and with his usual brisk step, on his turned-out feet that carried his full body so lightly, went to the window, raised the blind and rang loudly. At the ring there came in at once his old friend, the valet Matvei, carrying his clothes, his boots and a telegram. After Matvei came the barber too, with his shaving things.

“Are there papers from the office?” asked Stepan Arkadyich, taking the telegram and sitting down at the mirror.

“On the table,” answered Matvei; he glanced at his master questioningly, with sympathy, and, after waiting a little, added with a sly smile: “They came from the carriage-hirer.”

Stepan Arkadyich said nothing and only looked at Matvei in the mirror; in the look with which their eyes met in the mirror it could be seen how well they understood each other. Stepan Arkadyich’s look seemed to ask: “Why do you tell me that? Don’t you know?”

Matvei put his hands in the pockets of his jacket, set one foot aside and looked at his master in silence, good-naturedly, smiling just a little.

“I told him to come next Sunday, and until then not to trouble you or himself for nothing,” he said, in a phrase he had evidently prepared.

Stepan Arkadyich understood that Matvei wanted to make a joke and draw attention to himself. Tearing open the telegram, he read it, guessing at the words, which were garbled as always, and his face lit up.

“Matvei, my sister Anna Arkadyevna will be here tomorrow,” he said, stopping for a moment the barber’s glossy, plump little hand, which was clearing a pink road between his long curly side-whiskers.

“Thank God,” said Matvei, showing by this answer that he understood the meaning of this visit just as well as his master did, that is, that Anna Arkadyevna, Stepan Arkadyich’s favourite sister, might help to bring husband and wife together again.

“Alone, or with her husband?” asked Matvei.

Stepan Arkadyich could not speak, since the barber was busy with his upper lip, and he raised one finger. Matvei nodded in the mirror.

“Alone. Shall I get the rooms upstairs ready?”

“Tell Darya Alexandrovna, and do as she says.”

“Darya Alexandrovna?” Matvei repeated, as though in doubt.

“Yes, tell her. And here, take the telegram, give it to her, and then tell me what she says.”

“You want to try it out,” Matvei understood; but all he said was:

“Very good, sir.”

Stepan Arkadyich was already washed and combed and was getting ready to dress when Matvei, stepping slowly in his creaking boots over the soft carpet, came back into the room with the telegram in his hand. The barber had gone.

“Darya Alexandrovna told me to say that she is going away. Let him do as he pleases, that is, as you please,” he said, laughing only with his eyes; and, putting his hands in his pockets and tilting his head to one side, he fixed his eyes on his master.

Stepan Arkadyich was silent for a while. Then a kind and somewhat pitiful smile appeared on his handsome face.

“Eh? Matvei?” he said, shaking his head.

“Never mind, sir, it will all shape itself,” said Matvei.

“Shape itself?”

“Just so, sir.”

“You think so? Who is that there?” asked Stepan Arkadyich, hearing the rustle of a woman’s dress outside the door.

“It’s me, sir,” said a firm and pleasant woman’s voice, and from behind the door appeared the stern, pock-marked face of Matryona Filimonovna, the nurse.

“Well, what is it, Matryosha?” asked Stepan Arkadyich, going out to her at the door.

Although Stepan Arkadyich was entirely in the wrong towards his wife and felt it himself, almost everyone in the house, even the nurse, Darya Alexandrovna’s chief friend, was on his side.

“Well, what is it?” he said dejectedly.

“You go to her, sir, and beg her pardon once more. God willing, it will come right. She is suffering terribly, it’s a pity to look at her, and the whole house has gone topsy-turvy. You must have pity on the children, sir. Beg her pardon, sir. What else is there to do! If you like sledging, you must like pulling the sledge…”

“But she won’t see me…”

“You do your part. God is merciful; pray to God, sir, pray to God.”

“Well, all right, off you go,” said Stepan Arkadyich, suddenly blushing. “Well, let us get dressed,” he said, turning to Matvei, and threw off his dressing-gown decisively.

Matvei was already holding the shirt ready, gathered up like a horse’s collar, blowing away something invisible from it, and with evident pleasure he clothed his master’s well-tended body in it.

## Chapter III

When he was dressed, Stepan Arkadyich sprinkled himself with scent, pulled down the cuffs of his shirt, with a practised movement distributed among his pockets his cigarettes, his pocket-book, his matches, and his watch with its double chain and seals, and, shaking out his handkerchief, feeling himself clean, fragrant, healthy and physically cheerful in spite of his misfortune, he walked out, with a slight spring at each step, into the dining-room, where his coffee was already waiting for him, and beside the coffee his letters and the papers from the office.

Stepan Arkadyich sat down and read his letters. One was very unpleasant: it was from a merchant who was buying a forest on his wife’s estate. The forest had to be sold; but now, until he was reconciled with his wife, there could be no question of that. What was most unpleasant here was that a money interest was being mixed into the coming business of his reconciliation with his wife. And the thought that he might be guided by this interest, that he might seek reconciliation with his wife in order to sell this forest, that thought offended him.

When he had finished the letters, Stepan Arkadyich drew the office papers towards him, quickly leafed through two files, made a few notes with a big pencil, and, pushing the files aside, turned to his coffee; over his coffee he unfolded the morning paper, still damp, and began to read it.

Stepan Arkadyich took and read a liberal newspaper, not an extreme one, but of the tendency held by the majority. And although neither science nor art nor politics really interested him, he held firmly to the views on all these subjects held by the majority and by his paper, and changed them only when the majority changed them; or, to put it better, he did not change them, but they changed in him of themselves, without his noticing.

Stepan Arkadyich did not choose his tendency or his views; these tendencies and views came to him of themselves, exactly as he did not choose the shape of his hat or his frock-coat but took those that were being worn. And for him, living in a certain society, with the need for some activity of thought that usually develops in the years of maturity, having views was just as necessary as having a hat. If there was any reason why he preferred the liberal tendency to the conservative one, which many people of his circle also held, it was not that he found the liberal tendency more reasonable, but that it came closer to his way of life. The liberal party said that everything in Russia was bad; and indeed Stepan Arkadyich had a great many debts and decidedly too little money. The liberal party said that marriage was an outworn institution and must be rebuilt; and indeed family life gave Stepan Arkadyich little pleasure and forced him to lie and pretend, which was so contrary to his nature. The liberal party said, or rather implied, that religion was only a bridle for the barbarous part of the population; and indeed Stepan Arkadyich could not stand through even a short church service without pain in his legs, and could not understand what all those dreadful and high-flown words about the other world were for, when life in this one could be very jolly. At the same time Stepan Arkadyich, who loved a merry joke, liked now and then to puzzle some meek person by saying that if one was going to be proud of one’s ancestry, one ought not to stop at Rurik and disown the first founder of the line, the ape. And so the liberal tendency became a habit with Stepan Arkadyich, and he loved his newspaper, as he loved a cigar after dinner, for the light mist it produced in his head. He read the leading article, which explained that in our time it was quite pointless to raise an outcry that radicalism threatened to swallow up all the conservative elements and that the government was obliged to take measures to crush the hydra of revolution; that, on the contrary, “in our opinion the danger lies not in an imaginary hydra of revolution, but in the obstinacy of traditionalism, which holds back progress,” and so on. He read another article too, a financial one, in which Bentham and Mill were mentioned and fine little pins were stuck into the ministry. With the quickness of mind that was his, he understood the point of every pin: from whom and at whom and on what occasion it was aimed; and that, as always, gave him a certain pleasure. But today the pleasure was poisoned by the memory of Matryona Filimonovna’s advice and of how badly things stood in the house. He read, too, that Count Beust was said to have travelled to Wiesbaden, and that there need be no more grey hair, and of the sale of a light carriage, and an offer from a young person; but this news did not give him, as it used to, a quiet ironic pleasure.

When he had finished the paper, a second cup of coffee and a white roll with butter, he got up, shook the crumbs of the roll off his waistcoat and, squaring his broad chest, smiled joyfully, not because there was anything especially pleasant in his heart: the joyful smile came from a good digestion.

But this joyful smile at once reminded him of everything, and he grew thoughtful.

Two children’s voices (Stepan Arkadyich recognized the voices of Grisha, the younger boy, and Tanya, the elder girl) were heard outside the door. They were pulling something along and had dropped it.

“I told you you can’t put passengers on the roof,” the girl was shouting in English. “Now pick them up!”

“Everything is in confusion,” thought Stepan Arkadyich. “There are the children running about on their own.” And, going to the door, he called them.

They dropped the box that stood for a train and came in to their father.

The girl, her father’s favourite, ran in boldly, hugged him and, laughing, hung on his neck, delighting as always in the familiar smell of scent that came from his whiskers. Kissing him at last on his face, which was flushed from stooping and shining with tenderness, the girl unclasped her hands and wanted to run back; but her father held her.

“How is mama?” said her father, stroking his daughter’s smooth, soft little neck. “Good morning,” he said, smiling at the boy, who was greeting him.

He knew that he loved the boy less, and he always tried to be even-handed; but the boy felt it, and did not answer his father’s cold smile with a smile.

“Mama? She’s up,” answered the girl.

Stepan Arkadyich sighed. “That means she didn’t sleep all night again,” he thought.

“Well, is she cheerful?”

The girl knew that there had been a quarrel between her father and mother, and that her mother could not be cheerful, and that her father must know it, and that he was pretending when he asked about it so lightly. And she blushed for her father. He understood this at once and blushed too.

“I don’t know,” she said. “She didn’t tell us to do lessons; she told us to go for a walk with Miss Hull to grandmama’s.”

“Well, run along, my little Tanchurochka. Oh yes, wait a moment,” he said, still holding her and stroking her tender little hand.

He took down from the mantelpiece, where he had put it the day before, a little box of sweets, and gave her two, picking out her favourites, a chocolate one and a fondant.

“For Grisha?” said the girl, pointing to the chocolate one.

“Yes, yes.” And, stroking her little shoulder once more, he kissed her at the roots of her hair and on the neck, and let her go.

“The carriage is ready,” said Matvei. “And there is a woman with a petition,” he added.

“Has she been here long?” asked Stepan Arkadyich.

“Half an hour or so.”

“How many times have I told you to let me know at once!”

“You must be let have your coffee in peace at least,” said Matvei, in that friendly, gruff tone with which it was impossible to be angry.

“Well, show her in quickly,” said Oblonsky, frowning with vexation.

The petitioner, a Captain Kalinin’s wife, was asking for something impossible and senseless; but Stepan Arkadyich, as was his custom, sat her down, listened to her attentively without interrupting, and gave her detailed advice on whom to apply to and how, and even, briskly and fluently, in his large, sprawling, handsome and clear hand, wrote her a little note to a person who might help her. Having sent the captain’s wife away, Stepan Arkadyich took his hat and stopped, trying to remember whether he had forgotten anything. It turned out that he had forgotten nothing except what he wanted to forget: his wife.

“Ah yes!” He lowered his head, and his handsome face took on a melancholy expression. “To go, or not to go?” he said to himself. And an inner voice told him that he ought not to go, that nothing could come of it but falseness; that it was impossible to set their relations right, to mend them, because it was impossible to make her attractive again and capable of arousing love, or to make him an old man incapable of loving. Nothing but falseness and lies could come of it now; and falseness and lies were against his nature.

“But some time it has to be done; it can’t stay like this, after all,” he said, trying to give himself courage. He straightened his chest, took out a cigarette, lit it, drew on it twice, threw it into the mother-of-pearl shell that served as an ashtray, crossed the gloomy drawing-room with quick steps, and opened the other door, into his wife’s bedroom.
