## Chapter X

When Levin went into the hotel with Oblonsky, he could not help noticing a certain peculiarity of expression, a kind of restrained radiance, on the face and in the whole figure of Stepan Arkadyich. Oblonsky took off his overcoat and, with his hat tilted over one ear, went through into the dining-room, giving orders to the Tatar waiters in tailcoats with napkins over their arms, who clung to him. Bowing right and left to acquaintances who turned up here too, as everywhere, and greeted him joyfully, he went up to the buffet, took a little fish after his vodka, and said something to the painted Frenchwoman sitting at the counter, all ribbons, lace and ringlets, that made even this Frenchwoman laugh sincerely. Levin did not drink any vodka only because this Frenchwoman offended him: she seemed made up entirely of other people’s hair, poudre de riz and vinaigre de toilette. He moved hastily away from her, as from a dirty place. His whole soul was overflowing with the memory of Kitty, and a smile of triumph and happiness shone in his eyes.

“This way, your excellency, if you please; no one will disturb you here, your excellency,” said a particularly clinging old Tatar, whitish, with broad hips and the tails of his coat parting over them. “Your hat, your excellency, if you please,” he said to Levin, attending to Stepan Arkadyich’s guest too, as a mark of respect to Stepan Arkadyich.

In an instant he had spread a fresh cloth over the round table, which already had a cloth on it, under a bronze wall-lamp, drew up the velvet chairs, and stood before Stepan Arkadyich with a napkin and the bill of fare in his hands, awaiting orders.

“If you wish, your excellency, a private room will be free directly: Prince Golitsyn with a lady. Fresh oysters have come in.”

“Ah! Oysters.”

Stepan Arkadyich thought for a moment.

“Shall we change the plan, Levin?” he said, stopping his finger on the bill of fare. And his face expressed serious perplexity. “Are the oysters good? Mind, now!”

“Flensburg ones, your excellency; there are no Ostend ones.”

“Flensburg is all very well, but are they fresh?”

“They came in yesterday, sir.”

“Well then, shall we begin with oysters, and then change the whole plan? Eh?”

“I don’t mind. For me the best thing of all is cabbage soup and kasha; but they won’t have that here.”

“Kasha à la russe, would you like?” said the Tatar, bending over Levin like a nurse over a child.

“No, seriously, whatever you choose will be good. I’ve been skating and I’m hungry. And don’t think,” he added, noticing a look of displeasure on Oblonsky’s face, “that I don’t appreciate your choice. I shall enjoy a good dinner.”

“I should think so! Say what you like, it is one of the pleasures of life,” said Stepan Arkadyich. “Well then, my good fellow, give us two dozen oysters, or that’s too few, three dozen; soup with vegetables…”

“Printanière,” the Tatar put in. But Stepan Arkadyich evidently did not want to give him the pleasure of calling the dishes by their French names.

“With vegetables, you know? Then turbot with a thick sauce, then… roast beef; and mind it’s good. And capons, perhaps, and then some preserved fruit.”

The Tatar, remembering Stepan Arkadyich’s way of not calling the dishes by the French bill of fare, did not repeat after him, but gave himself the pleasure of repeating the whole order from the bill: “Soupe printanière, turbot sauce Beaumarchais, poularde à l’estragon, macédoine de fruits…” and at once, as if on springs, laying down one bound bill of fare and snatching up another, the wine list, he handed it to Stepan Arkadyich.

“What shall we drink?”

“Whatever you like, only not much; champagne,” said Levin.

“What? To begin with? But, after all, why not. Do you like the white seal?”

“Cachet blanc,” the Tatar put in.

“Well, bring us that brand with the oysters, and then we shall see.”

“Very good, sir. And what table wine would you like?”

“Bring Nuits. No, better the classic Chablis.”

“Very good, sir. Will you have your usual cheese?”

“Yes, Parmesan. Or do you like another?”

“No, it’s all the same to me,” said Levin, unable to hold back his smiles.

And the Tatar, the tails of his coat flying over his broad hips, ran off, and five minutes later flew in with a dish of opened oysters on their mother-of-pearl shells and a bottle between his fingers.

Stepan Arkadyich crumpled the starched napkin, tucked it into his waistcoat, and, settling his arms comfortably, set to on the oysters.

“Not bad,” he said, tearing the slippery oysters from their pearly shells with a little silver fork and swallowing them one after another. “Not bad,” he repeated, lifting his moist, shining eyes now to Levin, now to the Tatar.

Levin ate the oysters too, though white bread and cheese would have pleased him more. But he was admiring Oblonsky. Even the Tatar, who had unscrewed the cork and was pouring the sparkling wine into the wide, thin glasses, kept glancing at Stepan Arkadyich with a noticeable smile of pleasure as he set his white tie straight.

“You don’t much like oysters?” said Stepan Arkadyich, emptying his glass. “Or are you worried about something? Eh?”

He wanted Levin to be cheerful. But it was not that Levin was not cheerful; he was ill at ease. With what was in his soul, he felt uneasy and awkward in a restaurant, among private rooms where people dined with ladies, amid all this running about and bustle; these surroundings of bronzes, mirrors, gaslight, Tatars, all of it offended him. He was afraid of soiling what filled his soul to overflowing.

“I? Yes, I am worried; but besides that, all this makes me feel awkward,” he said. “You can’t imagine how wild all this is to me, a countryman, like the nails of that gentleman I saw in your office…”

“Yes, I saw that poor Grinevich’s nails interested you very much,” said Stepan Arkadyich, laughing.

“I can’t help it,” answered Levin. “Do try to get inside me, to take the countryman’s point of view. In the country we try to keep our hands in such a state that it is convenient to work with them; for that we cut our nails, and sometimes roll up our sleeves. And here people purposely let their nails grow as long as they will stay on, and hook on saucers by way of cuff-links, so that they can’t do anything at all with their hands.”

Stepan Arkadyich smiled merrily.

“Yes, it is a sign that he has no need of rough labour. It’s his mind that works…”

“Perhaps. But still it is wild to me, just as it is wild to me now that we countrymen try to eat our fill as quickly as possible, so as to be able to do our work, while you and I try to put off eating our fill as long as possible, and to that end we eat oysters…”

“Why, of course,” Stepan Arkadyich took him up. “But that is just the aim of civilization: to make everything a source of enjoyment.”

“Well, if that is the aim, I would rather be a savage.”

“You are a savage as it is. All you Levins are savages.”

Levin sighed. He remembered his brother Nikolai, and he felt ashamed and pained, and frowned; but Oblonsky began speaking of a subject that at once drew him away.

“Well, are you going to our people this evening, to the Shcherbatskys, I mean?” he said, pushing away the empty rough shells, drawing the cheese towards him, and with his eyes shining significantly.

“Yes, I shall certainly go,” answered Levin. “Though it seemed to me that the princess invited me unwillingly.”

“What nonsense! It’s her manner… Come on, my dear fellow, the soup!… It’s her manner, the grande dame,” said Stepan Arkadyich. “I shall come too, but I have to go to a choir rehearsal at Countess Banina’s first. Well, how can you say you’re not a savage? How else can one explain your suddenly vanishing from Moscow? The Shcherbatskys kept asking me about you, as though I ought to know. And I know only one thing: you always do what nobody else does.”

“Yes,” said Levin, slowly and with emotion. “You’re right, I am a savage. Only my savagery lies not in my having gone away but in my having come back now. Now I have come…”

“Oh, what a lucky man you are!” Stepan Arkadyich took him up, looking into Levin’s eyes.

“Why?”

“‘I know a mettled horse by the brand upon his hide, and a youth in love I know by the look in his eyes,’” declaimed Stepan Arkadyich. “Everything is before you.”

“And is it all behind you, then?”

“No, not behind me, but you have the future, and I have the present, and the present only so-so, a bit of this and a bit of that.”

“How so?”

“Oh, not good. Well, I don’t want to talk about myself, and besides, it can’t all be explained,” said Stepan Arkadyich. “So what have you come to Moscow for?… Here, take this away!” he called to the Tatar.

“Can’t you guess?” answered Levin, not taking his eyes, which shone from the depths, off Stepan Arkadyich.

“I can guess, but I can’t begin to talk about it. By that alone you can see whether I guess right or wrong,” said Stepan Arkadyich, looking at Levin with a subtle smile.

“Well, what do you say to me?” said Levin in a trembling voice, feeling that all the muscles of his face were trembling. “How do you look at it?”

Stepan Arkadyich slowly drank his glass of Chablis, not taking his eyes off Levin.

“I?” said Stepan Arkadyich. “There is nothing I should like better than that, nothing. It is the best thing that could happen.”

“But you are not mistaken? You know what we are talking about?” said Levin, fastening his eyes on his companion. “You think it is possible?”

“I think it is possible. Why should it be impossible?”

“No, do you really think it is possible? No, tell me everything you think! Well, and if, if a refusal awaits me?… And I am even sure…”

“Why do you think that?” said Stepan Arkadyich, smiling at his agitation.

“That’s how it seems to me sometimes. It would be terrible, you know, both for me and for her.”

“Well, in any case there is nothing terrible in it for the girl. Every girl is proud of a proposal.”

“Yes, every girl, but not she.”

Stepan Arkadyich smiled. He knew this feeling of Levin’s so well; he knew that for him all the girls in the world were divided into two sorts: one sort was all the girls in the world except her, and these had every human weakness and were very ordinary girls; the other sort was she alone, who had no weaknesses at all and was above everything human.

“Wait, take some sauce,” he said, holding back Levin’s hand, which was pushing the sauce away.

Levin obediently helped himself to sauce, but he would not let Stepan Arkadyich eat.

“No, wait, wait,” he said. “You must understand that for me this is a question of life and death. I have never spoken of it to anyone. And there is no one I can speak to about it as I can to you. You and I are strangers in everything, you know: different tastes, views, everything; but I know that you love me and understand me, and that is why I love you terribly. But for God’s sake be perfectly frank.”

“I am telling you what I think,” said Stepan Arkadyich, smiling. “But I’ll tell you more: my wife is the most wonderful woman…” Stepan Arkadyich sighed, remembering how things stood with his wife, and, after a moment’s silence, went on: “She has the gift of foresight. She sees through people; but that is not all; she knows what is going to happen, especially in the matter of marriages. She foretold, for instance, that Princess Shakhovskaya would marry Brenteln. No one would believe it, but so it turned out. And she is on your side.”

“How do you mean?”

“I mean that not only does she love you; she says that Kitty will certainly be your wife.”

At these words Levin’s face suddenly lit up with a smile, the kind that is close to tears of tenderness.

“She says that!” cried Levin. “I always said she was a darling, your wife. Well, that’s enough, enough talking about it,” he said, getting up from his seat.

“All right, but do sit down; here’s the soup.”

But Levin could not sit. He walked twice with his firm steps up and down the little cage of a room, blinked his eyes so that his tears should not be seen, and only then sat down at the table again.

“You must understand,” he said, “that this is not love. I have been in love, but this is not that. It is not my feeling, but some outside force that has taken possession of me. I went away, you see, because I decided that it could not be, you understand, like a happiness that does not happen on earth; but I have struggled with myself, and I see that without it there is no life. And it must be decided…”

“Then why did you go away?”

“Ah, wait! Ah, so many thoughts! So much to ask! Listen. You can’t imagine what you have done for me by what you have said. I am so happy that I have even become horrid; I have forgotten everything… I learned today that my brother Nikolai… you know, he is here… I had forgotten about him too. It seems to me that he is happy too. It is a kind of madness. But one thing is terrible… You have married, you know this feeling… The terrible thing is that we, who are old, with a past already… not of love, but of sins… suddenly draw close to a pure, innocent being; it is loathsome, and so one cannot help feeling unworthy.”

“Well, you haven’t many sins.”

“Ah, all the same,” said Levin, “all the same: ‘and with loathing reading over my life, I tremble and I curse, and bitterly complain…’ Yes.”

“What can one do? That is how the world is made,” said Stepan Arkadyich.

“There is one comfort, as in that prayer I have always loved: forgive me not according to my deserts, but according to Thy mercy. And so she too can only forgive.”
