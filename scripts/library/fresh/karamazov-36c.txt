## Chapter III · Ilyushechka’s Funeral. The Speech at the Stone

He really was late. They had been waiting for him and had even decided to carry the pretty little coffin, decked with flowers, to the church without him. It was the coffin of Ilyushechka, the poor boy. He had died two days after Mitya’s sentence. At the gate of the house Alyosha was met by the shouts of the boys, Ilyusha’s schoolfellows. They had all been waiting for him impatiently and were glad that he had come at last. About twelve of them had gathered, all with their satchels and bags over their shoulders. “Papa will cry; stay with papa,” Ilyusha had bidden them as he was dying, and the boys had remembered it. At their head was Kolya Krasotkin.

“How glad I am you have come, Karamazov!” he exclaimed, holding out his hand to Alyosha. “It is terrible here. Really, it is hard to look at. Snegiryov isn’t drunk; we know for certain he hasn’t had anything today; but he seems drunk… I am always firm, but this is terrible. Karamazov, if I am not keeping you, just one more question before you go in?”

“What is it, Kolya?” Alyosha stopped.

“Is your brother innocent or guilty? Did he kill his father, or did the lackey? Whatever you say, so it will be. I haven’t slept for four nights over this idea.”

“The lackey killed him, and my brother is innocent,” Alyosha answered.

“That’s what I say too!” the boy Smurov suddenly shouted.

“So he will perish an innocent victim for the truth!” exclaimed Kolya. “Though he is ruined, he is happy! I am ready to envy him!”

“What are you saying? How can you? And why?” exclaimed Alyosha in surprise.

“Oh, if only I too could sacrifice myself for the truth some day!” Kolya said with enthusiasm.

“But not in such a case, not with such disgrace, not with such horror!” said Alyosha.

“Of course… I should like to die for all mankind; and as for the disgrace, it doesn’t matter: let our names perish. I respect your brother!”

“And so do I!” suddenly and quite unexpectedly shouted from the crowd that very boy who had once announced that he knew who founded Troy, and, having shouted it, he blushed to his ears like a peony, exactly as he had then.

Alyosha went into the room. In a blue coffin trimmed with white frills lay Ilyusha, his little hands folded and his eyes closed. The features of his thin face had hardly changed at all, and, strangely, there was almost no smell from the body. The expression of his face was serious and as if thoughtful. His hands, crossed on his breast, were especially beautiful, as if carved out of marble. Flowers had been put in his hands, and the whole coffin was already decked inside and out with flowers, sent at dawn by Lise Khokhlakova. But flowers had come from Katerina Ivanovna too, and when Alyosha opened the door, the captain, with a bunch of flowers in his trembling hands, was strewing them again over his dear boy. He hardly glanced at Alyosha as he came in, and he did not want to look at anyone, not even at his weeping, crazed wife, his “mamochka”, who kept trying to raise herself on her bad legs and look more closely at her dead boy. As for Ninochka, the children had lifted her in her chair and moved her right up to the coffin. She sat with her head pressed against it, and she too must have been weeping quietly. Snegiryov’s face looked animated, but as if bewildered, and at the same time embittered. There was something half-crazed in his gestures and in the words that broke from him. “Batyushka, dear batyushka!” he exclaimed every minute, looking at Ilyusha. He had had the habit, while Ilyusha was alive, of saying to him tenderly: “Batyushka, dear batyushka!”, my little father, my dear.

“Papa, give me some flowers too; take one from his little hand, this little white one, and give it to me!” the crazed “mamochka” begged, sobbing. Whether she liked the little white rose in Ilyusha’s hands so much, or wanted to take a flower from his hands as a keepsake, she began to fidget all over, stretching out her hands for the flower.

“I won’t give anyone anything, nothing!” Snegiryov exclaimed hard-heartedly. “They are his flowers, not yours. Everything is his; nothing is yours!”

“Papa, give mama a flower!” Ninochka suddenly raised her tear-stained face.

“I won’t give anything, and to her least of all! She didn’t love him. She took his little cannon from him that time, and he gave it to her,” the captain suddenly sobbed aloud at the memory of how Ilyusha had given up his little cannon to his mama that time. The poor crazed woman simply dissolved in quiet weeping, covering her face with her hands. The boys, seeing at last that the father would not let go of the coffin and that meanwhile it was time to carry it out, suddenly crowded round the coffin in a close group and began to lift it.

“I don’t want him buried in the churchyard!” Snegiryov suddenly howled. “I’ll bury him by the stone, by our stone! Ilyusha told me to. I won’t let you carry him!”

He had been saying for all three days that he would bury him by the stone; but Alyosha, Krasotkin, the landlady, her sister and all the boys had intervened.

“Look what he’s thought of, burying him by a heathen stone, as if he had hanged himself,” the old landlady said sternly. “In the churchyard the ground has the cross. There they will pray for him. You can hear the singing from the church, and the deacon reads so clearly and finely that every word will reach him every time, as if they were reading over his little grave.”

The captain waved his hands at last: “Carry him wherever you like!” The children lifted the coffin, but as they carried it past the mother, they stopped before her for a moment and lowered it, so that she could take leave of Ilyusha. But on suddenly seeing that dear little face close to, which for all three days she had looked at only from some distance, she began to tremble all over and to jerk her grey head hysterically backwards and forwards over the coffin.

“Mama, make the sign of the cross over him, bless him, kiss him,” Ninochka cried to her. But she went on jerking her head like an automaton, and silently, her face twisted with burning grief, suddenly began beating her breast with her fist. The coffin was carried on. Ninochka pressed her lips for the last time to her dead brother’s lips as they carried him past her. Alyosha, going out of the house, turned to the landlady with a request to look after those who were left, but she did not let him finish:

“Of course I’ll stay with them; we are Christians too.” The old woman was crying as she said it.

It was not far to the church, three hundred paces, no more. The day had become clear and still; it was freezing, but only a little. The bell was still ringing. Snegiryov ran fussily and distractedly after the coffin in his old, short, almost summer overcoat, bareheaded, holding his old, wide-brimmed soft hat in his hands. He was in some insoluble anxiety: now he would suddenly stretch out his hand to support the head of the coffin, and only get in the way of the bearers; now he would run round to the side and look for some place to fit himself in. One flower fell on the snow, and he simply rushed to pick it up, as if God knows what depended on the loss of that flower.

“And the crust, we’ve forgotten the crust!” he suddenly exclaimed in terrible fright. But the boys at once reminded him that he had already taken the crust of bread earlier and that it was in his pocket. He instantly pulled it out of his pocket and, having made sure, was calm.

“Ilyushechka told me to, Ilyushechka,” he explained at once to Alyosha. “He was lying there at night, and I was sitting by him, and suddenly he ordered: ‘Papa, when they fill in my grave, crumble a crust of bread on it, so that the sparrows will come; I shall hear that they have come, and it will cheer me that I am not lying alone.’”

“That is very good,” said Alyosha. “You must take it often.”

“Every day, every day!” the captain began to babble, as if he had come all to life.

At last they reached the church and set the coffin down in the middle of it. All the boys stood round it and stood so decorously through the whole service. The church was ancient and rather poor; many of the icons stood with no metal covers at all; but in such churches one somehow prays better. During the liturgy Snegiryov seemed to grow somewhat quieter, though at times the same unconscious and as if bewildered anxiety still broke out in him: now he would go up to the coffin to straighten the pall or the band on the forehead; now, when a candle fell from a candlestick, he suddenly rushed to put it back and fussed with it for a terribly long time. Then he calmed down and stood quietly at the head with a dully anxious and as if perplexed face. After the Epistle he suddenly whispered to Alyosha, who was standing beside him, that the Epistle had not been read right, but he did not explain what he meant. During the Cherubic Hymn he began to sing along, but did not finish, and, sinking on his knees, pressed his forehead to the stone floor of the church and lay so for a rather long time. At last the funeral service began; candles were handed out. The distracted father began to fuss again, but the moving, shattering funeral chant woke and shook his soul. He seemed suddenly to shrink all over and began to sob in short, frequent sobs, at first hiding his voice, and at the end sobbing loudly. When they began to take leave and cover the coffin, he threw his arms round it, as if not letting them cover Ilyushechka, and began kissing his dead boy on the lips, often, greedily, without stopping. At last they persuaded him and had already led him down the step, when he suddenly stretched out his hand impetuously and snatched a few flowers from the little coffin. He looked at them, and some new idea seemed to dawn on him, so that he seemed to forget the main thing for a minute. Little by little he seemed to sink into thought, and no longer resisted when they lifted the coffin and carried it to the little grave. It was not far, in the churchyard, right by the church, an expensive one; Katerina Ivanovna had paid for it. After the usual rite the gravediggers lowered the coffin. Snegiryov, with his flowers in his hands, bent so far over the open grave that the boys in fright clutched at his coat and began to pull him back. But he seemed no longer to understand well what was happening. When they began to fill in the grave, he suddenly began anxiously pointing at the falling earth and even began to say something, but no one could make anything out, and he himself suddenly fell silent. Then they reminded him that he must crumble the crust, and he became terribly agitated, snatched out the crust and began pinching it, scattering bits over the grave: “There, come, little birds; there, come, little sparrows!” he muttered anxiously. One of the boys remarked to him that it was awkward to crumble with flowers in his hands and that he should give them to someone to hold for a while. But he would not give them; he was even suddenly frightened for his flowers, as if they wanted to take them away from him altogether, and, looking at the grave and as if making sure that everything had been done and the bits crumbled, he suddenly, unexpectedly and even quite calmly, turned and wandered home. His step, however, grew quicker and more hurried; he was hurrying, almost running. The boys and Alyosha kept up with him.

“Flowers for mamochka, flowers for mamochka! Mamochka has been hurt,” he suddenly began exclaiming. Someone shouted to him to put on his hat, for it was cold now; but hearing it, he flung his hat on the snow as if in anger and began repeating: “I don’t want my hat, I don’t want my hat!” The boy Smurov picked it up and carried it after him. Every one of the boys was crying, and Kolya and the boy who had discovered Troy most of all; and though Smurov, with the captain’s hat in his hands, was crying terribly too, he still managed, almost on the run, to snatch up a bit of brick that showed red on the snow of the path, to throw it at a flock of sparrows that flew swiftly past. Of course he missed, and ran on crying. Halfway home Snegiryov suddenly stopped, stood for half a minute as if struck by something, and suddenly, turning back towards the church, set off running to the grave he had left. But the boys caught up with him in an instant and clung to him on all sides. Then, as if powerless, as if struck down, he fell on the snow and, struggling, howling and sobbing, began to cry out: “Batyushka, Ilyushechka, dear batyushka!” Alyosha and Kolya began to lift him up, begging and persuading him.

“Captain, enough; a brave man must bear it,” Kolya muttered.

“You will spoil the flowers,” Alyosha said too, “and ‘mamochka’ is waiting for them; she is sitting crying because you didn’t give her any of Ilyushechka’s flowers just now. Ilyusha’s little bed is still there…”

“Yes, yes, to mamochka!” Snegiryov suddenly remembered again. “They’ll take the little bed away, they’ll take it away!” he added, as if in fright that they really would take it away; he jumped up and ran home again. But it was not far now, and they all ran in together. Snegiryov flung open the door impetuously and cried out to his wife, with whom he had quarrelled so hard-heartedly a little while before:

“Mamochka, dear, Ilyushechka has sent you some flowers, for your poor legs!” he cried, holding out to her the little bunch of flowers, frozen and broken when he had struggled in the snow just now. But at that very moment he saw, in front of Ilyusha’s little bed, in the corner, Ilyusha’s little boots standing side by side, just put tidy by the landlady: old, gone rusty-red, stiff little boots, with patches. Seeing them, he raised his hands and simply threw himself at them, fell on his knees, seized one little boot and, pressing his lips to it, began kissing it greedily, crying out: “Batyushka, Ilyushechka, dear batyushka, where are your little feet?”

“Where have you taken him? Where have you taken him?” the crazed woman wailed in a heart-rending voice. At that Ninochka too began to sob. Kolya ran out of the room; the boys began to go out after him. At last Alyosha went out after them too. “Let them cry it out,” he said to Kolya. “Of course there is no comforting them now. Let us wait a minute and go back.”

“Yes, there is no comforting them; it is terrible,” Kolya agreed. “You know, Karamazov,” he suddenly lowered his voice so that no one should hear, “I am very sad, and if only it were possible to bring him back to life, I would give anything in the world!”

“Ah, so would I,” said Alyosha.

“What do you think, Karamazov, should we come here this evening? He is sure to get drunk.”

“Perhaps he will. Just the two of us will come, you and I; that will be enough, to sit with them for an hour, with the mother and with Ninochka; but if we all come at once, we shall remind them of everything again,” Alyosha advised.

“The landlady is laying the table there now; it’s to be a memorial dinner or something; the priest will come; should we go back there now, Karamazov, or not?”

“Certainly,” said Alyosha.

“It is all strange, Karamazov: such grief, and suddenly pancakes of some sort; how unnatural it all is in our religion!”

“They’ll have salmon too,” the boy who had discovered Troy suddenly remarked aloud.

“I ask you seriously, Kartashov, not to butt in any more with your stupidities, especially when no one is talking to you and doesn’t even want to know whether you exist,” Kolya snapped irritably in his direction. The boy simply flushed, but did not dare say anything in answer. Meanwhile they were all wandering quietly along the path, and suddenly Smurov exclaimed:

“There’s Ilyusha’s stone, under which they wanted to bury him!”

They all stopped in silence by the big stone. Alyosha looked, and the whole picture of what Snegiryov had once told him about Ilyushechka, how, weeping and embracing his father, he had cried, “Papa, papa, how he humiliated you!”, at once rose up in his memory. Something seemed to tremble in his soul. With a serious and solemn look he ran his eyes over all those dear, bright faces of the schoolboys, Ilyusha’s companions, and suddenly said to them:

“Gentlemen, I should like to say one word to you here, at this very place.”

The boys surrounded him and at once fixed intent, expectant looks on him.

“Gentlemen, we shall soon part. For the present I shall be for some time with my two brothers, one of whom is going into exile and the other lies at death’s door. But soon I shall leave this town, perhaps for a very long time. So we shall part, gentlemen. Let us agree here, by Ilyusha’s stone, that we will never forget, first, Ilyushechka, and second, one another. And whatever happens to us later in life, even if we do not meet for twenty years afterwards, still let us remember how we buried the poor boy, at whom we once threw stones, do you remember, by the little bridge there? and then how we all came to love him. He was a splendid boy, a kind and brave boy; he felt the honour and the bitter wrong done to his father, and rose up against it. So, first of all, let us remember him, gentlemen, all our lives. And even if we are occupied with the most important affairs, attain honours or fall into some great misfortune, still never forget how good it was for us once here, all together, united by so good and kind a feeling, which, for the time of our love for the poor boy, made us too perhaps better than we really are. My little doves (let me call you that, little doves, for you are all very like them, those pretty blue-grey birds, now, at this moment, as I look at your kind, dear faces), my dear children, perhaps you will not understand what I am going to say to you, for I often speak very unclearly, but still you will remember it and some time later agree with my words. Know, then, that there is nothing higher, and stronger, and healthier, and more useful for life from now on, than some good memory, and especially one carried away from childhood, from one’s parents’ home. People talk to you a great deal about your education; but some such beautiful, holy memory, kept from childhood, is perhaps the very best education. If a man carries many such memories with him into life, he is saved for the whole of his life. And even if only one good memory stays with us in our hearts, even that may some time serve to save us. Perhaps we shall even become wicked later on; we may not even have the strength to resist a bad deed; we may laugh at men’s tears and at those people who say, as Kolya exclaimed just now, ‘I want to suffer for all people’, and perhaps we shall jeer spitefully at such people. And yet, however wicked we may become, which God forbid, when we remember how we buried Ilyusha, how we loved him in his last days, and how we have just been talking here so friendly and so together by this stone, then the cruellest and most mocking of us, if we do become such, will not dare to laugh inwardly at how kind and good he was at this present moment! More than that, perhaps that one memory alone will keep him from great evil, and he will think again and say: ‘Yes, I was kind then, bold and honest.’ Let him smile to himself; that does not matter; a man often laughs at what is kind and good; that is only from thoughtlessness; but I assure you, gentlemen, that as soon as he smiles, he will say at once in his heart: ‘No, I did wrong to smile, because one must not laugh at that!’”

“It will certainly be so, Karamazov; I understand you, Karamazov!” exclaimed Kolya, his eyes flashing. The boys were stirred and also wanted to exclaim something, but restrained themselves, looking intently and tenderly at the speaker.

“I say this in fear that we may become bad,” Alyosha went on, “but why should we become bad, isn’t that so, gentlemen? Let us be, first and above all, kind, then honest, and then let us never forget one another. I repeat that again. I give you my word, gentlemen, that I will not forget one of you; I shall remember every face that is looking at me now, at this moment, even after thirty years. Just now Kolya said to Kartashov that we supposedly don’t want to know ‘whether he exists or not’. But can I forget that Kartashov exists, and that he is not blushing now as he did when he discovered Troy, but is looking at me with his splendid, kind, merry little eyes? Gentlemen, my dear gentlemen, let us all be generous and brave, like Ilyushechka; clever, brave and generous, like Kolya (who will be much cleverer still when he grows up); and let us be as modest, but clever and sweet, as Kartashov. But why do I speak only of those two? All of you, gentlemen, are dear to me from now on; I shall shut you all up in my heart, and I ask you to shut me up in your hearts too! And who has united us in this kind, good feeling, which we shall always remember now, all our lives, and mean to remember? Who but Ilyushechka, the kind boy, the dear boy, the boy precious to us for ever and ever! Let us never forget him, then; eternal and good memory to him in our hearts, from now and for ever and ever!”

“Yes, yes, eternal, eternal,” all the boys cried in their ringing voices, with tender faces.

“Let us remember his face, and his clothes, and his poor little boots, and his little coffin, and his unhappy, sinful father, and how he rose up alone against the whole class for him!”

“We will, we will remember!” the boys cried again. “He was brave, he was kind!”

“Ah, how I loved him!” exclaimed Kolya.

“Ah, children, ah, dear friends, don’t be afraid of life! How good life is when you do something good and true!”

“Yes, yes,” the boys repeated rapturously.

“Karamazov, we love you!” one voice, Kartashov’s it seems, exclaimed irresistibly.

“We love you, we love you,” they all took it up. Many had little tears glistening in their eyes.

“Hurrah for Karamazov!” Kolya proclaimed rapturously.

“And eternal memory to the dead boy!” Alyosha added again with feeling.

“Eternal memory!” the boys took it up again.

“Karamazov!” cried Kolya. “Can it be true, as religion says, that we shall all rise from the dead and come to life and see one another again, and everyone, and Ilyushechka?”

“Certainly we shall rise again; certainly we shall see one another and tell one another gladly, joyfully, everything that has happened,” Alyosha answered, half laughing, half in rapture.

“Ah, how good that will be!” broke from Kolya.

“Well, and now let us end our speeches and go to his memorial dinner. Don’t be troubled that we shall eat pancakes. It is an old, an eternal custom, and there is good in it,” Alyosha laughed. “Well, let us go! And now we go hand in hand.”

“And always so, all our lives hand in hand! Hurrah for Karamazov!” Kolya cried once more rapturously, and once more all the boys took up his exclamation.
