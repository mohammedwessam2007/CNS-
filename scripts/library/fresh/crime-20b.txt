## CHAPTER VII

That same day, but in the evening, at about seven o’clock, Raskolnikov was approaching his mother’s and sister’s lodging, the same lodging in Bakaleev’s house where Razumikhin had settled them. The entrance to the stairs was from the street. Raskolnikov came up, still holding back his step and as if hesitating: should he go in or not? But he would not have turned back for anything; his decision was made. “Besides, it makes no difference; they don’t know anything yet,” he thought, “and they are already used to thinking me an eccentric...” His clothes were dreadful: everything dirty, having been out all night in the rain, torn, frayed. His face was almost disfigured by tiredness, bad weather, physical exhaustion and an almost day-long struggle with himself. He had spent the whole of that night alone, God knows where. But at least he had made up his mind.

He knocked at the door; his mother opened it to him. Dunechka was not at home. Even the servant was not there at that moment. At first Pulkheria Alexandrovna was struck dumb with joyful amazement; then she seized his hand and pulled him into the room.

“Well, here you are!” she began, stammering with joy. “Don’t be angry with me, Rodya, for meeting you so foolishly, with tears: I am laughing, not crying. You think I am crying? No, I am rejoicing, but I have such a silly habit: the tears flow. I have had it since your father’s death; I cry at everything. Sit down, my dear; you are tired, I can see. Ah, how dirty you have got.”

“I was out in the rain yesterday, Mama...” Raskolnikov began.

“No, no, don’t!” Pulkheria Alexandrovna started up, interrupting him. “You thought I would start questioning you at once, in my old womanish way; don’t worry. I understand, I understand everything; I have learned the ways here now, and really, I see myself that it is wiser here. I have decided once and for all: how am I to understand your ideas and demand accounts from you? Perhaps you have God knows what affairs and plans in your head, or some thoughts are being born there; so am I to nudge your elbow: what are you thinking about? Here I... Ah, Lord! What am I doing, rushing here and there like a madwoman... Here, Rodya, I am reading your article in the journal for the third time already; Dmitry Prokofich brought it to me. I simply gasped when I saw it: what a fool I am, I thought to myself, so this is what he is busy with, here is the answer to everything! Perhaps he has new thoughts in his head just now; he is thinking them over, and I torment him and trouble him. I read it, my dear, and of course there is a lot I don’t understand; but then, that is how it should be: how could I?”

“Show it to me, Mama.”

Raskolnikov took the paper and glanced at his article in passing. However much it contradicted his situation and his state, he felt that strange and caustically sweet feeling which an author experiences on seeing himself in print for the first time; besides, his twenty-three years told. This lasted only an instant. Having read a few lines, he frowned, and a terrible anguish gripped his heart. All his inner struggle of the last months came back to him at once. With disgust and vexation he threw the article on the table.

“But only, Rodya, however stupid I am, I can still judge that you will very soon be one of the first men, if not the very first, in our learned world. And they dared to think that you had gone mad. Ha-ha-ha! You don’t know, but they did think it! Ah, low worms, how could they understand what intelligence is! And Dunechka too almost believed it; just think! Your late father twice sent things to the journals: first poems (I still keep the exercise book; I will show it to you some day), and then a whole story (I begged him myself to let me copy it out), and how we both prayed that they would accept it; they didn’t! Six or seven days ago, Rodya, I was breaking my heart, looking at your clothes, how you live, what you eat and what you go about in. And now I see that I was stupid again, because if you want to, you will get everything for yourself at once now, by your intelligence and talent. It is just that for the time being you don’t want to, and are busy with much more important things...”

“Isn’t Dunya at home, Mama?”

“No, Rodya. Very often I don’t see her at home; she leaves me alone. Dmitry Prokofich, thanks to him, comes in to sit with me and keeps talking about you. He loves you and respects you, my dear. I am not saying that your sister is so very disrespectful to me. I am not complaining. She has her character and I have mine; she has some secrets of her own now; well, I have no secrets from you two. Of course I am firmly sure that Dunya is too intelligent, and besides, she loves both me and you... but I don’t know what all this will lead to. Here you have made me happy now, Rodya, by coming in, and she has gone out; she will come back, and I will say: your brother was here while you were out, and where were you pleased to spend your time? Don’t spoil me too much, Rodya: if you can, come in; if you can’t, there is nothing to be done, I will wait anyway. After all, I shall know all the same that you love me, and that is enough for me. I will read your writings, I will hear about you from everyone, and now and then you will come yourself to see me; what could be better? Here you have come now to comfort your mother, I can see...”

Here Pulkheria Alexandrovna suddenly began to cry.

“Me again! Don’t look at me, fool that I am! Ah, Lord, why am I sitting here,” she cried, jumping up from her place, “there is coffee, and I haven’t offered you any! That is what an old woman’s egoism means. At once, at once!”

“Mama, leave that; I am going in a moment. I didn’t come for that. Please, listen to me.”

Pulkheria Alexandrovna came up to him timidly.

“Mama, whatever happens, whatever you hear about me, whatever you are told about me, will you love me as you do now?” he asked suddenly, from the fullness of his heart, as if not thinking about his words and not weighing them.

“Rodya, Rodya, what is the matter with you? How can you ask such a thing! Who would tell me anything about you? I wouldn’t believe anyone, whoever came to me; I would simply drive them away.”

“I came to assure you that I have always loved you, and now I am glad that we are alone, glad even that Dunechka is not here,” he went on with the same impulse. “I came to tell you straight out that though you will be unhappy, still you should know that your son loves you now more than himself, and that everything you thought about me, that I am cruel and don’t love you, all that was untrue. I shall never stop loving you... Well, and that is enough; it seemed to me that I had to do this and begin with this...”

Pulkheria Alexandrovna silently embraced him, pressed him to her breast and wept quietly.

“What is the matter with you, Rodya, I don’t know,” she said at last. “All this time I thought that we were simply boring you, but now I see from everything that a great sorrow is in store for you; that is why you are so miserable. I have foreseen it for a long time, Rodya. Forgive me for speaking of it; I keep thinking about it and don’t sleep at night. Last night your sister too lay delirious the whole night through, and kept talking about you. I caught something, but understood nothing. All morning I went about as if before an execution, expecting something, foreseeing something, and now it has come! Rodya, Rodya, where are you going? Are you going away somewhere?”

“I am.”

“So I thought! But I can go with you, if you need me. And Dunya; she loves you, she loves you very much; and Sofya Semyonovna, perhaps, let her come with us, if need be; you see, I will gladly take her even as a daughter. Dmitry Prokofich will help us get ready together... but... where are you... going?”

“Goodbye, Mama.”

“What! Today!” she cried, as if losing him for ever.

“I can’t stay, it is time, I very much need to...”

“And I can’t come with you?”

“No, but kneel down and pray to God for me. Your prayer, perhaps, will reach him.”

“Let me make the sign of the cross over you, let me bless you! There, there. Oh God, what are we doing!”

Yes, he was glad, he was very glad that no one was there, that he and his mother were alone. It was as if his heart had softened all at once for all that terrible time. He fell down before her, he kissed her feet, and both, embracing, wept. And this time she did not wonder or ask questions. She had long understood that something terrible was happening to her son, and that now some dreadful moment had come for him.

“Rodya, my dear, my first-born,” she said, sobbing, “now you are just as you were when you were little; you used to come to me just like this, and hug me and kiss me just like this; back when your father and I lived together and were poor, you comforted us just by being with us, and when I buried your father, how many times did we weep, hugging each other just like now, on his grave. And if I have been crying for a long time, it is because a mother’s heart foresaw trouble. As soon as I saw you the first time then, in the evening, remember, when we had just arrived here, I guessed everything from your look alone; my heart simply trembled then, and today, when I opened the door to you and looked, well, I thought, the fatal hour has come. Rodya, Rodya, you aren’t going right now?”

“No.”

“You will come again?”

“Yes... I will come.”

“Rodya, don’t be angry, I don’t even dare ask. I know I don’t dare, but just tell me two little words: are you going far?”

“Very far.”

“What is there, some position, a career for you?”

“Whatever God sends... only pray for me...”

Raskolnikov went to the door, but she clung to him and looked into his eyes with a desperate look. Her face was distorted with horror.

“Enough, Mama,” said Raskolnikov, deeply regretting that he had taken it into his head to come.

“Not for ever? It isn’t for ever yet, is it? You will come, you will come tomorrow?”

“I will, I will; goodbye.”

He tore himself away at last.

The evening was fresh, warm and clear; the weather had cleared up since the morning. Raskolnikov was walking to his lodging; he was hurrying. He wanted to finish everything before sunset. Until then he would rather not meet anyone. Going up to his lodging, he noticed that Nastasya, tearing herself away from the samovar, was watching him intently and following him with her eyes. “Can there be someone in my room?” he thought. He imagined Porfiry, with disgust. But when he reached his room and opened it, he saw Dunechka. She was sitting all alone, in deep thought, and had evidently been waiting for him a long time. He stopped on the threshold. She rose from the sofa in fright and stood up straight before him. Her look, fixed motionlessly on him, expressed horror and inconsolable grief. And from that look alone he understood at once that she knew everything.

“Well, shall I come in to you, or go away?” he asked mistrustfully.

“I sat at Sofya Semyonovna’s all day; we were both waiting for you. We thought you would certainly go there.”

Raskolnikov came into the room and sat down on a chair in exhaustion.

“I am somehow weak, Dunya; I am very tired; and I would like to be in full control of myself at least at this moment.”

He glanced up at her mistrustfully.

“Where were you all night?”

“I don’t remember well; you see, sister, I wanted to make up my mind finally, and many times I walked near the Neva; that I remember. I wanted to end it there, but... I couldn’t bring myself to...” he whispered, again glancing mistrustfully at Dunya.

“Thank God! And how we were afraid of exactly that, Sofya Semyonovna and I! So you still believe in life: thank God, thank God!”

Raskolnikov smiled bitterly.

“I didn’t believe, but just now my mother and I were weeping in each other’s arms; I don’t believe, but I asked her to pray for me. God knows how this happens, Dunechka, and I understand nothing of it.”

“You were at Mother’s? And you told her?” Dunya exclaimed in horror. “Can it be that you brought yourself to tell her?”

“No, I didn’t tell her... in words; but she understood a great deal. She heard you raving in the night. I am sure she understands half already. Perhaps I did wrong to go. I don’t even know why I went. I am a low man, Dunya.”

“A low man, and yet you are ready to go to suffering! You are going, aren’t you?”

“I am. Right now. Yes, it was to escape this shame that I wanted to drown myself, Dunya, but I thought, standing over the water, that if I have considered myself strong until now, then let me not be afraid of shame now either,” he said, running ahead. “Is that pride, Dunya?”

“It is pride, Rodya.”

It was as if a fire flashed in his dulled eyes; it seemed to please him that he was still proud.

“And don’t you think, sister, that I was simply afraid of the water?” he asked with a hideous smile, looking into her face.

“Oh, Rodya, stop it!” Dunya exclaimed bitterly.

The silence lasted about two minutes. He sat with his eyes lowered, looking at the ground; Dunechka stood at the other end of the table and looked at him in torment. Suddenly he stood up:

“It is late, it is time. I am going now to give myself up. But I don’t know why I am going to give myself up.”

Big tears were running down her cheeks.

“You are crying, sister, but can you give me your hand?”

“And did you doubt it?”

She hugged him tightly.

“Aren’t you, by going to suffering, already washing away half your crime?” she cried, pressing him in her arms and kissing him.

“Crime? What crime?” he cried suddenly, in a kind of sudden fury. “That I killed a vile, harmful louse, an old moneylender, of no use to anyone, for whose killing forty sins would be forgiven, who sucked the juice out of the poor, and that is a crime? I don’t think about it and I don’t think of washing it away. And why does everyone keep jabbing at me from all sides: ‘crime, crime!’ Only now do I see clearly the whole absurdity of my faint-heartedness, now that I have decided to go to this unnecessary shame! I am deciding simply out of my baseness and lack of talent, or perhaps out of advantage, as that... Porfiry proposed!..”

“Brother, brother, what are you saying! But you have shed blood!” Dunya cried in despair.

“Which everyone sheds,” he took up, almost in a frenzy, “which flows and has always flowed in the world like a waterfall, which people pour out like champagne, and for which men are crowned in the Capitol and called afterwards benefactors of humanity. Just look more closely and see! I myself wanted good for people, and would have done hundreds, thousands of good deeds instead of this one piece of stupidity, not even stupidity but simply clumsiness, since the whole idea was not at all as stupid as it seems now, after the failure... (After failure everything seems stupid!) By this stupidity I only wanted to put myself in an independent position, to take the first step, to obtain means, and then everything would have been smoothed over by an immeasurable, comparatively, benefit... But I, I couldn’t even stand the first step, because I am a scoundrel! That is the whole point! And still I won’t look at it your way: if I had succeeded, they would have crowned me, and now it’s the trap!”

“But that is not it, not it at all! Brother, what are you saying!”

“Ah! Not the right form, not an aesthetically nice form! Well, I decidedly don’t understand: why is pelting people with bombs, in a regular siege, a more respectable form? Fear of aesthetics is the first sign of impotence!.. Never, never have I realised this more clearly than now, and more than ever I don’t understand my crime! Never, never have I been stronger and more convinced than now!..”

Colour even rushed into his pale, exhausted face. But as he uttered this last exclamation, his eyes happened to meet Dunya’s, and he met so much, so much torment for him in that look that he involuntarily came to himself. He felt that all the same he had made these two poor women unhappy. All the same, he was the cause...

“Dunya, dear! If I am guilty, forgive me (though I cannot be forgiven, if I am guilty). Goodbye! Let us not argue! It is time, it is very much time. Don’t follow me, I beg you; I still have to call somewhere... But go now and sit beside Mother at once. I beg you! It is my last and greatest request to you. Don’t leave her all the time; I left her in an anxiety which she will hardly bear: she will either die or go out of her mind. Be with her! Razumikhin will be with you; I have spoken to him... Don’t cry for me: I will try to be both brave and honest, all my life, though I am a murderer. Perhaps some day you will hear my name. I won’t disgrace you, you’ll see; I will still prove... now, for the time being, goodbye,” he hastened to conclude, again noticing a strange expression in Dunya’s eyes at his last words and promises. “Why are you crying so? Don’t cry, don’t cry; we are not parting for good!.. Ah, yes! Wait, I forgot!..”

He went to the table, took a thick, dusty book, opened it and took out a small portrait in watercolour on ivory, which had been put between the pages. It was a portrait of his landlady’s daughter, his former bride, who had died of a fever, that same strange girl who had wanted to go into a convent. For a minute he gazed at that expressive and sickly little face, kissed the portrait and handed it to Dunechka.

“I talked a great deal with her about this too, with her alone,” he said thoughtfully. “I told her heart much of what was afterwards fulfilled so hideously. Don’t worry,” he turned to Dunya, “she did not agree, any more than you, and I am glad that she is gone. The main thing, the main thing is that everything will now go a new way, will break in two,” he cried suddenly, returning again to his anguish, “everything, everything, but am I prepared for it? Do I want it myself? They say it is necessary for my trial! What for, what for, all these senseless trials? What for; will I be more aware then, crushed by torments, by idiocy, in an old man’s impotence after twenty years of penal servitude, than I am aware now? And what will I live for then? Why do I agree to live like that now? Oh, I knew I was a scoundrel when I stood over the Neva at dawn today!”

At last they both went out. It was hard for Dunya, but she loved him! She walked off, but after about fifty paces she turned round to look at him once more. He could still be seen. But on reaching the corner, he too turned round; for the last time their eyes met; but, noticing that she was looking at him, he waved his hand impatiently and even with vexation for her to go on, and turned sharply round the corner himself.

“I am wicked, I see it,” he thought to himself, ashamed a minute later of his vexed gesture to Dunya. “But why do they love me so much themselves, if I am not worth it! Oh, if only I were alone and no one loved me, and I had never loved anyone myself! None of this would have happened! And it is curious: can it be that in these coming fifteen or twenty years my soul will be so humbled that I shall whimper reverently before people, calling myself a robber at every word? Yes, just so, just so! That is what they are exiling me for now; that is what they need... Here they all are, scurrying up and down the street, and every one of them is a scoundrel and a robber by his very nature; worse than that, an idiot! But just try to let me off exile, and they will all go wild with noble indignation! Oh, how I hate them all!”

He fell deep into thought about this: “By what process can it happen that at last, before all of them, he will be humbled without reasoning, humbled by conviction! And why not? Of course, that is how it must be. Will not twenty years of unbroken oppression finish one off completely? Water wears away stone. And why, why live after that, why am I going now, when I know myself that it will all be exactly so, as in a book, and not otherwise!”

He had asked himself this question for perhaps the hundredth time since the evening before, but still he went.
