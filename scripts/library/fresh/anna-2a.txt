## Part One · Chapter VI

When Oblonsky asked Levin what, exactly, he had come for, Levin blushed and was angry with himself for blushing, because he could not answer him: “I have come to propose to your sister-in-law,” though that was the only thing he had come for.

The Levins and the Shcherbatskys were old Moscow noble families and had always been on close and friendly terms. The tie had grown still stronger during Levin’s student days. He had prepared for the university together with the young Prince Shcherbatsky, the brother of Dolly and Kitty, and had entered it together with him. At that time Levin was often at the Shcherbatskys’ house, and he fell in love with the Shcherbatskys’ house. Strange as it may seem, Konstantin Levin was in love precisely with the house, with the family, and especially with the women of the Shcherbatsky family. Levin himself did not remember his mother, and his only sister was older than he, so that in the Shcherbatskys’ house he saw for the first time that very world of an old, educated and honourable noble family of which he had been deprived by the death of his father and mother. All the members of this family, especially the women, seemed to him to be wrapped in a kind of mysterious, poetic veil, and not only did he see no faults in them, but under that poetic veil that covered them he supposed the loftiest feelings and every possible perfection. Why these three young ladies had to speak French and English on alternate days; why at certain hours they took turns playing the piano, whose sounds could always be heard upstairs in the brother’s room, where the students worked; why these teachers of French literature, of music, of drawing, of dancing kept coming; why at certain hours all three young ladies, with Mlle Linon, drove up in the carriage to the Tverskoy Boulevard in their satin fur coats, Dolly in a long one, Natalie in a half-length one, and Kitty in a quite short one, so that her shapely little legs in tightly drawn red stockings were in full view; why they had to walk along the Tverskoy Boulevard accompanied by a footman with a gold cockade on his hat: all this, and much else that went on in their mysterious world, he did not understand; but he knew that everything that went on there was beautiful, and he was in love precisely with the mystery of what went on.

During his student days he had very nearly fallen in love with the eldest, Dolly, but she was soon married to Oblonsky. Then he began to fall in love with the second. He seemed to feel that he had to fall in love with one of the sisters; only he could not make out which one. But Natalie too, as soon as she came out into society, married the diplomat Lvov. Kitty was still a child when Levin left the university. Young Shcherbatsky joined the navy and was drowned in the Baltic, and Levin’s relations with the Shcherbatskys, in spite of his friendship with Oblonsky, became rarer. But when this year, at the beginning of winter, Levin came to Moscow after a year in the country and saw the Shcherbatskys, he understood which of the three he had really been destined to fall in love with.

It would seem that nothing could be simpler than for him, a man of good family, rich rather than poor, thirty-two years old, to propose to the Princess Shcherbatskaya; in all likelihood he would at once have been considered a good match. But Levin was in love, and so it seemed to him that Kitty was such a perfection in every respect, a being so far above everything earthly, and he such an earthly, low being, that there could be no thought of others, or she herself, considering him worthy of her.

After spending two months in Moscow as if in a daze, seeing Kitty almost every day in society, which he began to go into so as to meet her, Levin suddenly decided that it could not be, and went away to the country.

Levin’s conviction that it could not be was founded on this: that in the eyes of her family he was an unprofitable, unworthy match for the lovely Kitty, and that Kitty herself could not love him. In her family’s eyes he had no customary, definite occupation and no position in society, whereas his comrades now, when he was thirty-two, were already one a colonel and aide-de-camp, another a professor, another the director of a bank and of railways, or the head of a government office, like Oblonsky; while he (he knew very well how he must look to others) was a landowner occupied with breeding cows, shooting snipe and putting up buildings: that is, a giftless fellow who had come to nothing, and who was doing, as society understood it, exactly what people do who are good for nothing.

And the mysterious, lovely Kitty herself could not love a man as plain as he considered himself, and above all such a simple man, in no way out of the ordinary. Besides, his former relation to Kitty, the relation of a grown man to a child, because of his friendship with her brother, seemed to him yet another barrier to love. A plain, kind man such as he considered himself could, he supposed, be loved as a friend; but to be loved with the kind of love with which he loved Kitty, one had to be handsome, and above all a special man.

He had heard that women often love plain, simple men, but he did not believe it, because he judged by himself, and he himself could love only beautiful, mysterious and special women.

But after two months alone in the country he became convinced that this was not one of those infatuations he had felt in early youth; that this feeling did not give him a moment’s peace; that he could not live without settling the question of whether she would or would not be his wife; and that his despair came only from his imagination, that he had no proof whatever that he would be refused. And so he had now come to Moscow with the firm decision to propose and to marry if he was accepted. Or… he could not think of what would become of him if he was refused.

## Chapter VII

Arriving in Moscow by the morning train, Levin put up at the house of his elder half-brother on his mother’s side, Koznyshev, and, after changing, went into his study, intending to tell him at once what he had come for and to ask his advice; but his brother was not alone. With him sat a well-known professor of philosophy who had come from Kharkov expressly to clear up a misunderstanding that had arisen between them over a very important philosophical question. The professor was carrying on a heated polemic against the materialists, and Sergei Koznyshev had followed this polemic with interest and, after reading the professor’s latest article, had written him his objections in a letter; he reproached the professor for making too great concessions to the materialists. And the professor had come at once to talk it out. The talk was about a fashionable question: is there a boundary between psychical and physiological phenomena in human activity, and where does it lie?

Sergei Ivanovich met his brother with the smile, kind and cold, that he had for everyone, and, introducing him to the professor, went on with the conversation.

The little yellow man in spectacles, with a narrow forehead, turned from the conversation for a moment to greet him, and then went on talking without paying any attention to Levin. Levin sat down to wait until the professor left, but soon became interested in the subject of the conversation.

Levin had come across the articles that were being discussed in the journals and had read them, interested in them as a development of the foundations of natural science, familiar to him as a natural scientist from the university; but he had never connected these scientific conclusions about the origin of man as an animal, about reflexes, about biology and sociology, with those questions about the meaning of life and death for himself which had lately come to his mind more and more often.

Listening to his brother’s conversation with the professor, he noticed that they connected the scientific questions with the questions of the heart; several times they almost came up to these questions; but each time, as soon as they came close to what seemed to him the most important thing, they at once hurriedly drew back and sank again into the region of fine subdivisions, reservations, quotations, allusions, references to authorities, and he could hardly understand what they were talking about.

“I cannot admit,” said Sergei Ivanovich, with the clearness and precision of expression and the elegance of diction that were usual with him, “I cannot in any case agree with Keiss that my whole idea of the external world comes from impressions. The most fundamental concept, that of being, I have not received through sensation, for there is not even a special organ for conveying that concept.”

“Yes, but they, Wurst, and Knaust, and Pripasov, will answer you that your consciousness of being comes from the sum of all the sensations, that this consciousness of being is the result of sensations. Wurst even says outright that, as soon as there is no sensation, there is no concept of being either.”

“I would say the reverse,” Sergei Ivanovich began…

But here again it seemed to Levin that, having come up to the most important thing, they were drawing back again, and he made up his mind to put a question to the professor.

“So, if my senses are destroyed, if my body dies, there can be no existence of any kind any more?” he asked.

The professor, with vexation and as if with a mental pain at being interrupted, looked round at the strange questioner, who looked more like a barge-hauler than a philosopher, and moved his eyes to Sergei Ivanovich, as though asking: what is there to say to that? But Sergei Ivanovich, who spoke with nothing like the professor’s strain and one-sidedness, and who had room enough in his head both to answer the professor and at the same time to understand the simple and natural point of view from which the question had been put, smiled and said:

“That question we have no right to decide as yet…”

“We have no data,” the professor confirmed, and went on with his arguments. “No,” he said, “I point out that if, as Pripasov says plainly, sensation does have impression as its basis, then we must strictly distinguish these two concepts.”

Levin listened no more, and waited for the professor to leave.

## Chapter VIII

When the professor had gone, Sergei Ivanovich turned to his brother.

“I am very glad you have come. For long? How is the farming?”

Levin knew that farming interested his elder brother very little and that he had asked about it only as a concession to him, and so he answered only about the sale of the wheat and about money.

Levin had meant to tell his brother of his intention to marry and to ask his advice; he had even firmly decided to. But when he saw his brother, listened to his conversation with the professor, when he heard afterwards the involuntarily patronizing tone in which his brother asked him about the affairs of the estate (their mother’s estate had not been divided, and Levin managed both shares), Levin felt that for some reason he could not begin to talk to his brother about his decision to marry. He felt that his brother would not look at it as he would have liked.

“Well, how is your zemstvo getting on?” asked Sergei Ivanovich, who was very much interested in the zemstvo and attached great importance to it.

“Oh, I really don’t know…”

“What? But you are a member of the board, aren’t you?”

“No, not a member any more; I have resigned,” answered Konstantin Levin, “and I don’t go to the meetings any more.”

“A pity!” said Sergei Ivanovich, frowning.

To justify himself, Levin began describing what went on at the meetings in his district.

“That’s how it always is!” Sergei Ivanovich interrupted him. “We Russians are always like that. Perhaps it is even a good trait of ours, this ability to see our own failings; but we overdo it, we console ourselves with irony, which is always ready on our tongues. I will tell you only this: give the same rights as our zemstvo institutions to another European people, and the Germans and the English would work them up into freedom, while we only laugh.”

“But what is to be done?” said Levin guiltily. “It was my last experiment. And I tried with all my heart. I can’t. I am not capable of it.”

“Not incapable,” said Sergei Ivanovich. “You don’t look at the thing in the right way.”

“Perhaps,” Levin answered dejectedly.

“And, you know, our brother Nikolai is here again.”

Nikolai was Konstantin Levin’s own elder brother and Sergei Ivanovich’s half-brother, a ruined man, who had squandered the greater part of his fortune, moved in the strangest and worst company, and quarrelled with his brothers.

“What are you saying?” Levin cried in horror. “How do you know?”

“Prokofy saw him in the street.”

“Here, in Moscow? Where is he? Do you know?” Levin got up from his chair, as though about to go at once.

“I am sorry I told you,” said Sergei Ivanych, shaking his head at his younger brother’s agitation. “I sent to find out where he is living, and sent him his promissory note to Trubin, which I had paid. This is what he answered me.”

And Sergei Ivanovich handed his brother a note from under the paperweight.

Levin read, in the strange handwriting so familiar to him: “I humbly beg to be left in peace. That is the one thing I demand of my dear brothers. Nikolai Levin.”

Levin read it and, without raising his head, stood before Sergei Ivanovich with the note in his hands.

In his soul a wish to forget his unhappy brother now was struggling with the awareness that this would be wrong.

“He obviously wants to insult me,” Sergei Ivanovich went on, “but he cannot insult me, and I would wish with all my soul to help him, but I know that it cannot be done.”

“Yes, yes,” Levin kept repeating. “I understand and value your attitude to him; but I shall go and see him.”

“If you want to, go; but I don’t advise it,” said Sergei Ivanovich. “That is, as far as I am concerned, I am not afraid of it; he will not set you against me; but for your own sake I advise you better not to go. He cannot be helped. However, do as you like.”

“Perhaps he can’t be helped, but I feel, especially at this moment (well, that’s another matter), I feel that I cannot be at peace.”

“Well, that I don’t understand,” said Sergei Ivanovich. “One thing I do understand,” he added: “it is a lesson in humility. I have come to look differently, and more indulgently, at what is called baseness since our brother Nikolai became what he is… You know what he did…”

“Oh, it’s terrible, terrible!” Levin kept repeating.

Having got his brother’s address from Sergei Ivanovich’s footman, Levin was about to go to him at once, but on second thoughts decided to put off his visit until the evening. First of all, in order to have peace of mind, he must settle the business for which he had come to Moscow. From his brother’s Levin drove to Oblonsky’s office and, having found out about the Shcherbatskys, drove to the place where he had been told he might find Kitty.
