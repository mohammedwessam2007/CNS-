## Chapter VII · “It Is Worth Talking to a Clever Man”

And he walked as he talked, too. Meeting Fyodor Pavlovich in the hall as soon as he came in, he suddenly shouted at him, waving his arms: “I am going up to my room, not to you, goodbye,” and went past, even trying not to look at his father. It may very well be that the old man was too hateful to him at that moment, but so unceremonious a display of hostility was unexpected even for Fyodor Pavlovich. And the old man evidently did want to tell him something quickly, and had come out into the hall on purpose to meet him; hearing such a civility, he stopped in silence and, with a mocking look, followed his son with his eyes up the stairs to the attic storey until he was out of sight.

“What’s the matter with him?” he quickly asked Smerdyakov, who had come in after Ivan Fyodorovich.

“He is angry about something, sir; who can make him out?” the other muttered evasively.

“The devil take him! Let him be angry! Bring the samovar and clear off yourself, quick. Is there anything new?”

Then began questions of exactly the kind Smerdyakov had just been complaining of to Ivan Fyodorovich, that is, all about the expected visitor, and we will leave them out here. Half an hour later the house was locked, and the crazed old man was pacing alone about the rooms, in tremulous expectation that at any moment the five agreed knocks would sound, now and then looking out of the dark windows and seeing nothing in them but the night.

It was already very late, and Ivan Fyodorovich was still not asleep but thinking. He went to bed late that night, at about two o’clock. But we will not relate the whole course of his thoughts; nor is it time for us to enter that soul: that soul will have its turn. And even if we tried to relate something, it would be very hard to do, because there were no thoughts, but something very indefinite and, above all, far too agitated. He felt himself that he had lost every thread. He was tormented too by various strange and almost wholly unexpected desires. For instance, after midnight he suddenly felt an urgent, unbearable wish to go downstairs, unlock the door, go to the lodge and beat Smerdyakov; but if you had asked him why, he could not definitely have stated a single reason precisely, except perhaps that the servant had become hateful to him as the most grievous offender that could be found in the world. On the other hand, more than once that night his soul was seized by some inexplicable and humiliating timidity, from which, he felt it, he even seemed suddenly to lose his physical strength. His head ached and swam. Something hateful gnawed at his soul, as if he were going to take revenge on someone. He hated even Alyosha, remembering their conversation earlier; at moments he hated himself very much too. Of Katerina Ivanovna he almost forgot to think, and he wondered at that a great deal afterwards, all the more since he remembered firmly that only yesterday morning, when he had boasted so grandly at Katerina Ivanovna’s that he would leave for Moscow tomorrow, he had whispered to himself at once in his soul: “That’s nonsense, you won’t go, and it won’t be as easy for you to tear yourself away as you brag now.” Recalling this night long afterwards, Ivan Fyodorovich remembered with particular disgust how he would suddenly get up from the sofa and quietly, as though terribly afraid of being watched, open the door, go out onto the stairs and listen down into the lower rooms to Fyodor Pavlovich stirring and pacing there below. He listened for a long time, five minutes at a stretch, with some strange curiosity, holding his breath, his heart pounding; and why he did all this, why he listened, of course he did not know himself. All his life afterwards he called this “act” “vile”, and all his life, deep within himself, in the secret places of his soul, he considered it the basest act of his whole life. For Fyodor Pavlovich himself he felt no hatred at all in those minutes, but was only curious, for some reason, with all his might: how he was walking down there, what he must be doing down there in his rooms now; he guessed and pictured how down there he must be looking out of the dark windows and suddenly stopping in the middle of the room and waiting, waiting: wasn’t someone knocking? Ivan Fyodorovich went out onto the stairs for this occupation two or three times. When everything was quiet and Fyodor Pavlovich had gone to bed, at about two o’clock, Ivan Fyodorovich too lay down, with a firm wish to fall asleep as quickly as possible, since he felt terribly worn out. And indeed he suddenly fell sound asleep and slept without dreams, but woke early, at about seven, when it was already light. Opening his eyes, to his amazement he suddenly felt in himself a surge of some extraordinary energy; he jumped up quickly and dressed quickly, then pulled out his trunk and at once, hurriedly, began to pack it. His linen had all come back from the laundress only yesterday morning. Ivan Fyodorovich even smiled at the thought that everything had fallen out so well, that there was nothing to delay a sudden departure. And the departure really was sudden. Although Ivan Fyodorovich had said yesterday (to Katerina Ivanovna, to Alyosha and then to Smerdyakov) that he would leave tomorrow, yet going to bed last night he remembered very well that at that moment he had not been thinking of leaving; at least he had had no idea that on waking in the morning his first impulse would be to rush to pack his trunk. At last the trunk and bag were ready. It was already about nine when Marfa Ignatyevna came up to him with her usual daily question: “Where will you be pleased to take your tea, here or will you come down?” Ivan Fyodorovich came down looking almost cheerful, though there was something sprawling and hurried in him, in his words and gestures. Greeting his father affably and even asking particularly after his health, he did not wait, however, for his father to finish answering, but announced at once that in an hour he was leaving for Moscow, for good, and asked him to send for horses. The old man heard the news without the least surprise, most improperly forgetting to grieve over his son’s departure; instead he suddenly became extremely busy, remembering, as luck would have it, an urgent business of his own.

“Oh, you! What a fellow! You didn’t tell me yesterday… well, never mind, we’ll settle it now. Do me a great kindness, my own dear father, drive round by Chermashnya. From Volovya station you only have to turn left, only twelve versts or so, and there is Chermashnya.”

“Forgive me, I can’t: it is eighty versts to the railway, and the train leaves the station for Moscow at seven in the evening; there is only just time to catch it.”

“You’ll catch it tomorrow, or the day after, but today turn off to Chermashnya. What does it cost you to set your father’s mind at rest? If I hadn’t business here, I’d have flown there myself long ago, because the thing there is urgent and out of the ordinary, but just now my time here is not my own… You see, there’s that wood of mine there, in two lots, at Begichevo and at Dyachkino, on the waste land. The Maslovs, father and son, merchants, offer only eight thousand for the timber, and only last year a buyer turned up who offered twelve, but he wasn’t from these parts, that’s the point. Because now there is no market among the local people: the Maslovs, father and son, are bullying everyone, men worth a hundred thousand; what they put down, you have to take, and none of the local people dares compete with them. And the priest at Ilyinskoye suddenly wrote to me here last Thursday that Gorstkin has come, another petty merchant; I know him; only the precious thing is that he isn’t local but from Pogrebovo, so he isn’t afraid of the Maslovs, because he isn’t local. ‘I’ll give eleven thousand for the wood,’ he says, do you hear? And he will only be here, the priest writes, for another week. So if you went and came to terms with him…”

“Then write to the priest; he will come to terms with him.”

“He doesn’t know how, that’s the thing. This priest doesn’t know how to look. He’s a golden man; I’d hand him twenty thousand this minute to keep for me without a receipt; but he can’t see a thing, as if he weren’t a man at all; a crow could cheat him. And he’s a learned man, imagine that. This Gorstkin looks like a peasant, in a blue coat, only by character he is a perfect scoundrel; that is the trouble for all of us: he lies, that’s the point. Sometimes he lies so that you can only wonder why he does it. Two years ago he lied that his wife had died and that he had married another, and none of it was true, imagine: his wife never died at all; she is alive now and beats him once every three days. So now too one has to find out: is he lying or telling the truth that he wants to buy and give eleven thousand?”

“But I can’t do anything there either; I haven’t the eye for it.”

“Wait, wait, you’ll do too, because I’ll tell you all his signs, Gorstkin’s; I have had dealings with him for a long time. You see: you must look at his beard. He has a nasty, thin little reddish beard. If his beard shakes while he talks and gets angry, then it’s all right, he’s telling the truth, he means business; but if he strokes his beard with his left hand and chuckles, well, that means he wants to cheat, he is up to tricks. Never look him in the eyes; you can’t make out anything from his eyes; deep water, a rogue; look at his beard. I’ll give you a note to him, and you show it. He is Gorstkin, only he isn’t Gorstkin, he’s Lyagavy, the Setter; but don’t tell him he’s Lyagavy, he’ll take offence. If you come to terms with him and see that it’s all right, write to me at once. Just write this: ‘He isn’t lying.’ Hold out for eleven; you may come down one thousand, no more. Think: eight and eleven, that’s three thousand difference. It’s just as if I had found those three thousand; it isn’t every day you get a buyer, and I need the money desperately. Let me know it is serious, and then I’ll fly over from here myself and finish it; I’ll snatch the time somehow. But why should I gallop off there now, if the priest has made it all up? Well, will you go or not?”

“Oh, I haven’t time, spare me.”

“Ah, oblige your father, I’ll remember it! You have no heart, any of you, that’s what it is! What is a day or two to you? Where are you going now, to Venice? Your Venice won’t fall to pieces in two days. I’d send Alyoshka, but what use is Alyoshka in such matters? I ask you only because you are a clever man, don’t I see it? You don’t deal in timber, but you have an eye. All that is needed is to see whether the man is speaking seriously or not. I tell you, look at his beard: if his little beard shakes, he means it.”

“So you yourself are pushing me off to that damned Chermashnya, eh?” cried Ivan Fyodorovich, with a spiteful smile.

Fyodor Pavlovich did not make out the spite, or did not want to, but caught at the smile:

“So you’ll go, you’ll go? I’ll scribble you the note this minute.”

“I don’t know whether I shall go; I don’t know; I’ll decide on the way.”

“Why on the way? Decide now. My dear, decide! If you come to terms, write me two lines, give them to the priest, and he will send me your note in a flash. And after that I won’t keep you; off you go to Venice. The priest will take you back to Volovya station with his own horses…”

The old man was simply delighted; he scribbled the note; they sent for the horses; snacks and brandy were served. When the old man was glad he always began to grow expansive, but this time he seemed to hold himself in. About Dmitri Fyodorovich, for instance, he did not utter a single word. And he was not touched at all by the parting. He even seemed not to find anything to talk about, and Ivan Fyodorovich noticed it very much: “I have bored him, though,” he thought to himself. Only when he was seeing his son off from the porch did the old man seem a little flustered and make to kiss him. But Ivan Fyodorovich quickly held out his hand to be shaken, evidently warding off the kisses. The old man understood at once and instantly checked himself.

“Well, God be with you, God be with you!” he kept repeating from the porch. “You’ll come again some time in your life, won’t you? Well, do come; I’ll always be glad. Well, Christ be with you!”

Ivan Fyodorovich climbed into the carriage.

“Goodbye, Ivan, don’t abuse me too much!” his father called for the last time.

All the household came out to see him off: Smerdyakov, Marfa and Grigory. Ivan Fyodorovich gave each of them ten roubles. When he had already settled into the carriage, Smerdyakov skipped up to straighten the rug.

“You see… I am going to Chermashnya…” somehow broke from Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly; again, as yesterday, it slipped out by itself, and with a sort of nervous little laugh besides. He remembered it for a long time afterwards.

“So it is true what people say, that it is worth talking to a clever man,” Smerdyakov answered firmly, with a penetrating look at Ivan Fyodorovich.

The carriage moved off and raced away. The traveller’s soul was troubled, but he looked eagerly round him at the fields, the hills, the trees, at a flock of geese flying high over him across the clear sky. And suddenly he felt so good. He tried to talk to the driver, and something the peasant answered interested him terribly; but a minute later he realised that it had all gone past his ears and that, to tell the truth, he had not understood the peasant’s answer. He fell silent; it was good enough as it was: the air pure, fresh, coolish, the sky clear. The images of Alyosha and Katerina Ivanovna flashed through his mind; but he smiled quietly and quietly blew on the dear phantoms, and they flew away: “Their time will come,” he thought. They covered the stage quickly, changed horses and raced on to Volovya. “Why is it worth talking to a clever man? What did he mean by that?” It suddenly took his breath away. “And why did I tell him I was going to Chermashnya?” They galloped up to Volovya station. Ivan Fyodorovich got out of the carriage, and the drivers crowded round him. They bargained for Chermashnya, twelve versts by country road, with private horses. He told them to harness. He went into the station house, looked round, glanced at the stationmaster’s wife, and suddenly went back out onto the porch.

“I don’t need Chermashnya. Shan’t I be late for the railway at seven, lads?”

“We’ll just make it. Shall we harness, then?”

“Harness at once. Will any of you be in town tomorrow?”

“To be sure; Mitry here will be.”

“Can you do me a service, Mitry? Go to my father, Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov, and tell him I didn’t go to Chermashnya. Can you or not?”

“Why not? We’ll go; we have known Fyodor Pavlovich a long time.”

“And here is something for your tea, because he probably won’t give you anything…” Ivan Fyodorovich laughed gaily.

“And that he won’t, true enough,” Mitry laughed too. “Thank you, sir, we’ll do it without fail…”

At seven in the evening Ivan Fyodorovich got into the train and flew off to Moscow. “Away with everything that was; I have done with the old world for ever, and let there be no news or echo from it; into a new world, to new places, and no looking back!” But instead of rapture, such darkness suddenly descended on his soul, and such sorrow ached in his heart, as he had never felt before in all his life. He was thinking all night; the train flew on, and only at dawn, already coming into Moscow, did he suddenly seem to come to himself.

“I am a scoundrel!” he whispered to himself.

And Fyodor Pavlovich, having seen his son off, remained very pleased. For two whole hours he felt almost happy and sipped his brandy. But suddenly something happened in the house that was most annoying and unpleasant for everyone and at once threw Fyodor Pavlovich into great confusion: Smerdyakov went to the cellar for something and fell down from the top step. It was lucky that Marfa Ignatyevna happened to be in the yard at the time and heard it in time. She did not see the fall, but she heard the scream, a peculiar, strange scream, but one long familiar to her: the scream of an epileptic falling in a fit. Whether the fit had come on him at the moment he was going down the steps, so that he must of course have tumbled straight down unconscious, or whether, on the contrary, the fit of Smerdyakov, a known epileptic, came from the fall and the shock, could not be made out; but they found him already at the bottom of the cellar, in convulsions and spasms, thrashing about and foaming at the mouth. At first they thought he must have broken something, an arm or a leg, and hurt himself badly; but “the Lord preserved him”, as Marfa Ignatyevna put it: nothing of the kind had happened; only it was hard to get him out and carry him up from the cellar into God’s daylight. But they asked the neighbours for help and managed it somehow. Fyodor Pavlovich himself was present at the whole ceremony, and helped too, evidently frightened and as if at a loss. The sick man, however, did not come to: the fits stopped for a while, but then began again, and everyone concluded that the same thing would happen as last year, when he had also fallen from the attic by accident. They remembered that ice had been put on the crown of his head then. Some ice was still to be found in the cellar, and Marfa Ignatyevna saw to it; and towards evening Fyodor Pavlovich sent for Doctor Herzenstube, who came at once. Having examined the sick man carefully (he was the most careful and attentive doctor in the whole province, an elderly and most respectable little old man), he concluded that the fit was an extraordinary one and “might threaten danger”; that for the moment he, Herzenstube, did not yet understand it all, but that tomorrow morning, if the present remedies did not help, he would decide on others. The sick man was put to bed in the lodge, in a little room next to Grigory and Marfa Ignatyevna’s quarters. After that Fyodor Pavlovich suffered nothing but misfortune upon misfortune all day: Marfa Ignatyevna cooked the dinner, and the soup, compared with Smerdyakov’s cooking, came out “like dishwater”, and the chicken was so overdone that it was impossible to chew it. To her master’s bitter, though just, reproaches, Marfa Ignatyevna retorted that the chicken had been very old to begin with, and that she herself had never trained as a cook. Towards evening came another worry: Fyodor Pavlovich was told that Grigory, who had been unwell since the day before yesterday, had now taken almost completely to his bed; his lower back had given out. Fyodor Pavlovich finished his tea as early as possible and locked himself in the house alone. He was in a state of terrible, anxious expectation. The fact was that that very evening he was expecting Grushenka’s arrival almost for certain; at least he had received from Smerdyakov, early in the morning, almost an assurance that “she has promised to come without fail, sir.” The restless old man’s heart beat anxiously; he paced his empty rooms and listened. He had to keep his ears open: Dmitri Fyodorovich might be on the watch for her somewhere, and as soon as she knocked at the window (Smerdyakov had assured Fyodor Pavlovich two days before that he had told her where and how to knock), he must open the door as quickly as possible and on no account keep her waiting a second in the passage, lest, God forbid, she should take fright and run away. It was a fretful time for Fyodor Pavlovich, but never had his heart bathed in sweeter hope: it could be said almost for certain that this time she would come without fail!…
