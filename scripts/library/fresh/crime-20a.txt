## CHAPTER VI

All that evening until ten o’clock he spent in various taverns and sewers, going from one to another. Katya turned up somewhere too, and sang another lackey’s song, about how someone, “a scoundrel and a tyrant”,

> Began to kiss Katya.

Svidrigailov stood drinks for Katya, and the organ-grinder, and the singers, and the waiters, and two little clerks of some sort. He had taken up with these little clerks, properly speaking, because both of them had crooked noses: one nose went crookedly to the right and the other to the left. This struck Svidrigailov. At last they lured him into some pleasure garden, where he paid for them and for their admission. In this garden there was one thin, three-year-old fir tree and three little bushes. Besides that, a “Vauxhall” had been built, in essence a drinking house, but one could get tea there too, and in addition there stood a few green tables and chairs. A chorus of wretched singers and some drunken German from Munich, a sort of clown with a red nose but for some reason extremely melancholy, entertained the public. The little clerks quarrelled with some other little clerks and started a fight. Svidrigailov was chosen by them as judge. He had been judging them for a quarter of an hour, but they shouted so much that there was not the slightest possibility of making anything out. The likeliest thing was that one of them had stolen something and had even managed to sell it on the spot to a Jew who happened to be there; but, having sold it, did not want to share with his comrade. It turned out in the end that the object sold was a teaspoon belonging to the Vauxhall. They missed it in the Vauxhall, and the matter began to take on troublesome proportions. Svidrigailov paid for the spoon, got up and left the garden. It was about ten o’clock. He himself had not drunk a single drop of wine all this time and had only ordered tea for himself in the Vauxhall, and that more for form’s sake. Meanwhile the evening was stifling and gloomy. By ten o’clock terrible clouds had come up from all sides; thunder struck, and the rain poured down like a waterfall. The water did not fall in drops but lashed the earth in whole streams. Lightning flashed every minute, and one could count to five during each glare. Soaked to the skin, he reached home, locked himself in, opened his bureau, took out all his money and tore up two or three papers. Then, thrusting the money into his pocket, he was about to change his clothes, but, looking out of the window and listening to the storm and the rain, he waved his hand, took his hat and went out without locking his lodging. He went straight to Sonya’s. She was at home.

She was not alone; around her were four of Kapernaumov’s little children. Sofya Semyonovna was giving them tea. She received Svidrigailov silently and respectfully, looked with surprise at his soaked clothes, but did not say a word. The children all ran away at once in indescribable terror.

Svidrigailov sat down at the table and asked Sonya to sit beside him. She timidly prepared to listen.

“I may go to America, Sofya Semyonovna,” said Svidrigailov, “and since we are probably seeing each other for the last time, I have come to make certain arrangements. Well, did you see that lady today? I know what she said to you; there is no need to repeat it.” (Sonya made a movement and blushed.) “Those people have a well-known turn. As for your little sisters and your little brother, they really are provided for, and the money due to them I have paid out for each of them, against receipts, where it ought to go, into safe hands. You had better take these receipts yourself, though, just in case. Here, take them! Well, sir, that is done now. Here are three five-per-cent bonds, three thousand in all. Take this for yourself, for yourself alone, and let it stay between us, so that no one knows, whatever you may hear. You will need it, because, Sofya Semyonovna, to live as before is vile, and you have no need to any more.”

“I have received so much kindness from you, sir, and the orphans, sir, and the late one,” Sonya hurried, “that if I have thanked you so little until now, then... don’t think...”

“Eh, enough, enough.”

“And this money, Arkady Ivanovich, I am very grateful to you, but I don’t need it now. I can always feed myself alone; don’t think it ingratitude: if you are so charitable, then this money, sir...”

“It is for you, for you, Sofya Semyonovna, and please, without any special talk, because I have no time even. And you will need it. Rodion Romanovich has two roads: either a bullet through the head, or down the Vladimirka road to Siberia.” (Sonya looked at him wildly and trembled.) “Don’t worry, I know everything, from him himself, and I am not a chatterer; I won’t tell anyone. You taught him well that time, to go and give himself up and tell. It will be much better for him. Well, if it turns out to be the Vladimirka, he will go down it, and you will follow him, won’t you? Won’t you? Won’t you? Well, and if so, then the money will be needed. It will be needed for him, you understand? Giving it to you is the same as giving it to him. Besides, you promised to pay Amalia Ivanovna’s debt too; I heard. Why do you take on such contracts and obligations so thoughtlessly, Sofya Semyonovna? It was Katerina Ivanovna who owed money to that German woman, not you, so you might well spit on the German woman. One can’t live in the world like that. Well, sir, if anyone ever asks you, well, tomorrow or the day after, about me or concerning me (and you will be asked), don’t mention that I came to see you now, and on no account show the money or tell anyone that I gave it to you. Well, goodbye now.” (He got up from his chair.) “My regards to Rodion Romanych. By the way: keep the money for the time being with Mr Razumikhin, say. Do you know Mr Razumikhin? Of course you do. He’s not a bad fellow. Take it to him tomorrow or... when the time comes. And until then hide it well away.”

Sonya also jumped up from her chair and looked at him in fright. She very much wanted to say something, to ask something, but in the first minutes she did not dare, and did not know how to begin.

“But how will you... how will you go out now, sir, in such rain?”

“Well, to be getting ready for America and afraid of rain, he-he! Goodbye, my dear Sofya Semyonovna! Live, and live long; you will be of use to others. By the way... tell Mr Razumikhin that I sent my regards. Pass it on just like that: Arkady Ivanovich Svidrigailov sends his regards. Without fail, now.”

He went out, leaving Sonya in amazement, in fright, and with a kind of vague and heavy suspicion.

It turned out later that on that same evening, after eleven o’clock, he paid yet another very eccentric and unexpected visit. The rain still had not stopped. Soaked all over, at twenty past eleven he entered the cramped little flat of his bride’s parents, on Vasilyevsky Island, on the Third Line, on Maly Prospect. He had difficulty getting them to open, and at first caused great confusion; but Arkady Ivanovich, when he wished, was a man of most charming manners, so that the first guess of the bride’s sensible parents (a very witty one, though) that Arkady Ivanovich had probably got so drunk somewhere that he no longer knew what he was doing at once fell of its own accord. The paralysed parent was wheeled out in his chair to Arkady Ivanovich by the bride’s tender-hearted and sensible mother, who, as was her custom, at once began on certain remote questions. (This woman never asked direct questions, but always first brought into play smiles and the rubbing of hands, and then, if something had to be found out without fail and for certain, for instance when Arkady Ivanovich would be pleased to fix the wedding, she would begin with the most curious and almost greedy questions about Paris and court life there, and only afterwards come by degrees to the Third Line of Vasilyevsky Island.) At another time all this would of course have inspired great respect, but this time Arkady Ivanovich proved somehow especially impatient and flatly wished to see the bride, though he had been told at the very beginning that the bride had already gone to bed. Of course the bride appeared. Arkady Ivanovich told her straight out that he had to leave Petersburg for a while on account of a very important circumstance, and so had brought her fifteen thousand silver roubles in various notes, asking her to accept them from him as a present, since he had long meant to give her this trifle before the wedding. No particular logical connection between the present and his immediate departure, and the absolute necessity of coming for that purpose in the rain and at midnight, was of course shown by these explanations, but the affair nevertheless went off very smoothly. Even the necessary oohs and aahs, questions and surprises became somehow suddenly extraordinarily moderate and restrained; on the other hand the most ardent gratitude was expressed and even reinforced by the tears of the most sensible mother. Arkady Ivanovich got up, laughed, kissed the bride, patted her on the cheek, confirmed that he would come soon, and, noticing in her little eyes a childish curiosity, but together with it a very serious, mute question, thought for a moment, kissed her a second time, and at once sincerely regretted in his soul that the present would go immediately under lock and key in the keeping of the most sensible of mothers. He went out, leaving everyone in an extraordinarily excited state. But the tender-hearted mama at once, in a half-whisper and a rapid patter, resolved certain most important perplexities, namely, that Arkady Ivanovich was a great man, a man of affairs and connections, a rich man; God knows what goes on in his head; he took it into his head and went, he took it into his head and gave the money, and so there was nothing to wonder at. Of course it was strange that he was soaked through, but the English, for instance, are even more eccentric, and all these people of high tone pay no attention to what is said about them and don’t stand on ceremony. Perhaps he even goes about like that on purpose, to show that he is afraid of no one. And above all, not a word to anyone about this, because God knows what may come of it yet, and the money under lock and key as quickly as possible, and of course the best thing in all this was that Fedosya had stayed in the kitchen, and above all on no account, on no account, on no account must anything be told to that sly Resslich woman, and so on and so on. They sat whispering until two o’clock. The bride, however, went to bed much earlier, surprised and a little sad.

And Svidrigailov meanwhile, at midnight exactly, was crossing — Bridge in the direction of the Petersburg Side. The rain had stopped, but the wind was roaring. He was beginning to shiver, and for a minute he looked with a kind of special curiosity, and even questioningly, at the black water of the Little Neva. But soon it seemed to him very cold to stand over the water; he turned and went along — Prospect. He walked along the endless — Prospect for a very long time, almost half an hour, more than once stumbling in the dark on the wooden paving, but did not stop searching with curiosity for something on the right side of the prospect. Somewhere here, already at the end of the prospect, he had noticed, driving past not long ago, a hotel, a wooden one but large, and its name, as far as he remembered, was something like the Adrianople. He was not mistaken in his reckoning: this hotel, in such a wilderness, was so conspicuous a point that it was impossible not to find it, even in the dark. It was a long, wooden, blackened building, in which, in spite of the late hour, lights were still burning and some animation could be noticed. He went in and asked a ragged fellow who met him in the corridor for a room. The ragged fellow, looking Svidrigailov over, roused himself and at once led him to a remote room, stuffy and cramped, somewhere at the very end of the corridor, in a corner, under the stairs. But there was no other; all were taken. The ragged fellow looked at him questioningly.

“Is there tea?” asked Svidrigailov.

“That can be done, sir.”

“What else is there?”

“Veal, sir, vodka, sir, snacks, sir.”

“Bring veal and tea.”

“And nothing else will be wanted?” the ragged fellow asked, even in some perplexity.

“Nothing, nothing!”

The ragged fellow withdrew, completely disappointed.

“It must be a fine place,” thought Svidrigailov. “How is it I didn’t know it. I too probably look like a man coming back from some café-chantant who has already had some adventure on the way. But it is curious, though, who stays and spends the night here?”

He lit a candle and examined the room in more detail. It was a little cage, so small that it was hardly high enough for Svidrigailov, with one window; a very dirty bed, a plain painted table and a chair took up almost all the space. The walls looked as if they were nailed together from boards, with scuffed wallpaper so dusty and torn that its colour (yellow) could still be guessed, but no pattern could be made out any more. One part of the wall and ceiling was cut away at a slant, as usual in attics, but here the staircase ran over this slope. Svidrigailov put down the candle, sat on the bed and fell to thinking. But a strange and incessant whispering in the next little cage, sometimes rising almost to a shout, drew his attention at last. This whispering had not stopped since he came in. He listened: someone was abusing another and reproaching him almost in tears, but only one voice could be heard. Svidrigailov stood up, shaded the candle with his hand, and at once a little crack gleamed in the wall; he went up to it and began to look. In a room somewhat larger than his own there were two guests. One of them, without his frock coat, with an extremely curly head and a red, inflamed face, stood in an orator’s pose, his legs apart to keep his balance, and, striking his breast with his hand, was pathetically reproaching the other for being a beggar and not even having any rank, for his having dragged him out of the mud and being able to throw him out whenever he liked, and that all this was seen only by the finger of the Most High. The reproached friend sat on a chair and looked like a man who extremely wants to sneeze but cannot manage it. Now and then he looked at the orator with a sheepish and bleary look, but obviously had no idea what it was about and was hardly even hearing anything. On the table a candle was burning down, and there stood an almost empty carafe of vodka, wine glasses, bread, tumblers, cucumbers and the cups of tea drunk long ago. Having examined this picture attentively, Svidrigailov indifferently moved away from the crack and sat down on the bed.

The ragged fellow, coming back with the tea and the veal, could not help asking once more: “isn’t anything else needed?”, and, hearing again the answer no, withdrew for good. Svidrigailov fell on the tea to warm himself and drank a glass, but could not eat a morsel, having completely lost his appetite. A fever was evidently beginning in him. He took off his coat and his jacket, wrapped himself in the blanket and lay down on the bed. He was vexed: “It would have been better to be well this time, at least,” he thought, and smiled. The room was stuffy, the candle burned dimly, the wind was roaring outside, somewhere in a corner a mouse was scratching, and the whole room seemed to smell of mice and of something leathery. He lay as if dreaming: thought followed thought; it seemed he would very much have liked to fasten his imagination on something in particular. “There must be some garden under the window,” he thought, “the trees are rustling; how I dislike the rustle of trees at night, in a storm and in the dark; a nasty sensation!” And he remembered how, passing Petrovsky Park earlier, he had thought of it even with disgust. Then he remembered — Bridge too, and the Little Neva, and again he seemed to grow cold, as he had earlier when he stood over the water. “Never in my life have I liked water, even in landscapes,” he thought again, and suddenly smiled again at a strange thought: “Why, it seems that now I ought not to care about all this aesthetics and comfort, and yet just here I have become choosy, like an animal that must choose a place for itself... in a similar case. I ought to have turned into the Petrovsky earlier! It seemed dark, I suppose, cold, he-he! Practically needed pleasant sensations!.. By the way, why don’t I put out the candle?” (He blew it out.) “The neighbours have gone to bed,” he thought, seeing no light in the crack of before. “Well, Marfa Petrovna, now would be the time for you to come: it is dark, and the place is suitable, and the moment original. And just now you won’t come...”

For some reason he suddenly remembered how earlier, an hour before carrying out his design on Dunechka, he had recommended Raskolnikov to entrust her to Razumikhin’s protection. “Really, I said it mostly to provoke myself then, as Raskolnikov guessed. A rogue, though, that Raskolnikov! He has carried a great deal on himself. He may become a great rogue in time, when the nonsense has jumped out of him, but now he wants to live far too much! On that point those people are scoundrels. Well, the devil take him, let him do as he likes; what is it to me.”

He still could not sleep. Little by little the image of Dunechka from earlier began to rise before him, and suddenly a trembling passed through his body. “No, I must give that up now,” he thought, coming to himself, “I must think of something else. Strange and funny: I never had any great hatred for anyone, never even especially wished for revenge, and that is a bad sign, a bad sign! I didn’t like arguing either, and never got heated; also a bad sign! And how much I promised her earlier: pah, the devil! And yet perhaps she would have ground me down somehow...” He fell silent again and clenched his teeth: again the image of Dunechka appeared before him exactly as she had been when, having fired the first time, she took terrible fright, lowered the revolver and, gone numb, looked at him, so that he would have had time to seize her twice, and she would not have raised a hand in defence if he had not reminded her himself. He remembered how at that moment he had felt as if sorry for her, as if his heart had been squeezed... “Eh! To the devil! These thoughts again; all this must be dropped, dropped!..”

He was already dozing off; the feverish trembling was subsiding, when suddenly something seemed to run under the blanket over his arm and his leg. He started: “Pah, the devil, I believe it’s a mouse!” he thought. “I left the veal on the table...” He terribly did not want to uncover himself, get up and freeze, but suddenly again something brushed unpleasantly against his leg; he tore off the blanket and lit the candle. Trembling with feverish cold, he bent down to examine the bed: there was nothing; he shook the blanket, and suddenly a mouse jumped out onto the sheet. He rushed to catch it; but the mouse did not run off the bed, but flashed in zigzags in all directions, slipped out from under his fingers, ran over his arm and suddenly darted under the pillow; he threw off the pillow, but in an instant felt something jump inside his shirt, brushing over his body, and already behind, on his back, under the shirt. He trembled nervously and woke up. The room was dark; he was lying on the bed, wrapped as before in the blanket; the wind was howling under the window. “What vileness!” he thought with vexation.

He got up and sat on the edge of the bed with his back to the window. “Better not to sleep at all,” he decided. It was cold and damp from the window, however; without getting up, he pulled the blanket over himself and wrapped himself in it. He did not light the candle. He was not thinking of anything and did not want to think; but dreams rose one after another, scraps of thoughts flashed by, without beginning or end and without connection. It was as if he were falling into a half-doze. Whether it was the cold, or the darkness, or the damp, or the wind howling under the window and shaking the trees that called up in him some persistent fantastic inclination and desire, he kept seeing flowers. He imagined a lovely landscape; a bright, almost hot day, a holiday, Trinity Sunday. A rich, luxurious country cottage in the English taste, all overgrown with fragrant beds of flowers, planted round with borders running all round the house; a porch twined with climbing plants and lined with beds of roses; a light, cool staircase laid with a sumptuous carpet and set about with rare flowers in Chinese jars. He particularly noticed in the jars of water on the windowsills bunches of white, delicate narcissi, bending on their bright green, fleshy, long stalks, with a strong aromatic scent. He did not even want to move away from them, but he went up the stairs and entered a large, high hall, and again here too, everywhere, by the windows, by the open doors to the terrace, on the terrace itself, everywhere there were flowers. The floors were strewn with freshly mown fragrant grass, the windows were open, fresh, light, cool air came into the room, little birds were chirping under the windows, and in the middle of the hall, on tables covered with white satin cloths, stood a coffin. This coffin was covered with white gros de Naples and edged with a thick white ruche. Garlands of flowers wound round it on all sides. All in flowers there lay in it a girl, in a white tulle dress, her hands folded and pressed to her breast, as if carved out of marble. But her loose hair, the hair of a light blonde, was wet; a wreath of roses was twined round her head. The stern and already rigid profile of her face was also as if carved out of marble, but the smile on her pale lips was full of a kind of unchildlike, boundless grief and great complaint. Svidrigailov knew this girl; there was no icon or lighted candles at this coffin, and no prayers could be heard. This girl was a suicide, drowned. She was only fourteen, but hers was already a broken heart, and it had destroyed itself, wounded by an outrage that had horrified and amazed this young, childish consciousness, flooded her angelically pure soul with undeserved shame and torn from her a last cry of despair, unheard, but insolently trampled on, in a dark night, in the gloom, in the cold, in a damp thaw, when the wind was howling...

Svidrigailov came to himself, got up from the bed and stepped to the window. He felt for the latch and opened the window. The wind rushed furiously into his cramped little room and as if with frost plastered his face and his chest, covered only by a shirt. Under the window there really must have been something like a garden, and it seems a pleasure garden too; probably in the daytime singers sang here as well and tea was brought out to the little tables. But now spray was flying from the trees and bushes into the window; it was dark as a cellar, so that only some dark patches could barely be made out, marking objects. Svidrigailov, bending down and leaning his elbows on the windowsill, had been looking for five minutes now, without tearing himself away, into this murk. Amid the darkness and the night a cannon shot rang out, and after it another.

“Ah, the signal! The water is rising,” he thought. “By morning it will pour into the streets, where the ground is lower, flood the basements and cellars, the basement rats will float up, and amid the rain and wind people will begin, cursing, wet, to drag their rubbish up to the upper floors... And what time is it now?” And just as he thought this, somewhere close by, ticking and as if hurrying with all its might, a wall clock struck three. “Ehe, in an hour it will be getting light! Why wait? I’ll go out now, go straight to the Petrovsky: somewhere there I’ll choose a big bush, all drenched with rain, so that you have only to brush it with your shoulder and millions of drops will shower your whole head...” He moved away from the window, shut it, lit the candle, pulled on his waistcoat and coat, put on his hat and went out with the candle into the corridor, to find the ragged fellow asleep somewhere in a little closet among all sorts of junk and candle ends, pay him for the room and leave the hotel. “The very best moment; one couldn’t choose a better!”

He walked a long time along the whole long, narrow corridor without finding anyone, and was about to call out loudly, when suddenly, in a dark corner between an old cupboard and a door, he made out some strange object, something seemingly alive. He bent down with the candle and saw a child, a girl of five, no more, in a little dress soaked like a floor cloth, trembling and crying. She did not seem frightened of Svidrigailov, but looked at him with dull surprise with her big black eyes, and now and then sobbed, like children who have cried for a long time but have already stopped and are even comforted, and yet every so often suddenly sob again. The little girl’s face was pale and worn out; she was stiff with cold, but “how did she get here? So she must have hidden here and not slept all night.” He began to question her. The little girl suddenly brightened up and began to babble something to him very, very quickly in her child’s language. There was something about “mummy”, and that “mummy will beat me”, about some cup that she had “bwoken”. The little girl talked without stopping; from all these stories one could somehow guess that this was an unloved child, whom her mother, some always drunken cook, probably from this very hotel, had beaten and terrified; that the little girl had broken her mother’s cup and was so frightened that she had run away in the evening; she had probably hidden for a long time somewhere in the yard, in the rain, at last got in here, hidden behind the cupboard and sat here in the corner all night, crying, trembling from the damp, from the dark and from fear that she would now be beaten hard for all this. He took her in his arms, went to his room, sat her on the bed and began to undress her. Her little shoes, full of holes, on her bare feet, were as wet as if they had lain all night in a puddle. Having undressed her, he laid her on the bed, covered her and wrapped her completely, head and all, in the blanket. She fell asleep at once. Having finished all this, he again fell gloomily to thinking.

“And now I have taken it into my head to get mixed up with this!” he decided suddenly, with a heavy and malicious feeling. “What nonsense!” In vexation he took the candle, to go and find the ragged fellow at all costs and leave here quickly. “Eh, the girl!” he thought with a curse, already opening the door, but he came back once more to look at the girl, whether she was asleep and how she was sleeping. He carefully lifted the blanket. The little girl was sleeping a sound and blissful sleep. She had grown warm under the blanket, and colour had already spread over her pale little cheeks. But strangely: this colour showed as if brighter and stronger than an ordinary child’s flush could be. “It is a feverish flush,” thought Svidrigailov; it was exactly like a flush from wine, exactly as if she had been given a whole glass to drink. Her scarlet little lips were as if burning, glowing; but what is this? It suddenly seemed to him that her long black lashes were quivering and blinking, as if lifting, and from under them a sly, sharp, somehow unchildlike winking little eye was peeping out, as if the little girl were not asleep but pretending. Yes, so it is: her little lips are parting in a smile; the corners of her lips are quivering, as if still holding back. But now she has quite stopped holding back; this is laughter now, open laughter; something insolent, provoking shines in this face that is not at all a child’s; it is depravity, it is the face of a camellia, the insolent face of a French courtesan for sale. Now, no longer hiding at all, both eyes open: they run over him with a fiery and shameless look, they call him, they laugh... There was something infinitely hideous and outrageous in that laughter, in those eyes, in all this vileness in a child’s face. “What! five years old!” Svidrigailov whispered in real horror. “This... what is this?” But now she is turning her whole burning little face to him, holding out her arms... “Ah, cursed thing!” cried Svidrigailov in horror, raising his hand over her... But at that very moment he woke up.

He is on the same bed, wrapped in the blanket just the same; the candle is not lit, and full daylight is already whitening in the windows.

“Nightmare all night long!” He raised himself angrily, feeling that he was all broken; his bones ached. Outside there was a completely thick fog and nothing could be made out. It was getting on for five; he had overslept! He got up and put on his jacket and coat, still damp. Feeling the revolver in his pocket, he took it out and adjusted the cap; then he sat down, took a notebook out of his pocket and on the title page, the most noticeable one, wrote a few lines in a large hand. Having reread them, he fell to thinking, his elbows on the table. The revolver and the notebook lay right there, by his elbow. Flies that had woken up were clinging to the untouched portion of veal that stood right there on the table. He looked at them for a long time, and at last began trying to catch one fly with his free right hand. He exhausted himself for a long time in the effort but could not catch it. At last, catching himself at this interesting occupation, he came to himself, started, got up and walked resolutely out of the room. A minute later he was in the street.

A thick, milky fog lay over the city. Svidrigailov walked along the slippery, dirty wooden paving towards the Little Neva. He imagined the water of the Little Neva risen high during the night, Petrovsky Island, the wet paths, the wet grass, the wet trees and bushes, and at last that very bush... In vexation he began examining the houses, so as to think of something else. He met neither a passer-by nor a cab along the prospect. The bright yellow wooden houses with their shutters closed looked dismal and dirty. The cold and damp went through his whole body, and he began to shiver. Now and then he came upon shop and greengrocers’ signs and read each one carefully. Now the wooden paving had ended. He was already level with a large stone house. A dirty, chilled little dog with its tail between its legs ran across his path. Some dead-drunk man in a greatcoat lay face down across the pavement. He looked at him and went on. A tall watchtower flashed on his left. “Ba!” he thought. “Why, here is the place; why go to the Petrovsky? At least with an official witness...” He almost smiled at this new thought and turned into — Street. Here stood the large building with the watchtower. At the large closed gates of the building, leaning his shoulder against them, stood a small man wrapped in a grey soldier’s greatcoat and wearing a brass Achilles helmet. With a drowsy look he glanced coldly sideways at Svidrigailov as he came up. On his face could be seen that age-old peevish grief which is imprinted so sourly on every face, without exception, of the Jewish tribe. The two of them, Svidrigailov and Achilles, examined each other silently for some time. At last it seemed to Achilles out of order that a man who was not drunk should stand before him three paces away, staring point-blank and saying nothing.

“And vat, vat do you vant here?” he said, still not stirring or changing his position.

“Why, nothing, brother; good morning!” answered Svidrigailov.

“Zis is not ze place.”

“I am going to foreign parts, brother.”

“To foreign parts?”

“To America.”

“To America?”

Svidrigailov took out the revolver and cocked it. Achilles raised his eyebrows.

“And vat, vat, zese jokes are not for here!”

“But why isn’t this the place?”

“Because it is not ze place.”

“Well, brother, it makes no difference. The place is a good one; if they ask you, just answer that he went, so to speak, to America.”

He put the revolver to his right temple.

“But here you can’t, zis is not ze place!” Achilles started up, his pupils growing wider and wider.

Svidrigailov pulled the trigger.
