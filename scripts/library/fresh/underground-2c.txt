## Part Two · Apropos of the Wet Snow

> When, out of the darkness of error,
> with a burning word of conviction
> I drew your fallen soul,
> and, full of deep torment,
> wringing your hands, you cursed
> the vice that had ensnared you;
> ...
> and suddenly, covering your face with your hands,
> full of shame and horror,
> you dissolved in tears,
> shaken, overwhelmed...
> And so on, and so on, and so on.
— from the poetry of N. A. Nekrasov

## I

At that time I was only twenty-four. Even then my life was gloomy, disordered and lonely to the point of savagery. I had nothing to do with anyone, I even avoided talking, and I buried myself deeper and deeper in my corner. At work, in the office, I even tried not to look at anyone, and I noticed very well that my colleagues not only considered me an oddity but, as it also seemed to me, looked at me with a kind of disgust. It occurred to me: why does nobody but me feel that people look at him with disgust? One of the clerks in our office had a repulsive, badly pock-marked face, even a sort of bandit’s face. I don’t think I would have dared to look at anyone with such an indecent face. Another had a uniform so worn that there was a bad smell near him. And yet not one of these gentlemen was embarrassed, either about his clothes, or about his face, or in any moral way. Neither of them imagined that people looked at them with disgust; and even if they had imagined it, they would not have cared, as long as it was not their superiors who were looking. It is perfectly clear to me now that, because of my boundless vanity, and therefore my demands on myself, I very often looked at myself with a furious dissatisfaction that reached the point of disgust, and so in my mind I attributed my view of myself to everyone else. I hated my face, for example; I thought it vile, and I even suspected that there was something mean in its expression, and so every time I came to work I tried agonisingly to carry myself as independently as possible, so that no one would suspect me of meanness, and to express as much nobility as possible with my face. “Let it be an ugly face,” I thought, “but let it be a noble face, expressive and, above all, extremely intelligent.” But I knew for certain, and it hurt, that my face would never express all these perfections. And what was most horrible of all, I found it positively stupid. I would have been quite content with intelligence. So much so that I would even have agreed to a mean expression, if only my face were thought terribly intelligent at the same time.

Of course I hated all our clerks, from the first to the last, and despised them all, and at the same time I was somehow afraid of them. Sometimes I would suddenly even rank them above myself. It happened to me suddenly in those days: one moment I despised them, the next I ranked them above myself. A developed and decent man cannot be vain without making boundless demands on himself and without despising himself at certain moments to the point of hatred. But whether I despised them or ranked them above myself, I lowered my eyes before almost everyone I met. I even made experiments: could I bear this or that person’s gaze on me? And I was always the first to look down. This tormented me to the point of fury. I was also morbidly afraid of being ridiculous, and so I slavishly worshipped routine in everything external; I lovingly fell into the common rut and was frightened with my whole soul of any eccentricity in myself. But how could I keep it up? I was morbidly developed, as a man of our time ought to be. They, on the other hand, were all dull and as like one another as sheep in a flock. Perhaps I was the only one in the whole office who constantly felt that I was a coward and a slave; and I felt it precisely because I was developed. But I did not only feel it: it really was so. I was a coward and a slave. I say this without any embarrassment. Every decent man of our time is, and must be, a coward and a slave. That is his normal condition. I am deeply convinced of it. He is made that way and built for it. And not just at the present time, because of some chance circumstances, but in general, at all times, a decent man must be a coward and a slave. That is the law of nature for all decent people on earth. If one of them happens to show courage about something, let him not take comfort in it or get carried away by it: he will lose his nerve before something else all the same. That is the one and everlasting way out. Only donkeys and their mongrels show courage, and even they only up to a certain wall. They are not worth paying attention to, because they mean absolutely nothing.

There was one more thing that tormented me then: namely, that nobody was like me and I was like nobody. “I am one, and they are all,” I thought, and fell to brooding.

From this you can see that I was still just a boy.

The opposite happened too. Sometimes going to the office became so loathsome that many times I came home from work ill. But suddenly, for no reason at all, a phase of scepticism and indifference would set in (everything with me came in phases), and then I myself would laugh at my intolerance and squeamishness, and reproach myself for romanticism. At one moment I did not want to talk to anyone, and at another I would go so far as not only to get talking but even to think of making friends with them. All my squeamishness would suddenly vanish at once, for no reason. Who knows, perhaps I never had it at all, and it was put on, out of books? I have not settled that question to this day. Once I even became real friends with them; I began visiting their homes, playing preference, drinking vodka, talking about promotions... But here allow me to make a digression.

We Russians, generally speaking, have never had those stupid, starry-eyed German and especially French romantics, on whom nothing has any effect: let the earth crack beneath them, let all of France perish on the barricades, they stay the same, they won’t change even for the sake of decency, and they go on singing their starry-eyed songs, so to speak, to the grave, because they are fools. Here, in the Russian land, there are no fools; that is well known; that is what distinguishes us from the other, German lands. Consequently, starry-eyed natures are not found among us in their pure state. It was all those “positive” journalists and critics of ours at the time, hunting for ideal landowners and sensible uncles out of novels, and foolishly taking them for our ideal, who made up these things about our romantics, taking them to be as starry-eyed as in Germany or France. On the contrary, the qualities of our romantic are completely and directly opposite to the starry-eyed European kind, and not a single European yardstick fits here. (Allow me to use this word “romantic”: an old-fashioned little word, respectable, well-earned and familiar to everyone.) The qualities of our romantic are: to understand everything, to see everything, and often to see it incomparably more clearly than our most positive minds see it; not to make peace with anyone or anything, but at the same time not to turn up one’s nose at anything; to get round everything, to give way to everything, to treat everyone with tact; never to lose sight of a useful, practical aim (some little government apartment, a little pension, a little decoration); to keep this aim in view through all the enthusiasms and volumes of lyric verse, and at the same time to preserve “the beautiful and sublime” in oneself intact to the grave; and, by the way, to preserve oneself too, wrapped in cotton wool like some piece of jewellery, if only, for example, for the sake of that same “beautiful and sublime”. Our romantic is a broad man, and the foremost rogue of all our rogues, I assure you of that... even from experience. All this, of course, if the romantic is intelligent. But what am I saying! The romantic is always intelligent; I only meant to note that although we did have fool romantics, they don’t count, for the sole reason that while still in the bloom of their strength they turned completely into Germans and, the better to preserve their piece of jewellery, settled somewhere over there, mostly in Weimar or the Black Forest. I, for example, sincerely despised my work at the office, and the only reason I did not spit on it was necessity, because I sat there myself and was paid for it. And as a result, note, I did not spit on it. Our romantic will sooner go mad (which, however, happens very rarely) than spit on it, if he has no other career in view; and he is never thrown out on his ear, but at most carted off to the madhouse as “the King of Spain”, and even then only if he goes very mad. But among us only the thin little fair-haired ones go mad. A countless number of romantics, though, later rise to considerable rank. An extraordinary many-sidedness! And what a capacity for the most contradictory feelings! I was consoled by this even then, and I think the same now. That is why we have so many “broad natures” who, even in their very lowest fall, never lose their ideal; and although they won’t lift a finger for that ideal, although they are notorious robbers and thieves, still they respect their original ideal to the point of tears and are extraordinarily honest at heart. Yes, sir, only among us can the most notorious scoundrel be completely, even sublimely, honest at heart, while not ceasing in the least to be a scoundrel. I repeat: again and again our romantics turn into such businesslike rascals (I use the word “rascals” lovingly), and suddenly show such a feel for reality and such knowledge of practical matters, that their astonished superiors and the public can only click their tongues at them in amazement.

A truly astonishing many-sidedness; and God knows what it will turn into and develop into in later circumstances, and what it promises us in the future. Not bad material, sir! I am not saying this out of some ridiculous, flag-waving patriotism. But I am sure you think again that I am laughing. And who knows, perhaps the reverse: perhaps you are sure that I really do think so. In any case, gentlemen, I will consider both your opinions an honour and a special pleasure. And forgive me my digression.

Of course, I did not keep up my friendship with my colleagues; I very soon fell out with them and, because of my youthful inexperience at the time, I even stopped greeting them, as if I had cut them off. That, however, happened to me only once. In general I was always alone.

At home, first of all, I mostly read. I wanted to drown everything that was constantly boiling up inside me with sensations from outside. And the only outside sensation possible for me was reading. Reading, of course, helped a great deal: it excited me, delighted me and tormented me. But at times it bored me terribly. I still wanted to move, and suddenly I would plunge into dark, underground, nasty... not debauchery, but petty debauchery. My little passions were sharp and burning because of my constant, morbid irritability. My outbursts were hysterical, with tears and convulsions. Besides reading, there was nowhere to go; that is, there was nothing in my surroundings at that time that I could respect or that drew me. On top of that, misery boiled up; there came a hysterical thirst for contradictions and contrasts, and so I took to debauchery. I haven’t said all this now to justify myself at all... But no, I am lying! I did want to justify myself. I am making a little note of that for myself, gentlemen. I don’t want to lie. I gave my word.

I went in for debauchery alone, at night, secretly, timidly, dirtily, with a shame that never left me even in the most loathsome moments, and that in such moments even reached the point of cursing. Even then I carried the underground in my soul. I was terribly afraid of being seen somehow, of being met, of being recognised. And I went to various very dark places.

Once, passing a little tavern at night, I saw through a lighted window some gentlemen fighting with billiard cues by the billiard table, and one of them being thrown out of the window. At another time I would have felt sick at it; but at that moment such a mood came over me that I envied the gentleman who had been thrown out, and envied him so much that I even went into the tavern, into the billiard room: “Perhaps,” I thought, “I’ll get into a fight too, and they’ll throw me out of the window as well.”

I was not drunk; but what can you do? Misery can eat you up to the point of such hysteria! But nothing came of it. It turned out that I was not even capable of jumping out of a window, and I left without a fight.

An officer put me in my place there at my very first step.

I was standing by the billiard table and, without realising it, blocking the way, and he needed to get past; he took me by the shoulders and silently, without warning or explanation, moved me from the place where I was standing to another, and walked past as if he had not even noticed. I could even have forgiven a beating, but I could never forgive his moving me and so completely failing to notice me.

The devil knows what I would have given then for a real, more proper quarrel, a more decent, more, so to speak, literary one! I had been treated like a fly. This officer was very tall, well over six feet; while I am a short, scrawny man. The quarrel, however, was in my hands: I had only to protest, and of course they would have thrown me out of the window. But I thought better of it and preferred... to fade away, seething.

I left the tavern confused and agitated and went straight home, and the next day I went on with my petty debauchery still more timidly, more crushed and sadder than before, as if with tears in my eyes; but I went on all the same. Don’t think, however, that I backed down before the officer out of cowardice: I have never been a coward at heart, although in practice I have constantly lost my nerve; but wait before you laugh, there is an explanation for this; I have an explanation for everything, you may be sure.

Oh, if only this officer had been one of those who agree to fight duels! But no, he was precisely one of those gentlemen (alas, long since vanished) who preferred to act with billiard cues or, like Lieutenant Pirogov in Gogol, through the authorities. They did not fight duels, and with people like us, civilians, they would have considered a duel improper in any case; and in general they thought a duel something unthinkable, free-thinking, French, while they themselves insulted people freely enough, especially when they were over six feet tall.

I lost my nerve here not out of cowardice but out of the most boundless vanity. I was not afraid of his height, or of being painfully beaten and thrown out of the window; I really would have had enough physical courage; but I lacked moral courage. I was afraid that everyone present, from the insolent billiard marker to the last rotten, pimply little clerk with a greasy collar who was hanging about there, would not understand and would laugh at me when I protested and spoke to them in literary language. Because about a point of honour, that is, not about honour, but about a point of honour (*point d’honneur*), one cannot speak among us even now except in literary language. In ordinary language a “point of honour” is never mentioned. I was quite sure (my feel for reality, in spite of all my romanticism!) that they would all simply split their sides laughing, and that the officer would not simply, that is, not harmlessly, beat me, but would certainly kick me round the billiard table with his knee, and only then perhaps take pity and throw me out of the window. Of course, this wretched story could not end for me with that alone. Afterwards I often met this officer in the street and took good note of him. Only I don’t know whether he recognised me. Probably not; I conclude this from certain signs. But I, I looked at him with spite and hatred, and so it went on... for several years, sir! My spite even grew stronger and larger with the years. At first I quietly began to make inquiries about this officer. It was hard for me, because I knew nobody. But once someone called out his surname in the street while I was following him at a distance, as if tied to him, and so I learned his name. Another time I followed him all the way to his flat, and for ten kopecks I learned from the porter where he lived, on which floor, whether alone or with someone, and so on; in a word, everything one can learn from a porter. One morning, though I had never written anything, the idea suddenly came to me of describing this officer in an exposé, as a caricature, in the form of a story. I wrote this story with delight. I exposed him, I even slandered him; at first I disguised his name so that it could be recognised at once, but then, on mature reflection, I changed it and sent the story to *Notes of the Fatherland*. But exposés were not yet in fashion then, and my story was not printed. I was very annoyed. Sometimes my spite simply choked me. At last I decided to challenge my opponent to a duel. I composed a beautiful, charming letter to him, begging him to apologise to me, and in case of refusal hinting fairly firmly at a duel. The letter was composed in such a way that if the officer had understood even a little of “the beautiful and sublime”, he would certainly have come running to me to throw his arms round my neck and offer me his friendship. And how good that would have been! How we would have lived! How we would have lived! He would have protected me with his rank; I would have ennobled him with my culture, and, well... with ideas, and so much else might have come of it! Just imagine: by then two years had passed since he had insulted me, and my challenge was the most hideous anachronism, in spite of all the cleverness of my letter in explaining and covering up the anachronism. But, thank God (to this day I thank the Almighty with tears), I did not send my letter. It sends a shiver down my spine when I remember what might have happened if I had sent it. And suddenly... and suddenly I took my revenge in the simplest, the most brilliant way! A most luminous idea suddenly dawned on me.

Sometimes on holidays I used to go to Nevsky Prospect at about four o’clock and stroll along the sunny side. That is, I did not stroll there at all, but went through countless torments, humiliations and overflowings of bile; but that, no doubt, was just what I needed. I darted like an eel, in the most unsightly way, among the passers-by, constantly making way now for generals, now for officers of the Horse Guards and the Hussars, now for ladies; at those moments I felt convulsive pains in my heart and heat down my back at the mere thought of the wretchedness of my clothes, the wretchedness and vulgarity of my little darting figure. It was sheer torture, a constant, unbearable humiliation from the thought, which turned into a constant and direct sensation, that I was a fly in front of all these people, a nasty, obscene fly; cleverer than all of them, more developed than all of them, nobler than all of them, that goes without saying, but a fly that constantly made way for everyone, humiliated by everyone and insulted by everyone. Why I took this torment upon myself, why I went to the Nevsky, I don’t know; but I was simply drawn there at every opportunity.

Even then I was beginning to feel surges of those pleasures I spoke of in the first chapter. And after the incident with the officer I was drawn there even more strongly: it was on the Nevsky that I met him most often, and there I could admire him. He too went there mostly on holidays. Although he too turned out of the way for generals and important persons, and also wriggled like an eel among them, people like me, or even a little better than me, he simply crushed; he walked straight at them as if there were empty space in front of him, and on no account gave way. I revelled in my spite as I looked at him, and... spitefully turned aside for him every time. It tormented me that even in the street I could not be on an equal footing with him. “Why must you always be the first to turn aside?” I nagged myself, in furious hysterics, sometimes waking at three in the morning. “Why you, and not him? There’s no law about it, it isn’t written anywhere, is it? Let it be equal, as it usually is when well-mannered people meet: he gives way half, and you give way half, and you pass each other with mutual respect.” But it was not like that, and I still turned aside, while he did not even notice that I made way for him. And then a most amazing idea suddenly dawned on me. “What if,” I thought, “what if I meet him and... don’t step aside? Deliberately don’t step aside, even if I have to bump into him: how would that be?” Little by little this bold idea took such possession of me that it gave me no peace. I dreamed of it constantly, terribly, and I went to the Nevsky more often on purpose, to picture to myself even more clearly how I would do it when I did it. I was in raptures. More and more this plan seemed to me both likely and possible. “Not really pushing him, of course,” I thought, already growing kinder in advance from joy, “just not stepping aside, bumping into him, not so as to hurt very much, but just shoulder to shoulder, exactly as much as decency allows; so that as hard as he bumps me, I bump him back.” At last I was completely decided. But the preparations took a very long time. The first thing was that I needed to look more respectable while carrying it out, and to see to my clothes. “Just in case, for example, a public scene develops (and the public there is superfine: a countess walks there, Prince D. walks there, the whole literary world walks there), one must be well dressed; that impresses people, and it will put us at once, in a way, on an equal footing in the eyes of high society.” With this aim I asked for an advance on my salary and bought black gloves and a decent hat at Churkin’s. Black gloves seemed to me both more solid and more stylish than the lemon-coloured ones I had had my eye on at first. “The colour is too loud, too much as if the man wants to show off,” and I did not take the lemon ones. A good shirt, with white bone studs, I had got ready long before; but the overcoat held me up badly.

My overcoat in itself was not at all bad, and it was warm; but it was padded with cotton wool, and its collar was raccoon, which was the height of a lackey’s taste. I had to change the collar at any cost and get a beaver one, like the officers’. For this I began going round the Gostiny Dvor arcade, and after several tries I set my sights on a cheap German beaver. These German beavers wear out very quickly and soon look wretched, but at first, when they are new, they look even quite decent; and after all I needed it for only one occasion. I asked the price: it was still expensive. After thorough reflection I decided to sell my raccoon collar. The rest of the sum, which was very considerable for me, I decided to borrow from Anton Antonych Setochkin, my head of section, a meek man but a serious and positive one, who never lent money to anyone, but to whom I had once, on entering the service, been specially recommended by the important person who had got me the post. I suffered terribly. To ask Anton Antonych for money seemed to me monstrous and shameful. I even did not sleep for two or three nights; and in general I slept little then, I was feverish; my heart would sink vaguely, or suddenly begin to jump, jump, jump!.. Anton Antonych was surprised at first, then frowned, then thought it over, and lent me the money after all, taking a receipt from me entitling him to take the borrowed money out of my salary in two weeks. And so everything was ready at last; the handsome beaver reigned in place of the vile raccoon, and little by little I began to set about the business. It would not do to decide at the very first try, rashly; the thing had to be managed skilfully, precisely little by little. But I confess that after many attempts I even began to despair: we simply would not collide, and that was that! Hadn’t I prepared, hadn’t I intended? It seemed we were just about to collide, and I look, and once again I have made way, and he has passed without noticing me. I even said prayers as I approached him, asking God to fill me with resolve. Once I had quite made up my mind, but it ended only with my getting under his feet, because at the very last moment, a couple of inches away, my nerve failed. He walked calmly over me, and I bounced aside like a ball. That night I was ill again with fever and delirious. And suddenly it all ended in the best possible way. The night before, I had finally resolved not to carry out my ruinous plan and to let it all come to nothing, and with this aim I went out to the Nevsky for the last time, just to see how I would let it all come to nothing. Suddenly, three steps from my enemy, I unexpectedly made up my mind, shut my eyes, and... we bumped hard, shoulder to shoulder! I did not yield an inch and walked past on a completely equal footing! He did not even look round, and pretended not to have noticed; but he only pretended, I am sure of it. I am sure of it to this day! Of course, I got the worst of it; he was stronger; but that was not the point. The point was that I had reached my goal, kept up my dignity, not yielded a single step, and publicly put myself on an equal social footing with him. I came home completely avenged for everything. I was in raptures. I was triumphant, and I sang Italian arias. Of course, I won’t describe to you what happened to me three days later; if you have read my first part, “Underground”, you can guess for yourselves. The officer was later transferred somewhere; I haven’t seen him for about fourteen years now. How is he now, my dear fellow? Whom is he crushing?
