## VII

“Oh, come, Liza, what has a book to do with it, when I myself am sick of all this, as an outsider. And not just as an outsider. All this has woken up in my soul now... Can it be, can it be that you yourself are not sick of it here? No, habit evidently counts for a lot! The devil knows what habit can make of a person. Do you seriously think you’ll never grow old, that you’ll always be pretty, and that they’ll keep you here for ever and ever? I won’t even speak of how vile it is here... But let me tell you this, about your present life: now, although you are young, attractive, good, with a soul, with feeling; well, do you know that as soon as I came to myself just now, I at once felt sick at being here with you! One only comes here drunk. But if you were somewhere else, living as decent people live, then I might not just run after you, but simply fall in love with you; I’d be glad of a look from you, never mind a word; I’d wait for you at the gate; I’d kneel before you; I’d look at you as my bride, and think it an honour too. I wouldn’t dare think anything impure about you. But here I know that I have only to whistle, and whether you want to or not, you must come with me; and it is not I who ask your will, but you who must ask mine. The lowest peasant who hires himself out as a labourer still doesn’t sell the whole of himself into bondage, and he knows his term will come to an end. But where is your term? Just think: what are you giving away here? What are you selling into bondage? Your soul, your soul, which isn’t yours to sell, you are selling into bondage together with your body! You are giving your love to be defiled by any drunkard! Love! Why, that is everything; it is a diamond, a girl’s treasure, love! To deserve that love, a man might be ready to give his soul, to go to his death. And what is your love worth now? You are bought, all of you, entirely; and why should anyone strive for love, when everything is possible without love? Why, there is no greater insult for a girl, do you understand? I’ve heard they amuse you fools by letting you have lovers here. But that is only pampering, only deception, only a joke at your expense, and you believe it. Does he really love you, that lover? I don’t believe it. How can he love you, when he knows you can be called away from him any moment? He’s a filthy wretch, after that! Does he respect you even a drop? What have you in common with him? He laughs at you and robs you too: that’s all his love! It’s lucky if he doesn’t beat you. And perhaps he does. Ask him, if you have such a man: will he marry you? He’ll laugh in your face, if he doesn’t spit at you or hit you; and he himself, maybe, isn’t worth two broken farthings. And for what, think, have you ruined your life here? Because they give you coffee to drink and feed you well? But why do they feed you? An honest girl couldn’t swallow such a mouthful, because she’d know why she was being fed. You are in debt here, and you will always be in debt, to the very end, until the clients begin to turn up their noses at you. And that will come soon; don’t count on your youth. Here everything flies by like the mail coach. They’ll throw you out. And not simply throw you out: long before that they’ll start finding fault with you, reproaching you, abusing you, as if it were not you who gave her your health and threw away your youth and your soul for her for nothing, but as if it were you who had ruined her, beggared her, robbed her. And don’t expect support: your friends here will attack you too, to curry favour with her, because everyone here is a slave and lost conscience and pity long ago. They have sunk so low that there is no abuse on earth viler, meaner or more insulting than theirs. And you will lay down everything here, everything, without reserve: your health, your youth, your beauty, your hopes; and at twenty-two you’ll look thirty-five, and it’s lucky if you’re not ill: pray to God for that. I suppose you think now that you have no work at all, only a good time! But there is no harder, no more convict-like work in the world, and there never has been. One’s heart alone, it seems, would melt away in tears. And you won’t dare say a word, not half a word, when they drive you out of here; you’ll go as if you were the guilty one. You’ll move to another place, then a third, then somewhere else, and at last you’ll get down to the Haymarket. And there they’ll start beating you as a matter of course; that is their way of being polite there; a client there doesn’t know how to caress a girl without beating her. You don’t believe it’s so disgusting there? Go and look some day; perhaps you’ll see with your own eyes. I once saw one there, on New Year’s Day, at a door. Her own companions had thrown her out as a joke, to cool her off a little because she was howling so much, and they shut the door behind her. At nine in the morning she was already completely drunk, dishevelled, half-naked, beaten all over. Her face was powdered white, but her eyes were blackened; blood was running from her nose and her teeth: some cabman had just given her a going-over. She sat on the stone steps holding some salted fish; she was howling, wailing something about her ‘lot’, and beating the fish on the steps. And the cabmen and drunken soldiers crowded round the porch and teased her. You don’t believe you’ll be like that? I wouldn’t want to believe it either; but how do you know, perhaps ten or eight years ago this same woman with the salted fish came here from somewhere fresh as a little cherub, innocent, pure; she knew no evil; she blushed at every word. Perhaps she was just like you, proud, touchy, unlike the others; she looked like a princess and knew that perfect happiness awaited the man who would love her and whom she would love. You see how it ended? And what if at that very minute, when she was beating that fish on the dirty steps, drunk and dishevelled, what if at that minute she remembered all her former pure years in her father’s house, when she was still going to school, and the neighbour’s son waited for her on the road and swore he would love her all his life, that he would lay his destiny at her feet, and when they promised each other to love each other for ever and to marry as soon as they were grown up! No, Liza, it will be happiness, happiness for you, if you die quickly of consumption somewhere in a corner, in a cellar, like that woman this morning. In hospital, you say? All right, they’ll take you there; but what if your madam still needs you? Consumption is that kind of illness; it isn’t a fever. With it a person hopes to the last minute and says he is well. He comforts himself. And that suits the madam. Don’t worry, that’s how it is; you have sold your soul, and you owe money besides, so you won’t dare make a squeak. And when you are dying, everyone will abandon you, everyone will turn away, because what will they get out of you then? They’ll even reproach you for taking up room for nothing and not dying quickly. You’ll beg for a drink and they’ll give it to you with curses: ‘When are you going to croak, you slut? You keep us awake with your moaning; the clients don’t like it.’ That’s true; I’ve overheard such words myself. They’ll shove you, dying, into the stinkiest corner of the cellar, darkness, damp; what will you think about then, lying there alone? You’ll die, and they’ll lay you out in a hurry, strangers’ hands, grumbling, impatient; no one will bless you, no one will sigh for you; they’ll only want to get you off their hands as fast as they can. They’ll buy a coffin, carry you out as they carried out that poor woman today, and go to the tavern to drink to your memory. In the grave there’ll be slush, filth, wet snow; why should they stand on ceremony for you? ‘Let her down, Vanyukha; look at her “lot”, she’s gone in head first even here, that sort. Shorten the ropes, you rascal.’ ‘It’ll do as it is.’ ‘What do you mean, it’ll do? Look, she’s lying on her side. Was she a human being or not? Oh, well, never mind, fill it in.’ And they won’t want to quarrel over you for long. They’ll quickly shovel wet blue clay over you and go off to the tavern... And that will be the end of your memory on earth; other people have children who visit their graves, fathers, husbands; but for you, not a tear, not a sigh, not a prayer, and no one, no one, ever in the whole world will come to you; your name will vanish from the face of the earth, as if you had never been, never been born! Mud and marsh, and you may knock on the lid of your coffin at night when the dead rise: ‘Let me out, good people, to live in the world! I lived, but I never saw life; my life was used as a doormat; they drank it away in a tavern in the Haymarket; let me out, good people, to live in the world once more!..’”

I worked myself up into such pathos that a lump was rising in my own throat, and... suddenly I stopped, sat up in fright and, bending my head timidly, began to listen with a pounding heart. There was reason to be alarmed.

I had felt for a long time that I had turned her whole soul upside down and broken her heart, and the more certain I became of it, the more I wanted to reach my goal as quickly and as forcefully as possible. The game, the game carried me away; but not only the game...

I knew that I was speaking stiffly, artificially, even bookishly; in a word, I did not know how to speak except “just like a book”. But that did not trouble me; I knew, I had a feeling, that I would be understood, and that this very bookishness might help matters still more. But now, having produced the effect, I suddenly lost my nerve. No, never, never had I witnessed such despair! She lay face down, her face pressed hard into the pillow, which she clasped with both arms. Her breast was being torn apart. Her whole young body shuddered as if in convulsions. The sobs pent up in her breast choked her, tore her, and suddenly burst out in wails and cries. Then she pressed herself still harder into the pillow: she did not want anyone here, not a single living soul, to know of her torment and her tears. She bit the pillow; she bit her own hand till it bled (I saw it afterwards); or, clutching her loosened plaits with her fingers, she would go rigid with the effort, holding her breath and clenching her teeth. I began to say something to her, to beg her to calm down, but felt that I did not dare; and suddenly I myself, all in a kind of shivering fit, almost in horror, began groping about to get ready to leave, anyhow, in a hurry. It was dark: however hard I tried, I could not finish quickly. Suddenly my hand found a box of matches and a candlestick with a whole new candle. As soon as the light lit up the room, Liza suddenly sprang up, sat, and with a kind of twisted face and a half-mad smile looked at me almost senselessly. I sat down beside her and took her hands; she came to herself, rushed towards me, was about to throw her arms round me, but did not dare, and quietly bowed her head before me.

“Liza, my dear, I was wrong... forgive me,” I began, but she squeezed my hands in her fingers with such force that I realised I was saying the wrong thing, and stopped.

“Here is my address, Liza; come and see me.”

“I will...” she whispered resolutely, still not raising her head.

“And now I’ll go; goodbye... until we meet.”

I got up; she got up too, and suddenly blushed all over, shuddered, seized a shawl that lay on a chair and threw it over her shoulders right up to her chin. Having done this, she smiled again somehow painfully, blushed, and looked at me strangely. It hurt me; I hurried to leave, to vanish.

“Wait,” she said suddenly, in the passage, right by the door, stopping me with her hand on my overcoat; she hastily put down the candle and ran off; she must have remembered something, or wanted to bring something to show me. As she ran off she blushed all over; her eyes shone; a smile appeared on her lips: what could it be? I could not help waiting; she came back a minute later with a look that seemed to ask forgiveness for something. It was no longer the same face, the same look as before: sullen, mistrustful and obstinate. Her look now was pleading, soft, and at the same time trusting, tender, timid. So children look at those they love very much and of whom they are asking something. Her eyes were light hazel, beautiful eyes, lively, able to reflect both love and sullen hatred.

Without explaining anything to me, as if I, like some higher being, must know everything without explanation, she held out a piece of paper to me. Her whole face lit up at that moment with the most naive, almost childlike triumph. I unfolded it. It was a letter to her from some medical student or someone of that kind: a very high-flown, flowery, but extremely respectful declaration of love. I don’t remember the expressions now, but I remember very well that through the lofty style there showed a true feeling, which cannot be faked. When I had finished reading, I met her warm, curious and childishly impatient look fixed on me. She had riveted her eyes on my face and was impatiently waiting: what would I say? In a few words, hurriedly, but somehow joyfully and as if proudly, she explained to me that she had been at a dance somewhere, in a family house, with some “very, very good people, family people, where they know nothing yet, nothing at all”, because she was still new here and it was only for now... and she had not at all decided to stay, and would certainly leave as soon as she had paid off her debt... “Well, and that student was there; he danced with her all evening and talked to her, and it turned out that he had known her in Riga, when they were children; they had played together, only a very long time ago; and he knew her parents; but about *this* he knew nothing, nothing, nothing, and suspected nothing! And the day after the dance (three days ago) he sent her this letter through the friend she had gone to the party with... and... well, that’s all.”

She lowered her shining eyes somehow shyly when she had finished.

Poor thing, she kept that student’s letter as a treasure and had run to fetch this one treasure of hers, not wanting me to leave without knowing that she too was loved honestly and sincerely, that she too was spoken to with respect. That letter was surely destined to lie in a box with no consequences. But no matter; I am sure she would have kept it all her life as a treasure, as her pride and her justification; and now, at such a moment, she had remembered it herself and brought it, to be naively proud before me, to restore herself in my eyes, so that I too would see it, so that I too would praise it. I said nothing, pressed her hand and went out. I so wanted to leave... I walked the whole way home, although the wet snow was still falling in flakes. I was exhausted, crushed, bewildered. But the truth was already gleaming through the bewilderment. A vile truth!
