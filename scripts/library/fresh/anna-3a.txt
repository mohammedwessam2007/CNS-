## Part One · Chapter XII

Princess Kitty Shcherbatskaya was eighteen. This was her first winter out in society. Her success in society was greater than that of both her elder sisters, and greater even than the princess had expected. Not only were almost all the young men who danced at the Moscow balls in love with Kitty, but already in this first winter two serious matches had presented themselves: Levin, and, immediately after his departure, Count Vronsky.

Levin’s appearance at the beginning of the winter, his frequent visits and his evident love for Kitty had given rise to the first serious conversations between Kitty’s parents about her future, and to disputes between the prince and the princess. The prince was on Levin’s side; he said he wished for nothing better for Kitty. The princess, with the habit women have of going round a question, said that Kitty was too young, that Levin gave no sign of having serious intentions, that Kitty felt no attachment to him, and other arguments; but she did not say the main thing, which was that she was waiting for a better match for her daughter, that Levin was not to her liking, and that she did not understand him. When Levin suddenly went away, the princess was glad, and said triumphantly to her husband: “You see, I was right.” And when Vronsky appeared she was gladder still, confirmed in her opinion that Kitty must make not merely a good match but a brilliant one.

For the mother there could be no comparison between Vronsky and Levin. The mother disliked Levin’s strange and abrupt opinions, and his awkwardness in society, founded, as she supposed, on pride, and his life in the country, which by her notions was somehow savage, occupied with cattle and peasants; and she disliked very much that he, in love with her daughter, had been coming to the house for six weeks as though waiting for something, watching, as though afraid that it would be too great an honour if he proposed, and had not understood that a man who comes to a house where there is a marriageable girl must declare himself. And suddenly, without declaring himself, he had gone away. “It’s a good thing he is so unattractive that Kitty didn’t fall in love with him,” thought the mother.

Vronsky satisfied all the mother’s wishes. Very rich, clever, of good family, on the way to a brilliant career in the army and at court, and a fascinating man. Nothing better could be wished for.

At balls Vronsky openly courted Kitty, danced with her and came to the house; so there could be no doubt of the seriousness of his intentions. And yet, in spite of this, the mother spent the whole winter in terrible anxiety and agitation.

The princess herself had married thirty years before, through an aunt’s matchmaking. The bridegroom, about whom everything was known beforehand, came, saw the bride and was seen; the matchmaking aunt found out and passed on the impression made on both sides; the impression was good; then, on an appointed day, the expected proposal was made to the parents and accepted. It all happened very easily and simply. At least so it seemed to the princess. But with her own daughters she had found out how far from easy and simple it is, this business that seems so ordinary, the marrying off of daughters. How many fears she had lived through, how many thoughts she had thought over, how much money had been spent, how many clashes with her husband there had been over the marrying of the two elder ones, Darya and Natalya! Now, with the youngest coming out, the same fears were being lived through, the same doubts, and still greater quarrels with her husband than over the elder ones. The old prince, like all fathers, was particularly scrupulous about the honour and purity of his daughters; he was unreasonably jealous over his daughters, and especially over Kitty, who was his favourite, and at every step he made scenes with the princess for compromising their daughter. The princess had grown used to this with the first daughters, but now she felt that the prince’s scrupulousness had more grounds. She saw that a great deal had changed lately in the ways of society, that a mother’s duties had become still harder. She saw that girls of Kitty’s age formed some sort of societies, went to some sort of courses, mixed freely with men, drove about the streets alone; many of them did not curtsey, and, above all, they were all firmly convinced that choosing a husband was their own business and not their parents’. “Girls are not married off nowadays the way they used to be,” all these young girls thought and said, and so did all the old people too. But how girls were married off nowadays the princess could not find out from anyone. The French custom, that parents decide their children’s fate, was not accepted; it was condemned. The English custom, the complete freedom of the girl, was not accepted either, and was impossible in Russian society. The Russian custom of matchmaking was considered something monstrous; everyone laughed at it, the princess herself included. But how girls ought to marry and be married, nobody knew. Everyone with whom the princess happened to discuss it said one thing to her: “Mercy, in our day it is time to give up these old ways. After all, it is the young people who are getting married, not the parents; so the young people must be left to arrange things as they see fit.” But it was all very well for people who had no daughters to say that; the princess understood that, as they came together, her daughter might fall in love, and fall in love with someone who did not want to marry, or with someone who was not fit to be a husband. And however much it was impressed on the princess that in our day young people must arrange their fate for themselves, she could not believe it, any more than she could have believed that, at any time whatever, the best toys for five-year-old children must be loaded pistols. And so the princess was more anxious over Kitty than she had been over the elder daughters.

Now she was afraid that Vronsky would confine himself to merely courting her daughter. She saw that her daughter was already in love with him, but she comforted herself with the thought that he was an honourable man and so would not do that. And yet she knew how easy it is, with today’s freedom of manners, to turn a girl’s head, and how lightly men in general look on that offence. The week before, Kitty had told her mother of a conversation she had had with Vronsky during the mazurka. This conversation had partly reassured the princess, but she could not be entirely at ease. Vronsky had told Kitty that he and his brother were both so used to submitting to their mother in everything that they would never decide on anything important without consulting her. “And now I am waiting, as for a special happiness, for my mother to arrive from Petersburg,” he had said.

Kitty told this without attaching any significance to the words. But her mother understood it differently. She knew that the old lady was expected any day, she knew that the old lady would be pleased with her son’s choice, and she found it strange that he was not proposing for fear of offending his mother; however, she so wanted the marriage itself, and above all relief from her anxieties, that she believed it. Bitter as it now was for the princess to see the unhappiness of her eldest daughter, Dolly, who was preparing to leave her husband, her agitation over the deciding of her youngest daughter’s fate absorbed all her feelings. Today, with the appearance of Levin, a new anxiety had been added. She was afraid that her daughter, who had at one time, as it seemed to her, felt something for Levin, might out of excessive honesty refuse Vronsky, and in general that Levin’s arrival might complicate and delay a matter so near its conclusion.

“Has he been here long?” said the princess of Levin, when they got home.

“Since today, maman.”

“There is one thing I want to say…” the princess began, and from her seriously animated face Kitty guessed what it would be about.

“Mama,” she said, flushing and turning quickly towards her, “please, please don’t say anything about that. I know, I know everything.”

She wished for the same thing as her mother, but her mother’s motives for wishing it offended her.

“I only want to say that, having given hope to one…”

“Mama, darling, for God’s sake don’t speak. It is so dreadful to talk about it.”

“I won’t, I won’t,” said the mother, seeing tears in her daughter’s eyes; “but one thing, my love: you promised me that you would have no secrets from me. You won’t?”

“Never, mama, none at all,” answered Kitty, blushing and looking straight into her mother’s face. “But I have nothing to tell now. I… I… if I wanted to, I don’t know what to say or how… I don’t know…”

“No, she can’t tell an untruth with those eyes,” thought the mother, smiling at her agitation and her happiness. The princess was smiling at how enormous and significant what was now happening in her soul seemed to her, poor little thing.

## Chapter XIII

After dinner, until the beginning of the evening, Kitty felt something like what a young man feels before a battle. Her heart beat violently, and her thoughts could not settle on anything.

She felt that this evening, when the two of them would meet for the first time, must be decisive in her fate. And she kept picturing them to herself, now each separately, now both together. When she thought of the past, she dwelt with pleasure, with tenderness, on her memories of her relations with Levin. Memories of childhood and of Levin’s friendship with her dead brother gave a special poetic charm to her relations with him. His love for her, of which she was sure, was flattering and joyful to her. And it was easy for her to remember Levin. With her memories of Vronsky, on the contrary, something awkward was mixed, though he was in the highest degree a calm man of the world; as though there were some falseness, not in him, he was very simple and nice, but in herself; whereas with Levin she felt herself perfectly simple and clear. But then, as soon as she thought of the future with Vronsky, a prospect of brilliant happiness rose before her; with Levin the future seemed misty.

Going upstairs to dress for the evening and looking in the mirror, she noticed with joy that this was one of her good days and that she was in full possession of all her powers, which she so needed for what lay ahead: she felt in herself an outward calm and a free grace of movement.

At half past seven, just as she came down into the drawing-room, the footman announced: “Konstantin Dmitrich Levin.” The princess was still in her room, and the prince had not come out. “So it is,” thought Kitty, and all the blood rushed to her heart. Glancing in the mirror, she was horrified at how pale she was.

Now she knew for certain that he had come early precisely to find her alone and to propose. And only now, for the first time, did the whole matter appear to her from quite another side, a new one. Only now did she understand that the question concerned not her alone, whom she would be happy with and whom she loved, but that this very minute she must wound a man she loved. And wound him cruelly… For what? Because he, the dear man, loved her, was in love with her. But there was nothing to be done; it had to be so, it must be so.

“My God, must I really tell him myself?” she thought. “What shall I say to him? Am I really to tell him that I don’t love him? That would be untrue. What shall I say to him? Say that I love another? No, that is impossible. I’ll go away, I’ll go away.”

She was already going towards the door when she heard his steps. “No! It’s not honest. What have I to be afraid of? I have done nothing wrong. What will be, will be! I shall tell the truth. And with him one can’t feel awkward. Here he is,” she said to herself, seeing his whole strong and timid figure, with his shining eyes fixed on her. She looked him straight in the face, as though begging him for mercy, and gave him her hand.

“I have come at the wrong time, I think; too early,” he said, glancing round the empty drawing-room. When he saw that his expectations had come true, that nothing prevented him from speaking out, his face became gloomy.

“Oh, no,” said Kitty, and sat down at the table.

“But that was just what I wanted, to find you alone,” he began, not sitting down and not looking at her, so as not to lose his courage.

“Mama will be out in a moment. She was very tired yesterday. Yesterday…”

She was talking without knowing herself what her lips were saying, and without taking her pleading, caressing eyes off him.

He looked at her; she blushed and fell silent.

“I told you that I didn’t know whether I had come for long… that it depended on you…”

She bent her head lower and lower, not knowing herself what she would answer to what was coming.

“That it depended on you,” he repeated. “I wanted to say… I wanted to say… That is what I came for… that… to be my wife!” he brought out, not knowing himself what he was saying; but, feeling that the most terrible thing had been said, he stopped and looked at her.

She was breathing heavily, not looking at him. She felt rapture. Her soul was overflowing with happiness. She had never expected that his declared love would make such a strong impression on her. But this lasted only an instant. She remembered Vronsky. She raised her clear, truthful eyes to Levin, and, seeing his desperate face, answered hurriedly:

“That cannot be… forgive me…”

How close to him she had been a minute before, how important to his life! And how alien and far from him she had become now!

“It could not have been otherwise,” he said, not looking at her.

He bowed and was about to leave.

## Chapter XIV

But at that very moment the princess came out. Horror showed on her face when she saw them alone and their upset faces. Levin bowed to her and said nothing. Kitty was silent and did not raise her eyes. “Thank God, she has refused him,” thought the mother, and her face lit up with the usual smile with which she greeted her guests on Thursdays. She sat down and began asking Levin about his life in the country. He sat down again, waiting for the guests to arrive so as to leave unnoticed.

Five minutes later a friend of Kitty’s came in, Countess Nordston, who had married the winter before.

She was a dry, sallow, sickly, nervous woman with shining black eyes. She loved Kitty, and her love for her, as the love of married women for girls always does, showed itself in a wish to marry Kitty according to her own ideal of happiness; and so she wished to marry her to Vronsky. Levin, whom she had often met at their house at the beginning of the winter, had always been disagreeable to her. Her constant and favourite occupation when she met him was to make fun of him.

“I love it when he looks down at me from the height of his greatness: either he breaks off his clever conversation with me because I am stupid, or he condescends. I like that very much: he condescends to me! I am very glad that he can’t stand me,” she would say of him.

She was right, because Levin really could not stand her and despised her for the very things she was proud of and counted to her credit: her nervousness, her refined contempt and indifference for everything coarse and everyday.

Between Countess Nordston and Levin there had been established that relationship, not uncommon in society, in which two people, outwardly remaining on friendly terms, despise each other so much that they cannot even treat each other seriously, and cannot even be offended by each other.

Countess Nordston at once pounced on Levin.

“Ah! Konstantin Dmitrich! You have come back to our depraved Babylon,” she said, giving him her tiny yellow hand and remembering his words, said some time at the beginning of the winter, that Moscow was Babylon. “Well, has Babylon reformed, or have you been corrupted?” she added, glancing round at Kitty with a smirk.

“I am very flattered, Countess, that you remember my words so well,” answered Levin, who had managed to recover himself and, from habit, at once fell into his jokingly hostile manner with Countess Nordston. “They must have a very strong effect on you.”

“Oh, of course! I write everything down. Well, Kitty, have you been skating again?…”

And she began talking to Kitty. However awkward it was for Levin to leave now, it was still easier for him to commit this awkwardness than to stay the whole evening and see Kitty, who glanced at him now and then and avoided his eyes. He was about to get up, but the princess, noticing that he was silent, turned to him.

“Have you come to Moscow for long? You are busy with the zemstvo, I believe, and can’t stay long.”

“No, Princess, I am no longer busy with the zemstvo,” he said. “I have come for a few days.”

“There is something special about him today,” thought Countess Nordston, peering into his stern, serious face; “somehow he isn’t getting drawn into his arguments. But I’ll bring him out. I do love making a fool of him in front of Kitty, and I shall.”

“Konstantin Dmitrich,” she said to him, “do explain to me, please, what it means (you know all about these things): in our village in Kaluga all the peasants and all the women have drunk away everything they had, and now they pay us nothing. What does that mean? You always praise the peasants so.”

At that moment another lady came into the room, and Levin got up.

“Excuse me, Countess, but I really know nothing about it and can tell you nothing,” he said, and looked round at the officer who was coming in behind the lady.

“That must be Vronsky,” thought Levin, and, to make sure, he glanced at Kitty. She had already had time to glance at Vronsky, and she looked round at Levin. And by that one glance of her eyes, which had lit up in spite of herself, Levin understood that she loved this man; understood it as surely as if she had told him so in words. But what sort of man was he?

Now, whether for good or ill, Levin could not help staying; he had to find out what sort of man it was whom she loved.

There are people who, on meeting a rival who has been lucky in anything whatever, are ready at once to turn away from everything good in him and to see only what is bad; and there are people who, on the contrary, wish above all to find in this lucky rival the qualities by which he has beaten them, and who look in him, with an aching pain in the heart, only for what is good. Levin was one of these. But it was not hard for him to find what was good and attractive in Vronsky. It struck him at once. Vronsky was a dark man, not tall, solidly built, with a good-natured, handsome, exceedingly calm and firm face. In his face and figure, from his close-cropped black hair and freshly shaved chin to his loose-fitting, brand-new uniform, everything was simple and at the same time elegant. Making way for the lady who was coming in, Vronsky went up to the princess and then to Kitty.

As he came up to her, his handsome eyes shone with particular tenderness, and with a barely noticeable smile, happy and modestly triumphant (so it seemed to Levin), bending over her respectfully and carefully, he held out his small but broad hand to her.

Having greeted everyone and said a few words, he sat down without once looking at Levin, who did not take his eyes off him.

“Allow me to introduce you,” said the princess, indicating Levin. “Konstantin Dmitrich Levin. Count Alexei Kirillovich Vronsky.”

Vronsky got up and, looking Levin in the eyes in a friendly way, shook his hand.

“I was to have dined with you this winter, I believe,” he said, smiling his simple and open smile, “but you went off to the country unexpectedly.”

“Konstantin Dmitrich despises and hates the town and us townspeople,” said Countess Nordston.

“My words must have a strong effect on you, since you remember them so well,” said Levin; and, remembering that he had already said that, he blushed.

Vronsky looked at Levin and at Countess Nordston and smiled.

“And are you always in the country?” he asked. “I should think it is dull in winter?”

“It isn’t dull if one has things to do, and one isn’t dull with oneself,” Levin answered sharply.

“I love the country,” said Vronsky, noticing Levin’s tone and pretending not to notice it.

“But I hope, Count, that you would not agree to live in the country always,” said Countess Nordston.

“I don’t know; I have never tried it for long. I have had a strange feeling,” he went on. “Nowhere have I missed the country so much, the Russian country, with bast shoes and peasants, as when I spent a winter with my mother in Nice. Nice is dull in itself, you know. And Naples and Sorrento are good only for a short time. And it is precisely there that Russia comes back so vividly, and precisely the country. They are just like…”

He was talking to both Kitty and Levin, moving his calm and friendly gaze from one to the other, and saying, evidently, whatever came into his head.

Noticing that Countess Nordston wanted to say something, he stopped without finishing what he had begun, and listened to her attentively.

The conversation did not flag for a minute, so that the old princess, who always kept two heavy guns in reserve in case there was nothing to talk about, classical against modern education, and universal military service, did not have to bring them out, and Countess Nordston did not get a chance to tease Levin.

Levin wanted to join in the general conversation and could not; saying to himself every minute, “Now I’ll go,” he did not go, but waited for something.

The conversation turned to table-turning and spirits, and Countess Nordston, who believed in spiritualism, began to tell of the wonders she had seen.

“Ah, Countess, you must take me, for God’s sake take me to them! I have never seen anything out of the ordinary, though I look for it everywhere,” said Vronsky, smiling.

“Very well, next Saturday,” answered Countess Nordston. “But you, Konstantin Dmitrich, do you believe in it?” she asked Levin.

“Why do you ask me? You know what I shall say.”

“But I want to hear your opinion.”

“My opinion is only this,” answered Levin: “that these turning tables prove that so-called educated society is no higher than the peasants. They believe in the evil eye, and in spells, and in love potions, and we…”

“So you don’t believe in it?”

“I can’t believe in it, Countess.”

“But if I have seen it myself?”

“Peasant women, too, tell how they have seen house-spirits with their own eyes.”

“So you think I am telling an untruth?”

And she laughed joylessly.

“Oh no, Masha, Konstantin Dmitrich says that he cannot believe it,” said Kitty, blushing for Levin; and Levin understood this and, still more irritated, wanted to answer; but Vronsky, with his open, cheerful smile, at once came to the rescue of a conversation that threatened to become unpleasant.

“You don’t admit the possibility at all?” he asked. “Why, we admit the existence of electricity, which we don’t understand; why can’t there be a new force, still unknown to us, which…”

“When electricity was found,” Levin interrupted quickly, “only the phenomenon had been discovered, and it was not known where it came from or what it produced, and centuries passed before anyone thought of applying it. The spiritualists, on the contrary, began with little tables writing for them and spirits coming to them, and only afterwards began to say that it was an unknown force.”

Vronsky listened attentively to Levin, as he always listened, evidently interested in his words.

“Yes, but the spiritualists say: we don’t know now what this force is, but the force exists, and here are the conditions under which it acts. Let the scientists work out what the force consists of. No, I don’t see why it can’t be a new force, if it…”

“Because,” Levin interrupted again, “with electricity, every time you rub resin on wool, a known phenomenon appears; and here it doesn’t every time; so it is not a natural phenomenon.”

Probably feeling that the conversation was taking a turn too serious for a drawing-room, Vronsky did not object but, trying to change the subject, smiled cheerfully and turned to the ladies.

“Let’s try it now, Countess,” he began; but Levin wanted to finish saying what he thought.

“I think,” he went on, “that this attempt of the spiritualists to explain their wonders by some new force is the least successful of all. They speak outright of a spiritual force and want to subject it to material experiment.”

Everyone was waiting for him to finish, and he felt it.

“And I think you would make an excellent medium,” said Countess Nordston; “there is something rapturous in you.”

Levin opened his mouth, wanted to say something, blushed, and said nothing.

“Let us try the tables now, Princess, please,” said Vronsky. “Princess, you will allow it?”

And Vronsky got up, looking about for a little table.

Kitty got up to go to the table and, as she passed, met Levin’s eyes. She pitied him with all her soul, all the more because she pitied him for an unhappiness of which she herself was the cause. “If I can be forgiven, forgive me,” her look said; “I am so happy.”

“I hate everyone, and you, and myself,” his look answered, and he took up his hat. But it was not his fate to leave. Just as they were about to settle round the table, and Levin to go, the old prince came in and, after greeting the ladies, turned to Levin.

“Ah!” he began joyfully. “Have you been here long? I didn’t even know you were here. Very glad to see you.”

The old prince sometimes said “thou” to Levin and sometimes “you”. He embraced Levin and, talking to him, did not notice Vronsky, who had stood up and was calmly waiting for the prince to turn to him.

Kitty felt how heavy her father’s kindness was for Levin after what had happened. She saw too how coldly her father at last answered Vronsky’s bow, and how Vronsky looked at her father with friendly bewilderment, trying to understand, and not understanding, how and why anyone could be unfriendly towards him; and she blushed.

“Prince, let us have Konstantin Dmitrich,” said Countess Nordston. “We want to make an experiment.”

“What experiment? Turning tables? Well, excuse me, ladies and gentlemen, but in my opinion it’s more fun to play hunt-the-ring,” said the old prince, looking at Vronsky and guessing that he had started it. “There is some sense in hunt-the-ring.”

Vronsky looked at the prince in surprise with his steady eyes and, smiling slightly, at once began talking to Countess Nordston about the big ball to be held the following week.

“I hope you will be there?” he said, turning to Kitty.

As soon as the old prince turned away from him, Levin went out unnoticed, and the last impression he carried away from that evening was Kitty’s smiling, happy face as she answered Vronsky’s question about the ball.
