## Chapter V · Not You, Not You!

On the way to Ivan he had to pass the house where Katerina Ivanovna lodged. There were lights in the windows. He suddenly stopped and decided to go in. He had not seen Katerina Ivanovna for more than a week. But it occurred to him now that Ivan might be with her at that moment, especially on the eve of such a day. Having rung and gone in onto the stairs, dimly lit by a Chinese lantern, he saw a man coming down from above, in whom, as they came level, he recognised his brother. So he had been at Katerina Ivanovna’s and was leaving.

“Ah, it’s only you,” Ivan Fyodorovich said drily. “Well, goodbye. Are you going to her?”

“Yes.”

“I don’t advise it; she is ‘agitated’, and you will upset her still more.”

“No, no!” a voice suddenly cried from above, from a door that had instantly opened. “Alexei Fyodorovich, have you come from him?”

“Yes, I have been with him.”

“Did he send me any message? Come in, Alyosha, and you, Ivan Fyodorovich, you must come back, you must. Do you hear?”

There was such a commanding note in Katya’s voice that Ivan Fyodorovich, after hesitating for a moment, decided all the same to go up again with Alyosha.

“She was eavesdropping!” he whispered irritably to himself, but Alyosha heard it.

“Allow me to keep my overcoat on,” said Ivan Fyodorovich, going into the drawing room. “I won’t even sit down. I shan’t stay more than a minute.”

“Sit down, Alexei Fyodorovich,” said Katerina Ivanovna, remaining standing herself. She had changed little in this time, but her dark eyes flashed with an ominous fire. Alyosha remembered afterwards that she had seemed to him extraordinarily beautiful at that moment.

“Well, what message did he send?”

“Only one thing,” said Alyosha, looking her straight in the face: “that you should spare yourself and not give evidence at the trial about…” he hesitated a little, “what happened between you… at the very beginning of your acquaintance… in that town…”

“Ah, about the bow to the ground for that money!” she caught him up with a bitter laugh. “Well, is he afraid for himself or for me, eh? He said I should spare… whom? Him or myself? Tell me, Alexei Fyodorovich.”

Alyosha looked at her intently, trying to understand her.

“Both yourself and him,” he said quietly.

“Just so,” she rapped out somehow spitefully, and suddenly flushed. “You don’t know me yet, Alexei Fyodorovich,” she said menacingly, “and I don’t know myself yet either. Perhaps you will want to trample me underfoot after tomorrow’s examination.”

“You will give your evidence honestly,” said Alyosha; “that is all that is needed.”

“A woman is often dishonest,” she ground out. “Only an hour ago I was thinking that I was afraid to touch that monster… as if he were a reptile… and yet no, he is still a human being to me! But did he kill him? Was it he who killed him?” she suddenly exclaimed hysterically, turning quickly to Ivan Fyodorovich. Alyosha understood in an instant that she had already asked Ivan Fyodorovich this very question, perhaps only a minute before he came in, and not for the first time but for the hundredth, and that they had ended in a quarrel.

“I have been to see Smerdyakov… It was you, you who convinced me that he killed his father. It was you alone I believed!” she went on, still addressing Ivan Fyodorovich. He smiled as if with an effort. Alyosha started on hearing that “you”, spoken so familiarly. He could never have suspected such relations between them.

“Well, that’s enough, though,” Ivan cut her short. “I’m going. I’ll come tomorrow.” And turning at once, he went out of the room and straight onto the stairs. Katerina Ivanovna suddenly, with a sort of commanding gesture, seized Alyosha by both hands.

“Go after him! Catch him up! Don’t leave him alone for a minute,” she whispered quickly. “He is out of his mind. Don’t you know he has gone out of his mind? He has a fever, a nervous fever! The doctor told me; go, run after him…”

Alyosha jumped up and rushed after Ivan Fyodorovich. Ivan had not gone fifty paces.

“What do you want?” he suddenly turned to Alyosha, seeing him catching up. “She told you to run after me because I’m mad. I know it by heart,” he added irritably.

“She is mistaken, of course, but she is right that you are ill,” said Alyosha. “I was looking at your face at her house just now; you have a very ill face, very, Ivan!”

Ivan walked on without stopping. Alyosha followed.

“And do you know, Alexei Fyodorovich, how people go mad?” Ivan asked in a suddenly quite quiet voice, no longer irritable at all, in which the most simple-hearted curiosity could suddenly be heard.

“No, I don’t; I suppose there are many different kinds of madness.”

“And can one observe oneself going mad?”

“I think one can’t watch oneself clearly in such a case,” Alyosha answered in surprise. Ivan was silent for half a minute.

“If you want to talk to me about anything, change the subject, please,” he said suddenly.

“Here, before I forget, there is a letter for you,” Alyosha said timidly, and, taking Lise’s letter from his pocket, he held it out to him. They had just come up to a street lamp. Ivan recognised the hand at once.

“Ah, it’s from that little demon!” he laughed spitefully, and, without opening the envelope, he suddenly tore it into several pieces and threw them to the wind. The scraps flew away.

“She isn’t sixteen yet, I believe, and already she is offering herself!” he said contemptuously, striding along the street again.

“What do you mean, offering herself?” exclaimed Alyosha.

“As depraved women offer themselves, of course.”

“What are you saying, Ivan, what are you saying?” Alyosha defended her sorrowfully and hotly. “She is a child; you are insulting a child! She is ill, she is very ill herself; perhaps she too is going out of her mind… I couldn’t help giving you her letter… On the contrary, I wanted to hear something from you… to save her.”

“You have nothing to hear from me. If she is a child, I am not her nurse. Be quiet, Alexei. Don’t go on. I don’t even think about it.”

They were silent again for a minute.

“She will be praying to the Mother of God all night now to show her how to act at the trial tomorrow,” he began again suddenly, sharply and spitefully.

“You… you mean Katerina Ivanovna?”

“Yes. Is she to appear as Mitenka’s saviour or his destroyer? That is what she will be praying for: that light may shine into her soul. She doesn’t know herself yet, you see; she hasn’t had time to prepare. She takes me for a nurse too; she wants me to lull her to sleep!”

“Katerina Ivanovna loves you, brother,” Alyosha said with a sad feeling.

“Perhaps. Only I don’t care for her.”

“She is suffering. Then why do you say such things to her… sometimes… that give her hope?” Alyosha went on with timid reproach. “For I know you have given her hope; forgive me for saying so,” he added.

“I can’t act as I ought here, break it off and tell her straight out!” Ivan said irritably. “I must wait till they pass sentence on the murderer. If I break with her now, she will ruin that scoundrel at the trial tomorrow out of revenge on me, because she hates him and knows she hates him. It is all lies here, lie upon lie! But now, while I haven’t broken with her, she still hopes and won’t ruin that monster, knowing how much I want to get him out of trouble. Oh, when will that cursed verdict come!”

The words “murderer” and “monster” echoed painfully in Alyosha’s heart.

“But how could she ruin our brother?” he asked, thinking over Ivan’s words. “What could she give in evidence that could ruin Mitya outright?”

“You don’t know that yet. She has a document in her hands, in Mitenka’s own hand, that proves mathematically that he killed Fyodor Pavlovich.”

“That can’t be!” exclaimed Alyosha.

“Why can’t it? I have read it myself.”

“There can be no such document!” Alyosha repeated hotly. “There can’t be, because he isn’t the murderer. It wasn’t he who killed father, not he!”

Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly stopped.

“Who is the murderer, then, in your opinion?” he asked, coldly to all appearance, and there was even a sort of haughty note in the tone of the question.

“You know yourself who,” Alyosha said quietly and with deep feeling.

“Who? That fable about that crazy idiot of an epileptic? About Smerdyakov?”

Alyosha suddenly felt that he was trembling all over.

“You know yourself who,” broke from him helplessly. He was gasping.

“But who, who?” Ivan shouted now almost savagely. All his restraint had suddenly vanished.

“I know one thing only,” Alyosha said, still almost in a whisper. “It wasn’t you who killed father.”

“‘Not you’! What do you mean, not you?” Ivan was dumbfounded.

“It wasn’t you who killed father, not you!” Alyosha repeated firmly.

The silence lasted for half a minute.

“Why, I know myself it wasn’t I; are you raving?” said Ivan with a pale, twisted smile. He seemed to fasten his eyes on Alyosha. They were both standing by the lamp again.

“No, Ivan, you have told yourself several times that you are the murderer.”

“When did I say so?… I was in Moscow… When did I say so?” Ivan murmured, quite at a loss.

“You have said it to yourself many times, when you were alone during these two terrible months,” Alyosha went on as before, quietly and distinctly. But he was speaking now as if beside himself, as if not of his own will, obeying some irresistible command. “You have accused yourself and confessed to yourself that the murderer was no one but you. But it wasn’t you who killed him; you are mistaken; you are not the murderer, do you hear me, not you! God has sent me to tell you this.”

Both were silent. The silence lasted a whole long minute. They both stood and kept looking into each other’s eyes. Both were pale. Suddenly Ivan began to tremble all over and gripped Alyosha tightly by the shoulder.

“You have been in my room!” he said in a grating whisper. “You were in my room at night when he came… Confess… you saw him, you saw him?”

“Whom do you mean… Mitya?” Alyosha asked in bewilderment.

“Not him, to the devil with the monster!” Ivan yelled frantically. “Do you know that he comes to me? How did you find out? Tell me!”

“Who is he? I don’t know whom you mean,” Alyosha murmured, frightened now.

“Yes, you do know… otherwise how could you… it can’t be that you don’t know…”

But suddenly he seemed to check himself. He stood as if thinking something over. A strange smile twisted his lips.

“Brother,” Alyosha began again in a trembling voice, “I said that to you because you will believe my word; I know that. I have said that word to you for your whole life: not you! Do you hear, for your whole life. And God has laid it on my soul to say it to you, even if you hate me for ever from this hour…”

But Ivan Fyodorovich had evidently quite regained his self-control.

“Alexei Fyodorovich,” he said with a cold smile, “I can’t stand prophets and epileptics, messengers of God especially; you know that only too well. From this moment I break with you, and, I think, for ever. I beg you to leave me this very minute, at this very crossroads. Besides, your way home is down this lane. And be especially careful not to come to me today! Do you hear?”

He turned and, striding firmly, walked straight on without looking back.

“Brother,” Alyosha called after him, “if anything happens to you today, think of me first of all!…”

But Ivan did not answer. Alyosha stood at the crossroads by the lamp until Ivan had disappeared completely into the darkness. Then he turned and walked slowly home down the lane. Both he and Ivan Fyodorovich lodged separately, in different places: neither of them wanted to live in Fyodor Pavlovich’s empty house. Alyosha rented a furnished room in the family of some townspeople; Ivan Fyodorovich lived rather far from him and occupied a spacious and fairly comfortable apartment in the lodge of a good house belonging to a well-to-do civil servant’s widow. But in the whole lodge he was waited on by only one ancient, completely deaf little old woman, full of rheumatism, who went to bed at six in the evening and got up at six in the morning. Ivan Fyodorovich had become strangely undemanding in these two months and liked very much to be quite alone. He even tidied the room he lived in himself, and rarely even went into the other rooms of his apartment. Reaching the gate of his house and already taking hold of the bell handle, he stopped. He felt that he was still trembling all over with a spiteful trembling. Suddenly he let go of the bell, spat, turned back and walked quickly off again to quite the other, opposite end of the town, about two versts from his lodgings, to a tiny, lopsided log house where Marya Kondratyevna lodged, Fyodor Pavlovich’s former neighbour, who used to come to Fyodor Pavlovich’s kitchen for soup and to whom Smerdyakov had sung his songs and played the guitar in those days. She had sold her former little house and now lived with her mother almost in a peasant hut; and the sick, almost dying Smerdyakov had settled with them ever since Fyodor Pavlovich’s death. It was to him that Ivan Fyodorovich now set off, drawn by a sudden and irresistible consideration.
