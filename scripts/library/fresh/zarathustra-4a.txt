## On the Famous Wise Men

You have served the people and the people’s superstition, all you famous wise men! And not the truth! And that is exactly why you were paid reverence.

And that too is why your unbelief was put up with, because it was a joke and a detour on the way to the people. So the master lets his slaves have their way, and even enjoys their high spirits.

But the one who is hated by the people as a wolf is by the dogs: that is the free spirit, the enemy of fetters, the one who does not worship, the one who lives in forests.

To hunt him out of his hiding place: that has always been what the people call “a sense of what is right”; against him it still sets its sharpest-toothed dogs.

“For the truth is here: the people are here, after all! Woe, woe to those who seek!” So it has always rung out.

You wanted to justify your people in its reverence: that you called “will to truth”, you famous wise men!

And your heart always said to itself: “I came from the people; from there the voice of God came to me too.”

Stiff-necked and clever, like the donkey, you have always been the people’s advocates.

And many a powerful man who wanted to get along well with the people harnessed in front of his horses a little donkey, a famous wise man.

And now, you famous wise men, I wish you would at last throw off the lion’s skin altogether!

The skin of the beast of prey, the spotted one, and the shaggy hair of the explorer, the seeker, the conqueror!

Ah, for me to learn to believe in your “truthfulness”, you would first have to break your will to revere.

Truthful: that is what I call the one who goes into godless deserts and has broken his revering heart.

In the yellow sand, burned by the sun, he may well squint thirstily at the islands rich in springs, where living things rest under dark trees.

But his thirst does not persuade him to become like these comfortable ones: for where there are oases, there are also idols.

Hungry, violent, lonely, godless: so the lion-will wants itself.

Free from the happiness of servants, released from gods and worship, fearless and fearsome, great and lonely: so is the will of the truthful one.

The truthful, the free spirits, have always lived in the desert, as lords of the desert; but in the cities live the well-fed, famous wise men, the draught animals.

For they always pull, like donkeys, the people’s cart!

Not that I am angry with them for that: but they remain servants to me, and harnessed, even when they shine in golden harness.

And often they were good servants, and worthy of praise. For so virtue speaks: “If you must be a servant, look for the one whom your service helps most!

The spirit and virtue of your master should grow through your being his servant: so you yourself grow with his spirit and his virtue!”

And truly, you famous wise men, you servants of the people! You yourselves grew with the people’s spirit and virtue, and the people through you! I say this to your credit!

But you remain the people to me, even in your virtues, the people with dull eyes, the people who do not know what spirit is!

Spirit is the life that itself cuts into life: by its own torment it increases its own knowledge. Did you know that already?

And the happiness of the spirit is this: to be anointed and consecrated by tears as the sacrificial animal. Did you know that already?

And the blindness of the blind man, and his seeking and groping, shall still bear witness to the power of the sun he looked into. Did you know that already?

And the one who seeks knowledge shall learn to build with mountains! It is little that the spirit moves mountains. Did you know that already?

You know only the sparks of the spirit: but you do not see the anvil that it is, nor the cruelty of its hammer!

Truly, you do not know the pride of the spirit! But still less could you bear the modesty of the spirit, if it ever wanted to speak!

And never yet have you been allowed to throw your spirit into a pit of snow: you are not hot enough for that! So you do not know the raptures of its coldness either.

But in everything you are too familiar with the spirit; and you have often made of wisdom a poorhouse and a hospital for bad poets.

You are not eagles: so you have not known the happiness in the terror of the spirit either. And whoever is not a bird should not camp above abysses.

You are lukewarm to me: but all deep knowledge flows cold. Ice-cold are the innermost wells of the spirit: a refreshment to hot hands and to those who act.

You stand there honourably, stiff, with straight backs, you famous wise men! No strong wind or will drives you.

Have you never seen a sail go over the sea, rounded and swelling and trembling before the violence of the wind?

Like the sail, trembling before the violence of the spirit, my wisdom goes over the sea, my wild wisdom!

But you servants of the people, you famous wise men, how could you go with me!

Thus spoke Zarathustra.

## The Night Song

It is night: now all the leaping fountains speak more loudly. And my soul too is a leaping fountain.

It is night: only now do all the songs of lovers awake. And my soul too is the song of a lover.

Something unstilled, unstillable is in me; it wants to be heard. A craving for love is in me, which itself speaks the language of love.

I am light: ah, that I were night! But this is my loneliness, that I am girded round with light.

Ah, that I were dark and like the night! How I would suck at the breasts of light!

And I would even bless you, you little twinkling stars and glow-worms up there! And be happy in your gifts of light.

But I live in my own light; I drink back into myself the flames that break out of me.

I do not know the happiness of the one who takes; and I have often dreamed that stealing must be even more blessed than taking.

This is my poverty, that my hand never rests from giving; this is my envy, that I see waiting eyes and the lit-up nights of longing.

Oh, the unhappiness of all who give! Oh, the darkening of my sun! Oh, the craving to crave! Oh, the ravenous hunger in being full!

They take from me: but do I still touch their soul? There is a gulf between giving and taking; and the smallest gulf is the last to be bridged.

A hunger grows out of my beauty: I would like to hurt those I shine for, I would like to rob those I have given to: so I hunger for malice.

Drawing back my hand when a hand is already reaching out to it; hesitating like the waterfall that hesitates even as it falls: so I hunger for malice.

Such revenge my fullness devises; such spite wells up out of my loneliness.

My happiness in giving died in giving; my virtue grew tired of itself in its overflowing!

Whoever always gives is in danger of losing his shame; whoever always hands out, his hand and heart grow calloused from so much handing out.

My eye no longer overflows at the shame of those who ask; my hand has become too hard for the trembling of filled hands.

Where have the tears gone from my eye, and the down from my heart? Oh, the loneliness of all who give! Oh, the silence of all who shine!

Many suns circle in empty space: to everything that is dark they speak with their light; to me they are silent.

Oh, this is the enmity of light towards what shines: without mercy it goes its ways.

Unfair to what shines in its deepest heart, cold to suns: so every sun goes its way.

Like a storm the suns fly along their paths: that is their going. They follow their unrelenting will: that is their coldness.

Oh, it is only you, you dark ones, you night-like ones, who make warmth out of what shines! Oh, only you drink milk and refreshment from the udders of light!

Ah, there is ice around me; my hand burns itself on what is icy! Ah, there is thirst in me, that pines for your thirst!

It is night: ah, that I must be light! And thirst for what is of the night! And loneliness!

It is night: now my longing breaks out of me like a spring; I long to speak.

It is night: now all the leaping fountains speak more loudly. And my soul too is a leaping fountain.

It is night: only now do all the songs of lovers awake. And my soul too is the song of a lover.

Thus sang Zarathustra.

## The Dance Song

One evening Zarathustra was walking through the forest with his disciples; and as he was looking for a spring, he came upon a green meadow, quietly surrounded by trees and bushes, where girls were dancing together. As soon as the girls recognised Zarathustra, they stopped dancing; but Zarathustra went up to them with a friendly gesture and spoke these words:

“Do not stop dancing, you lovely girls! No spoilsport has come to you with an evil eye, no enemy of girls.

I am God’s advocate before the devil; and the devil is the spirit of gravity. How could I, you light ones, be an enemy of divine dances? Or of girls’ feet with pretty ankles?

I am a forest, it is true, and a night of dark trees: but whoever is not afraid of my darkness will also find banks of roses under my cypresses.

And he will surely find the little god too, whom girls love best: he lies beside the spring, quiet, with his eyes closed.

Truly, he has fallen asleep in broad daylight, the idler! Has he been chasing butterflies too much?

Do not be angry with me, you beautiful dancers, if I punish the little god a little! He will scream, I expect, and cry; but he is laughable even when he cries!

And with tears in his eyes he shall ask you for a dance; and I myself will sing a song for his dance:

a dancing song and a mocking song on the spirit of gravity, my supreme and most powerful devil, who they say is ‘the lord of the world’.”

And this is the song Zarathustra sang, while Cupid and the girls danced together:

Into your eye I looked lately, O life! And I seemed to myself to sink into the unfathomable.

But you pulled me out with a golden fishing rod; you laughed mockingly when I called you unfathomable.

“That is what all fish say,” you said; “what they cannot fathom is unfathomable.

But I am only changeable, and wild, and in everything a woman, and not a virtuous one:

even if you men call me ‘the deep’, or ‘the faithful’, ‘the eternal’, ‘the mysterious’.

But you men always make us presents of your own virtues. Ah, you virtuous ones!”

So she laughed, the unbelievable one; but I never believe her and her laughter when she speaks badly of herself.

And when I spoke in private with my wild wisdom, she said to me angrily: “You will, you crave, you love; that is the only reason you praise life!”

I almost answered her wickedly then, and told the angry one the truth; and one cannot answer more wickedly than when one “tells the truth” to one’s wisdom.

For this is how things stand between the three of us. From the bottom of my heart I love only life, and truly, most of all when I hate it!

But that I am fond of wisdom, and often too fond: that is because she reminds me so very much of life!

She has her eye, her laugh, and even her little golden fishing rod: how can I help it that the two look so alike?

And when life once asked me, “Who is she, then, this wisdom?” I said eagerly: “Ah, yes! Wisdom!

One thirsts for her and is never satisfied; one looks through veils, one snatches through nets.

Is she beautiful? What do I know! But the oldest carp are still caught with her as bait.

She is changeable and stubborn; I have often seen her bite her lip and comb her hair against the grain.

Perhaps she is wicked and false, and in everything a woman; but when she speaks badly of herself, that is just when she is most seductive.”

When I said this to life, she laughed maliciously and closed her eyes. “Whom are you talking about?” she said. “About me, surely?

And even if you were right, is that the kind of thing one says to my face? But now speak about your wisdom too!”

Ah, and now you opened your eye again, O beloved life! And I seemed to myself to sink into the unfathomable again.

Thus sang Zarathustra. But when the dance was over and the girls had gone away, he became sad.

“The sun went down long ago,” he said at last; “the meadow is damp, a coolness comes from the forests.

Something unknown is around me, and looks thoughtful. What! You are still alive, Zarathustra?

Why? What for? By what? Where to? Where? How? Is it not folly to go on living?

Ah, my friends, it is the evening that asks such questions out of me. Forgive me my sadness!

Evening has come: forgive me that evening has come!”

Thus spoke Zarathustra.

## The Grave Song

“Over there is the island of graves, the silent one; there too are the graves of my youth. I will take an evergreen wreath of life there.”

So resolving in my heart, I crossed the sea.

O you visions and apparitions of my youth! O all you glances of love, you divine moments! How quickly you died on me! I remember you today as I remember my dead.

From you, my dearest dead, a sweet scent comes to me, a scent that loosens heart and tears. Truly, it shakes and loosens the heart of the lonely sailor.

I am still the richest and the most to be envied, I, the loneliest! For I had you, and you have me still: tell me, for whom else did such rosy apples fall from the tree?

I am still the heir and soil of your love, blooming in your memory with bright, wild-growing virtues, O you most beloved!

Ah, we were made to stay near each other, you sweet strange wonders; and you did not come to me and my longing like shy birds, no, but as the trusting to the trusting!

Yes, made for faithfulness, like me, and for tender eternities: must I now name you by your faithlessness, you divine glances and moments? I have not yet learned any other name.

Truly, you died too quickly on me, you fugitives. Yet you did not flee me, nor I you: we are innocent towards each other in our faithlessness.

To kill me, they strangled you, you songbirds of my hopes! Yes, it was always at you, my dearest, that malice shot its arrows, to hit my heart!

And it hit! For you were always what was dearest to my heart, my possession and my being possessed: that is why you had to die young, and all too soon!

They shot the arrow at the most vulnerable thing I had: that was you, whose skin is like down, and still more like the smile that dies at a glance!

But this word I will say to my enemies: what is all murder of men compared with what you did to me!

You did something worse to me than any murder of men; you took from me what cannot be brought back: so I speak to you, my enemies!

For you murdered the visions of my youth and my dearest wonders! You took my playmates from me, the blessed spirits! To their memory I lay down this wreath and this curse.

This curse against you, my enemies! For you made my eternal short, as a note breaks off in the cold night! It came to me only as a flash of divine eyes, as a moment!

So once, in a good hour, my purity spoke: “All beings shall be divine to me.”

Then you attacked me with dirty ghosts; ah, where has that good hour fled to now!

“All days shall be holy to me”: so the wisdom of my youth once spoke; truly, the speech of a joyful wisdom!

But then you enemies stole my nights from me and sold them to sleepless torment: ah, where has that joyful wisdom fled to now?

Once I longed for happy omens from birds: then you led an owl-monster across my path, a repulsive one. Ah, where did my tender longing flee to then?

Once I vowed to give up all disgust: then you turned those near and nearest to me into boils. Ah, where did my noblest vow flee to then?

As a blind man I once walked blessed paths: then you threw filth on the blind man’s path, and now he is disgusted with the old blind man’s footpath.

And when I did my hardest and celebrated the victory of my overcomings, then you made those who loved me cry out that I was hurting them most.

Truly, that has always been your doing: you embittered my best honey and the industry of my best bees.

To my charity you always sent the most shameless beggars; around my pity you always crowded the incurably shameless. So you wounded my virtue in its faith.

And when I laid my holiest down as a sacrifice, at once your “piety” set its fatter gifts beside it, so that my holiest was choked in the steam of your fat.

And once I wanted to dance as I had never danced before: I wanted to dance away over all the heavens. Then you talked my dearest singer round.

And now he struck up a gruesome, dull tune; ah, he hooted in my ears like a gloomy horn!

Murderous singer, instrument of malice, most innocent one! I was already standing ready for the best dance: then with your notes you murdered my rapture!

Only in dance do I know how to speak the parable of the highest things; and now my highest parable has stayed unspoken in my limbs!

Unspoken and unredeemed my highest hope has stayed! And all the visions and consolations of my youth have died on me!

How did I bear it? How did I get over and overcome such wounds? How did my soul rise again out of these graves?

Yes, there is something in me that cannot be wounded, that cannot be buried, something that bursts rocks: it is called my will. Silently it strides, unchanged, through the years.

It wants to go its way on my feet, my old will; its mind is hard of heart and cannot be wounded.

I am invulnerable only in my heel. You still live there and are the same, most patient one! You have always broken out through all graves!

In you lives on too what was unredeemed in my youth; and as life and youth you sit here, hoping, on yellow ruins of graves.

Yes, you are still for me the destroyer of all graves: hail to you, my will! And only where there are graves are there resurrections.

Thus sang Zarathustra.
