## IX

> And into my house, boldly and freely,
> come in, its mistress in full right!
— from the same poem

I stood before her crushed, disgraced, loathsomely embarrassed, and I think I was smiling, trying with all my might to wrap myself in the skirts of my shaggy, wadded old dressing gown: exactly, exactly as I had pictured it not long before, in my low spirits. Apollon stood over us for a couple of minutes and went away, but I felt no better. Worst of all, she too suddenly became embarrassed, more than I could ever have expected. Looking at me, of course.

“Sit down,” I said mechanically, and moved a chair up to the table for her, while I sat on the sofa. She sat down at once, obediently, looking at me with wide eyes and evidently expecting something from me at any moment. It was this naive expectation that drove me into a fury, but I controlled myself.

This was just the moment to try to notice nothing, as if everything were quite ordinary; but she... And I felt vaguely that she would pay dearly for all this.

“You have found me in a strange position, Liza,” I began, stammering, and knowing that this was exactly how I should not begin.

“No, no, don’t imagine anything!” I cried, seeing that she had suddenly blushed. “I am not ashamed of my poverty... On the contrary, I look on my poverty with pride. I am poor but noble... One can be poor and noble,” I muttered. “But... would you like some tea?”

“No...” she began.

“Wait!”

I jumped up and ran to Apollon. I had to vanish somewhere.

“Apollon,” I whispered in a feverish patter, throwing down in front of him the seven roubles I had been holding in my fist all along, “here are your wages; you see, I am paying you; but in return you must save me: bring tea and ten rusks from the tavern at once. If you won’t go, you will make a man unhappy! You don’t know what kind of woman this is... She is everything! You may be thinking something... But you don’t know what kind of woman this is!..”

Apollon, who had already sat down to his work and put on his spectacles again, at first, without letting go of his needle, glanced silently and sidelong at the money; then, paying no attention to me and not answering, he went on fiddling with the thread he was still threading. I waited for three minutes, standing in front of him with my arms folded *à la Napoléon*. My temples were wet with sweat; I was pale, I could feel it. But, thank God, he must have felt sorry for me, looking at me. Having finished with his thread, he slowly got up, slowly pushed back his chair, slowly took off his spectacles, slowly counted the money and at last, asking me over his shoulder whether he should get a full pot, slowly left the room. As I was going back to Liza, it occurred to me on the way: shouldn’t I just run away, as I was, in my old dressing gown, anywhere at all, and come what may?

I sat down again. She looked at me uneasily. For several minutes we were silent.

“I’ll kill him!” I cried suddenly, banging my fist on the table so hard that ink splashed out of the inkstand.

“Oh, what are you saying!” she cried, starting.

“I’ll kill him, I’ll kill him!” I shrieked, pounding the table, in a complete frenzy, and at the same time understanding completely how stupid it was to be in such a frenzy.

“You don’t know, Liza, what that executioner is to me. He is my executioner... He has gone for the rusks now; he...”

And suddenly I burst into tears. It was a fit. How ashamed I was between my sobs; but I could not hold them back. She was frightened.

“What’s the matter? What’s the matter with you?” she cried, fussing around me.

“Water, give me some water, over there!” I muttered in a weak voice, aware, however, that I could very well have done without the water and without muttering in a weak voice. But I was, as they say, putting it on, to save appearances, though the fit was real.

She gave me water, looking at me as if lost. At that moment Apollon brought in the tea. It suddenly seemed to me that this ordinary, prosaic tea was terribly indecent and paltry after everything that had happened, and I blushed. Liza looked at Apollon almost with fear. He went out without glancing at us.

“Liza, do you despise me?” I said, looking her straight in the face, trembling with impatience to know what she thought.

She was confused and did not know what to answer.

“Drink your tea!” I said spitefully. I was angry with myself, but of course it was she who had to suffer for it. A terrible spite against her suddenly boiled up in my heart; I think I could have killed her. To take revenge on her, I swore to myself not to say a single word to her the whole time. “She is the cause of it all,” I thought.

Our silence had already lasted about five minutes. The tea stood on the table; we did not touch it. I had got to the point of deliberately not wanting to begin drinking, so as to make things still harder for her; and it was awkward for her to begin. Several times she glanced at me in sad perplexity. I was stubbornly silent. The chief martyr was of course myself, because I was fully aware of all the loathsome baseness of my spiteful stupidity, and at the same time I could not restrain myself at all.

“I want to... get right away... from there,” she began, to break the silence somehow; but, poor thing! that was exactly what she should not have begun to talk about at such an already stupid moment, to such an already stupid man as me. My heart even ached with pity at her clumsiness and her needless directness. But something hideous at once crushed all the pity in me; it even egged me on still more: let the whole world go to ruin! Another five minutes passed.

“Am I in your way?” she began timidly, barely audibly, and began to get up.

But as soon as I saw this first flash of wounded dignity, I trembled with rage and at once burst out.

“Why have you come to me, will you tell me, please?” I began, gasping, and not even minding the logical order of my words. I wanted to say everything at once, in one volley; I did not even care where I began.

“Why have you come? Answer! Answer!” I cried, hardly knowing what I was doing. “I’ll tell you, my dear, why you have come. You have come because I spoke pitiful words to you that time. So you went soft, and now you want ‘pitiful words’ again. Well, know then, know that I was laughing at you that time. And I am laughing now. Why are you trembling? Yes, laughing! I had been insulted just before, at a dinner, by those men who came there before me. I came to your place to beat up one of them, the officer; but it didn’t come off, I didn’t find him; I had to take it out on someone, to get my own back; you turned up, and I vented my spite on you and laughed at you. I had been humiliated, so I wanted to humiliate; I had been ground into a rag, so I wanted to show my power... That’s what it was; and you thought I had come on purpose that time to save you, didn’t you? Is that what you thought? Is that what you thought?”

I knew that she might get confused and not understand the details; but I knew too that she would understand the essence perfectly well. And so it was. She turned white as a sheet, wanted to say something; her lips twisted painfully; but she fell on the chair as if she had been cut down with an axe. And all the time after that she listened to me with her mouth open, her eyes wide, trembling with terrible fear. The cynicism, the cynicism of my words crushed her...

“Save you!” I went on, jumping up from my chair and running back and forth in front of her about the room. “Save you from what! Why, I may be worse than you myself. Why didn’t you throw it in my face when I was preaching at you: ‘And you, what did you come here for? To read us a lesson in morals?’ Power, power was what I needed then; I needed a game; I needed to get your tears, your humiliation, your hysterics: that is what I needed then! I couldn’t bear it myself, then, because I am trash; I took fright and, the devil knows why, like a fool gave you my address. And afterwards, before I even got home, I was already cursing you for all I was worth for that address. I already hated you, because I had lied to you then. Because I only play with words, dream in my head; but in reality, do you know what I want? For you all to go to hell, that’s what! I need peace. Why, to be left undisturbed I would sell the whole world this minute for a kopeck. Is the world to go to hell, or am I to go without my tea? I say: let the world go to hell, so long as I always get my tea. Did you know that or not? Well, I know that I am a scoundrel, a wretch, an egoist, a lazybones. I have been trembling these three days for fear you would come. And do you know what worried me most these three days? That I had played such a hero before you that time, and now you would suddenly see me in this torn old dressing gown, a beggar, loathsome. I told you just now that I am not ashamed of my poverty; well, know that I am ashamed, more ashamed of it than of anything, more afraid of it than of anything, more than if I were stealing; because I am as vain as if I had been skinned, and the very air hurts me. Can it be that even now you haven’t guessed that I will never forgive you for finding me in this old dressing gown, when I was flying like a vicious little dog at Apollon? The resurrector, the former hero, flies at his servant like a mangy, shaggy mongrel, and the servant laughs at him! And the tears just now, which I could not hold back before you like a shamed old woman, I will never forgive you for! And what I am confessing to you now, I will never forgive you for either! Yes, you, you alone must answer for all this, because you turned up like that, because I am a scoundrel, because I am the nastiest, the most ridiculous, the pettiest, the stupidest, the most envious of all the worms on earth, who are not a bit better than me, but who, the devil knows why, are never embarrassed; while I will be flicked on the nose by every louse all my life: that is my trait! And what do I care if you understand nothing of this! And what, what, what do I care about you, and whether you go to ruin there or not? Do you understand how I will hate you now, having said all this to you, for having been here and listened? A man speaks out like this only once in his life, and then only in hysterics!.. What more do you want? Why, after all this, do you still stand there in front of me, tormenting me, not going away?”

But at this point a strange thing suddenly happened.

I was so used to thinking and imagining everything as in books, and picturing everything in the world as I had composed it beforehand in my dreams, that at first I did not even understand this strange thing. And what happened was this: Liza, insulted and crushed by me, understood far more than I imagined. She understood out of all this what a woman always understands first of all if she loves sincerely, namely, that I myself was unhappy.

The frightened and insulted look on her face gave way first to a sorrowful astonishment. And when I began calling myself a scoundrel and a wretch, and my tears began to flow (I spoke the whole of this tirade in tears), her whole face twitched as if in a spasm. She wanted to get up, to stop me; and when I had finished, she paid no attention to my shouts, “Why are you here, why don’t you go away!”, but to the fact that it must have been very hard for me to say all this. Besides, she was so crushed, poor thing; she thought herself infinitely beneath me; how could she be angry, or offended? She suddenly jumped up from her chair in an irresistible impulse and, straining towards me with her whole being, but still shy and not daring to move from where she stood, held out her arms to me... At that my heart too turned over. Then she suddenly rushed to me, threw her arms round my neck and began to weep. I could not hold out either, and sobbed as I had never sobbed before...

“They won’t let me... I can’t be... good!” I barely managed to say; then I went to the sofa, fell on it face down and for a quarter of an hour sobbed in real hysterics. She pressed herself to me, put her arms round me, and seemed to freeze in that embrace.

But the trouble was that the hysterics had to pass. And then (I am writing the loathsome truth) lying face down on the sofa, pressing my face hard into my wretched leather cushion, I began little by little, distantly, involuntarily but irresistibly, to feel that it would be awkward for me now to raise my head and look Liza straight in the eye. What was I ashamed of? I don’t know, but I was ashamed. It also came into my overwrought head that the roles were now completely reversed; that she was now the heroine, and I was just such a humiliated and crushed creature as she had been before me that night, four days ago... And all this came to me while I was still lying face down on the sofa!

My God! Can it be that I envied her then?

I don’t know; to this day I cannot decide; and then, of course, I could understand it even less than now. I cannot live, after all, without power and tyranny over someone... But... but nothing can be explained by reasoning, and consequently there is no point in reasoning.

I overcame myself, however, and raised my head: I had to raise it some time... And I am convinced to this day that precisely because I was ashamed to look at her, another feeling suddenly kindled and flared up in my heart then... a feeling of mastery and possession. My eyes gleamed with passion, and I gripped her hands hard. How I hated her, and how drawn to her I was at that moment! One feeling strengthened the other. It was almost like revenge!.. On her face there appeared at first a kind of bewilderment, even fear, but only for a moment. She embraced me rapturously and warmly.
