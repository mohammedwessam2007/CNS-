## Chapter IV · A Hymn and a Secret

It was already quite late (and how long is a November day?) when Alyosha rang at the prison gate. It was even beginning to get dark. But Alyosha knew he would be let in to Mitya without hindrance. All this is the same in our little town as everywhere. At first, of course, after the whole preliminary investigation was over, access to Mitya for visits from relatives and certain other persons was still surrounded with some necessary formalities; but afterwards the formalities, not that they were relaxed, but for certain persons at least who came to see Mitya, certain exceptions somehow established themselves of their own accord. So much so that sometimes even visits to the prisoner in the room set aside for them took place almost tête-à-tête. There were very few such persons, however: only Grushenka, Alyosha and Rakitin. But Grushenka was very much in the good graces of the police captain Mikhail Makarovich himself. The old man had on his conscience his shouting at her at Mokroye. Later, having learned the whole truth, he had completely changed his opinion of her. And, strange to say, though he was firmly convinced of Mitya’s crime, since his imprisonment he had somehow come to look on him more and more gently: “A man with a good soul perhaps, and he has come to grief like a Swede at Poltava, from drink and disorder!” The former horror had given way in his heart to a kind of pity. As for Alyosha, the police captain was very fond of him and had known him a long time; and Rakitin, who had later taken to visiting the prisoner very often, was one of the most intimate acquaintances of “the police captain’s young ladies”, as he called them, and was always hanging about their house. He also gave lessons in the house of the prison governor, a good-natured old man, though a sound old officer. And Alyosha again was a special and long-standing acquaintance of the governor too, who loved to talk with him about “wisdom” in general. Ivan Fyodorovich, for instance, the governor did not so much respect as fear, above all his opinions, though he was a great philosopher himself, “having worked it out with his own wits”, of course. But for Alyosha he had a kind of invincible liking. In the last year the old man had just taken up the apocryphal gospels and was constantly telling his young friend his impressions of them. Before, he had even visited him at the monastery and talked with him and the hieromonks for hours on end. In a word, even if Alyosha was late for the prison, he had only to go to the governor and it was always arranged. Besides, everyone in the prison, down to the last warder, had grown used to Alyosha. The guard, of course, made no difficulties, so long as the authorities gave permission. When he was called, Mitya always came down from his cell to the place appointed for visits. Coming into the room, Alyosha ran straight into Rakitin, who was just leaving Mitya. They were both talking loudly. Mitya, seeing him out, was laughing heartily at something, and Rakitin seemed to be grumbling. Rakitin, especially of late, did not like meeting Alyosha; he hardly spoke to him and even bowed to him with an effort. Seeing Alyosha come in now, he frowned particularly and looked away, as if entirely absorbed in buttoning his big warm overcoat with its fur collar. Then he at once began looking for his umbrella.

“Mustn’t forget anything of my own,” he muttered, simply to say something.

“Mind you don’t forget anything of other people’s!” Mitya joked, and at once roared with laughter at his own joke. Rakitin flared up instantly.

“Recommend that to your Karamazovs, your brood of serf-owners, not to Rakitin!” he suddenly shouted, shaking with rage.

“What’s the matter with you? I was joking!” Mitya cried out. “Ugh, the devil! They are all like that,” he turned to Alyosha, nodding at the quickly retreating Rakitin. “There he sat laughing and cheerful all the time, and suddenly he boils over! He didn’t even nod to you; have you quarrelled altogether, or what? Why are you so late? I wasn’t just waiting for you, I was thirsting for you all morning. Well, never mind! We’ll make up for it.”

“Why has he taken to coming to you so often? Have you made friends with him, or what?” asked Alyosha, nodding too at the door through which Rakitin had gone.

“Made friends with Mikhail? No, not exactly. He’s a pig, what else! He thinks I am… a scoundrel. And they don’t understand a joke either; that’s the main thing about them. They’ll never understand a joke. And their souls are dry, flat and dry, just as I was when I drove up to the prison and looked at the prison walls. But a clever man, clever. Well, Alexei, my head is lost now!”

He sat down on the bench and sat Alyosha down beside him.

“Yes, the trial is tomorrow. Can you really have no hope at all, brother?” Alyosha said with a timid feeling.

“What do you mean?” Mitya looked at him somehow vaguely. “Ah, you mean the trial! Oh, the devil! We have talked of nothing but trifles all this time, nothing but this trial, and I have said nothing to you about the most important thing. Yes, the trial is tomorrow, only it wasn’t the trial I meant when I said my head is lost. My head isn’t lost, but what was sitting in my head is lost. Why are you looking at me with such criticism in your face?”

“What do you mean, Mitya?”

“Ideas, ideas, that’s what! Ethics. What is ethics?”

“Ethics?” Alyosha was surprised.

“Yes, is it a science or something?”

“Yes, there is such a science… only… I confess I can’t explain to you what science it is.”

“Rakitin knows. Rakitin knows a lot, the devil take him! He won’t become a monk. He is planning to go to Petersburg. There, he says, he’ll go into the critical department, but with a noble tendency. Well, he may be of use and make a career. Ugh, they are masters at careers! The devil take ethics! I am lost, Alexei, I am, you man of God! I love you more than anyone. My heart trembles for you, that’s what. Who was that Karl Bernard?”

“Karl Bernard?” Alyosha was surprised again.

“No, not Karl; wait, I’m wrong: Claude Bernard. What is that? Chemistry or something?”

“It must be a scientist,” Alyosha answered, “only, I confess, I can’t tell you much about him either. I have only heard that he is a scientist, but what kind, I don’t know.”

“Well, the devil take him; I don’t know either,” Mitya swore. “Some scoundrel, most likely; they are all scoundrels. But Rakitin will get on; Rakitin will squeeze through a crack; he is a Bernard too. Ugh, these Bernards! There are lots of them now!”

“But what is the matter with you?” Alyosha asked insistently.

“He wants to write an article about me, about my case, and begin his part in literature with it; that’s why he comes; he explained it himself. He wants it to have a tendency: ‘he couldn’t help killing, he was eaten up by his environment’, and so on; he explained it to me. It will have a tinge of socialism, he says. Well, the devil take him; a tinge, then, a tinge; it’s all the same to me. He doesn’t like brother Ivan; he hates him; and he isn’t fond of you either. Well, and I don’t drive him away, because he is a clever man. He gives himself airs, though. I told him just now: ‘The Karamazovs are not scoundrels but philosophers, because all real Russian people are philosophers, and you, though you have studied, are not a philosopher; you are a lout.’ He laughs, so spitefully. And I said to him: de thoughtibus non est disputandum. Good joke? At least I’ve gone in for the classics too,” Mitya suddenly roared with laughter.

“Why are you lost? You said so just now,” Alyosha broke in.

“Why am I lost? Hm! In fact… if you take the whole thing, I am sorry for God, that’s why!”

“Sorry for God?”

“Imagine: it’s there in the nerves, in the head, that is, there in the brain, these nerves (the devil take them!)… there are sort of little tails, the nerves have little tails, and as soon as they start quivering there… that is, you see, I look at something with my eyes, like this, and they quiver, those little tails… and when they quiver, an image appears, and not at once, but some instant, a second, passes, and there appears a sort of moment, that is, not a moment, the devil take the moment, but an image, that is, an object or an event, well, the devil take it; and that is why I contemplate, and then think… because of the little tails, and not at all because I have a soul and am some sort of image and likeness; all that is nonsense. Mikhail explained this to me only yesterday, brother, and it was as if I’d been scalded. It is magnificent, Alyosha, this science! A new man is coming; that I do understand… And yet I am sorry for God!”

“Well, that is good too,” said Alyosha.

“That I’m sorry for God? Chemistry, brother, chemistry! There’s no help for it, your reverence; move up a little; chemistry is coming! But Rakitin doesn’t love God; ugh, how he doesn’t love him! That is the sorest spot with all of them! But they hide it. They lie. They pretend. ‘Well, will you put that forward in the critical department?’ I ask. ‘Well, they won’t let me do it openly,’ he says, and laughs. ‘But then what becomes of man?’ I ask. ‘Without God and without a future life? So now everything is permitted, one can do anything?’ ‘Didn’t you know?’ he says. He laughs. ‘For a clever man,’ he says, ‘everything is permitted; a clever man knows how to catch crayfish; but you,’ he says, ‘you killed and got caught, and you are rotting in prison!’ He says that to me! A natural pig! I used to throw such fellows out, but now I listen. He says a lot that is sensible, too. And he writes cleverly. About a week ago he began reading me an article; I copied out three lines on purpose then; wait, here they are.”

Mitya hurriedly took a scrap of paper from his waistcoat pocket and read:

“‘In order to resolve this question it is necessary, first of all, to set one’s personality in opposition to one’s reality.’ Do you understand it or not?”

“No, I don’t,” said Alyosha.

He was looking at Mitya with curiosity and listening to him.

“I don’t understand it either. Dark and obscure, but clever. ‘Everyone writes like that now,’ he says, ‘because such is the environment…’ They are afraid of the environment. He writes verses too, the scoundrel; he has sung of Khokhlakova’s foot, ha-ha-ha!”

“I have heard,” said Alyosha.

“You have? And have you heard the verses?”

“No.”

“I have them; here, I’ll read them. You don’t know; I haven’t told you; there is a whole story here. The rogue! Three weeks ago he took it into his head to tease me: ‘There you are,’ he says, ‘you have landed yourself like a fool over three thousand, but I’ll grab a hundred and fifty thousand; I’ll marry a little widow and buy a stone house in Petersburg.’ And he told me he was courting Khokhlakova, and she wasn’t clever even when she was young, and at forty she has quite lost her wits. ‘But she is very sentimental,’ he says, ‘and that’s how I shall finish her off. I’ll marry her, take her to Petersburg, and there I’ll start publishing a newspaper.’ And there was such nasty, lustful slobber on his lips, not slobber over Khokhlakova but over those hundred and fifty thousand. And he convinced me, convinced me; he kept coming to me every day: she is giving way, he says. He was beaming with joy. And then suddenly he was turned out: Pyotr Ilyich Perkhotin got the upper hand, good man! I could have kissed that fool of a woman for turning him out! It was while he was coming to see me that he made up these verses. ‘It’s the first time I have soiled my hands,’ he says, ‘writing verses, for seduction, that is, for a useful purpose. Once I have got the capital from the fool of a woman, I can be of civic use.’ They have a civic justification for every vileness! ‘And all the same,’ he says, ‘I’ve written better than your Pushkin, because even into a comic verse I have managed to slip civic grief.’ What he says about Pushkin, I understand. Well, if he really was a gifted man, and only described feet! And how proud he was of his little verses! The vanity of them, the vanity! ‘On the Recovery of the Ailing Foot of My Beloved’: that’s the title he thought up. A playful fellow!

> Oh, the little foot, the sweet,
> Swollen up, poor little feet!
> Doctors come to treat it, bind it,
> Bandage it, and maim, and wind it.
> But it’s not for feet I languish;
> Leave the feet to Pushkin’s singing:
> For the little head’s my anguish,
> Where ideas won’t start ringing.
> Once it understood a little;
> Then the foot got in its way!
> Let the foot be cured, the little,
> So the head may think one day.

A pig, a pure pig, but the scoundrel has made it playful! And he really has slipped in the ‘civic’ stuff. And how angry he was when they turned him out. He ground his teeth!”

“He has taken his revenge already,” said Alyosha. “He has written a piece about Khokhlakova for the paper.”

And Alyosha told him hastily about the correspondence in the newspaper Rumours.

“It’s he, it’s he!” Mitya confirmed, frowning. “It’s he! Those correspondences… I know… I mean, how many base things have been written already, about Grusha, for instance!… And about the other one too, about Katya… Hm!”

He walked up and down the room with a worried look.

“Brother, I can’t stay long,” Alyosha said after a pause. “Tomorrow is a terrible, great day for you: God’s judgement will be passed on you… and I wonder at you: you walk about, and instead of talking business you talk of God knows what.”

“No, don’t wonder,” Mitya broke in hotly. “What, am I to talk about that stinking dog? About the murderer? We have talked enough about that, you and I. I don’t want to talk any more about the stinking one, the son of Stinking Lizaveta! God will kill him, you’ll see; say no more!”

He came up to Alyosha in agitation and suddenly kissed him. His eyes were burning.

“Rakitin won’t understand this,” he began, as if in a kind of rapture, “but you, you will understand everything. That’s why I was thirsting for you. You see, for a long time I have wanted to say a great deal to you here, within these peeling walls, but I kept silent about the most important thing: the time seemed not to have come yet. Now I have waited till the last moment to pour out my soul to you. Brother, these last two months I have felt a new man in myself; a new man has risen in me! He was shut up inside me, but he would never have appeared if it hadn’t been for this thunderbolt. It’s terrifying! And what do I care if I spend twenty years in the mines hammering out ore? I am not afraid of that at all; it is something else I am afraid of now: that the man who has risen in me may leave me! One can find there too, in the mines, under the earth, next to oneself, in another convict and murderer, a human heart, and become close to him, because there too one can live and love and suffer! One can bring back to life and raise up the frozen heart in that convict; one can tend him for years and at last bring up from the den into the light a lofty soul, a suffering consciousness, give new life to an angel, raise up a hero! And there are many of them, hundreds, and we are all to blame for them! Why did I dream of that ‘babe’ at such a moment? ‘Why is the babe poor?’ That was a prophecy to me at that moment! I shall go for the ‘babe’. Because we are all to blame for all. For all the ‘babes’, because there are little children and big children. All are ‘babes’. I shall go for all, because someone must go for all. I didn’t kill my father, but I must go. I accept it! All this came to me here… here within these peeling walls. And there are many of them, hundreds of them there, underground, with hammers in their hands. Oh yes, we shall be in chains and there will be no freedom; but then, in our great grief, we shall rise again into joy, without which man cannot live, nor God exist, for God gives joy; it is his privilege, his great privilege… Lord, let man melt away in prayer! How shall I be there underground without God? Rakitin is lying: if they drive God from the earth, we shall meet him under the earth! A convict cannot be without God; it is even more impossible than for one who is not a convict! And then we, the men underground, will sing from the bowels of the earth a tragic hymn to God, in whom there is joy! Hail to God and his joy! I love him!”

Mitya was almost gasping as he uttered his wild speech. He had turned pale, his lips were quivering, tears were rolling from his eyes.

“No, life is full; there is life even underground!” he began again. “You won’t believe, Alexei, how I want to live now, what a thirst to exist and to be conscious has been born in me precisely within these peeling walls! Rakitin doesn’t understand it; all he wants is to build a house and let out flats; but I was waiting for you. And what is suffering? I am not afraid of it, even if it were beyond counting. I am not afraid now; I was afraid before. You know, perhaps I won’t even answer at the trial… And it seems there is so much strength in me now that I can overcome everything, all sufferings, only to say and to tell myself every minute: I am! In a thousand torments, I am; I writhe on the rack, but I am! I sit on a pillar, but I exist; I see the sun; and if I don’t see the sun, I know it is there. And to know that the sun is there, that is already the whole of life. Alyosha, my cherub, all sorts of philosophies are killing me, the devil take them! Brother Ivan…”

“What about brother Ivan?” Alyosha tried to interrupt, but Mitya did not hear.

“You see, I never had any of these doubts before, but it was all lying hidden in me. Perhaps it was precisely because unknown ideas were raging in me that I drank and fought and raged. I fought to quench them in myself, to subdue them, to crush them. Brother Ivan isn’t Rakitin; he hides an idea. Brother Ivan is a sphinx and keeps silent, always silent. But God torments me. That alone torments me. What if he doesn’t exist? What if Rakitin is right, that it is an artificial idea in mankind? Then, if he doesn’t exist, man is the chief of the earth, of the universe. Magnificent! Only how is he going to be virtuous without God? That’s the question! I keep coming back to it. For whom will man love then? To whom will he be grateful, to whom will he sing the hymn? Rakitin laughs. Rakitin says one can love mankind without God. Well, only a snivelling little toadstool could assert that; I can’t understand it. Life is easy for Rakitin. ‘You,’ he said to me today, ‘you had better bother about extending the civil rights of man, or at least about keeping the price of beef from going up; that way you will show your love for mankind more simply and directly than with philosophies.’ And I came out with this to him: ‘And you,’ I said, ‘without God, you’ll put up the price of beef yourself, if you get the chance, and make a rouble on every kopeck.’ He was angry. For what is virtue? Answer me that, Alexei. I have one virtue, and a Chinaman has another; so it is a relative thing. Or isn’t it? Or isn’t it relative? A treacherous question! You won’t laugh if I tell you I haven’t slept for two nights over it. The only thing I wonder at now is how people live there and think nothing about it. Vanity! Ivan has no God. He has an idea. It isn’t on my scale. But he keeps silent. I think he is a freemason. I asked him; he keeps silent. I wanted to drink a little water from his spring; he keeps silent. Only once he said one little word.”

“What did he say?” Alyosha caught it up hastily.

“I said to him: so then everything is permitted, if that is so? He frowned: ‘Fyodor Pavlovich, our papa, was a pig,’ he said, ‘but his thinking was right.’ That is what he came out with. That is all he said. That goes further than Rakitin.”

“Yes,” Alyosha confirmed bitterly. “When was he with you?”

“Of that later; now something else. I have told you almost nothing about Ivan so far. I have put it off till the end. When this business of mine here is over and they have pronounced the sentence, then I’ll tell you something, I’ll tell you everything. There is a terrible matter here… And you shall be my judge in this matter. But don’t begin on it now; mum’s the word for now. There you are talking about tomorrow, about the trial, and would you believe it, I know nothing.”

“Have you talked with this lawyer?”

“What of the lawyer! I told him everything. A soft rogue from the capital. A Bernard! Only he doesn’t believe me a broken farthing’s worth. He believes I killed him, imagine; I can see it. ‘Then why have you come to defend me?’ I ask him. I spit on them. They have sent for a doctor too; they want to make me out a madman. I won’t allow it! Katerina Ivanovna wants to do ‘her duty’ to the end. With an effort!” Mitya smiled bitterly. “A cat! A cruel heart! And she knows what I said about her then at Mokroye, that she is a woman ‘of great wrath’! They passed it on. Yes, the evidence has multiplied like the sand of the sea! Grigory sticks to his story. Grigory is honest, but a fool. Many people are honest because they are fools. That is a thought of Rakitin’s. Grigory is my enemy. Some people it pays better to have among one’s enemies than one’s friends. I say that of Katerina Ivanovna. I am afraid, oh, I am afraid she will tell at the trial about her bow to the ground after the four thousand five hundred! She will pay me back to the end, to the last farthing. I don’t want her sacrifice! They will put me to shame at the trial! I’ll bear it somehow. Go to her, Alyosha, ask her not to tell that at the trial. Or is it impossible? Oh, the devil, it’s all the same; I’ll bear it! And I don’t pity her. She wants it herself. The thief deserves his torment. I shall make my speech, Alexei.” He smiled bitterly again. “Only… only Grusha, Grusha, Lord! Why should she take such torment on herself now?” he suddenly exclaimed with tears. “Grusha is killing me; the thought of her is killing me, killing me! She was here with me earlier…”

“She told me. She was very much hurt by you today.”

“I know. The devil take my character. I was jealous! As I let her go I repented and kissed her. I didn’t ask her forgiveness.”

“Why didn’t you?” exclaimed Alyosha.

Mitya suddenly laughed almost gaily.

“God preserve you, my dear boy, from ever asking forgiveness of a woman you love for something you have done wrong! Of one you love especially, especially, however much you are to blame before her! Because a woman, brother, is the devil knows what; I at least know something about them! Well, try confessing your fault to her, ‘I am to blame, forgive me, excuse me’: then the hail of reproaches begins! She will never forgive you straight and simply, but will humble you to a rag, read you a lecture on things that never even happened, take everything, forget nothing, add her own, and only then will she forgive you. And that is the best, the best of them! She will scrape up the last scrapings and pile them all on your head; such a flaying spirit, I tell you, sits in them, in every one of them, in these angels without whom we can’t live! You see, my dear, I will say it frankly and simply: every decent man ought to be under the heel of some woman or other. That is my conviction; not a conviction, a feeling. A man ought to be magnanimous, and it won’t soil a man. It won’t soil even a hero; it won’t soil Caesar! But still, don’t ask forgiveness, never and for nothing. Remember the rule: it was given you by your brother Mitya, who was ruined by women. No, I had better earn Grusha’s forgiveness some other way, without asking. I revere her, Alexei, I revere her! Only she doesn’t see it; no, she thinks there is never enough love. And she torments me, torments me with love. What was before! Before, it was only her infernal curves that tormented me, but now I have taken her whole soul into my soul, and through her I have become a man myself! Will they marry us? Otherwise I shall die of jealousy. I dream of something or other every day… What did she say to you about me?”

Alyosha repeated all that Grushenka had said earlier. Mitya listened to it in detail, asked about much of it again, and was satisfied.

“So she isn’t angry that I’m jealous!” he exclaimed. “A real woman! ‘I have a cruel heart myself.’ Ugh, I love them like that, cruel ones, though I can’t bear being made jealous, can’t bear it! We shall fight. But love, I shall love her endlessly. Will they marry us? Do they marry convicts? That’s the question. And I can’t live without her…”

Mitya walked about the room frowning. It was growing almost dark in the room. He suddenly became terribly worried.

“So there is a secret, she says, a secret? That the three of us are plotting against her, and ‘Katka’ is mixed up in it? No, my dear Grushenka, that isn’t it. You have made a mistake there, your silly little woman’s mistake! Alyosha, my dear, oh, come what may! I will tell you our secret!”

He looked round in all directions, came quickly right up to Alyosha, who was standing in front of him, and began to whisper to him with a mysterious air, though in reality no one could hear them: the old warder was dozing on a bench in the corner, and not a word could reach the soldiers on guard.

“I will tell you our whole secret!” Mitya whispered hurriedly. “I meant to tell you later, because how could I decide on anything without you? You are everything to me. Though I say that Ivan is above us, you are my cherub. Only your decision will decide it. Perhaps it is you who are the higher man, and not Ivan. You see, it is a matter of conscience, a matter of the highest conscience, a secret so important that I can’t manage it myself and have put it all off until you. And yet it is too early to decide now, for we must wait for the verdict: when the verdict comes, then you will decide my fate. Don’t decide now; I’ll tell you now, you’ll hear, but don’t decide. Stand and keep silent. I won’t tell you everything. I’ll only tell you the idea, without details, and you keep silent. Not a question, not a movement; agreed? And yet, Lord, what shall I do with your eyes? I am afraid your eyes will tell me the decision, even if you are silent. Ugh, I am afraid! Alyosha, listen: brother Ivan is proposing that I escape. I won’t tell you the details: everything has been foreseen; everything can be arranged. Keep silent; don’t decide. To America with Grusha. For I can’t live without Grusha! What if they won’t let her come to me there? Do they marry convicts? Brother Ivan says not. And without Grusha what shall I do there underground with a hammer? I shall only smash my head in with that hammer! And on the other hand, what about my conscience? I shall have run away from suffering! There was a sign, and I rejected the sign; there was a path of purification, and I turned about face. Ivan says that in America, ‘with good inclinations’, one can be of more use than underground. Well, and where will our underground hymn be sung? What is America? America is vanity again! And there is a lot of swindling in America too, I expect. I shall have run away from crucifixion! I tell you this, Alexei, because you alone can understand it, and no one else; for the others it is nonsense, raving, all that I told you about the hymn. They’ll say he has gone mad, or is a fool. But I haven’t gone mad, and I am not a fool. Ivan understands about the hymn too, oh, he understands; only he doesn’t answer about it; he keeps silent. He doesn’t believe in the hymn. Don’t speak, don’t speak: I can see how you are looking: you have decided already! Don’t decide; spare me; I can’t live without Grusha; wait for the trial!”

Mitya finished as if in a frenzy. He held Alyosha by the shoulders with both hands and fastened his thirsting, feverish eyes on Alyosha’s eyes.

“Do they marry convicts?” he repeated for the third time, in an imploring voice.

Alyosha listened in extreme surprise and was deeply shaken.

“Tell me one thing,” he said. “Is Ivan very insistent, and who thought of it first?”

“He did, he thought of it; he insists! He never came to see me, and suddenly he came a week ago and began straight away with this. He insists terribly. He doesn’t ask; he orders. He has no doubt that I shall obey, though I turned my whole heart inside out to him, as to you, and talked about the hymn. He told me how he would arrange it; he has collected all the information; but that later. He wants it to the point of hysterics. The main thing is money: ten thousand for the escape, he says, and twenty thousand for America; and with ten thousand, he says, we can arrange a magnificent escape.”

“And he told you on no account to tell me?” Alyosha asked again.

“On no account, to no one, and above all to you: on no account to you! He is afraid, I expect, that you will stand before me like a conscience. Don’t tell him I told you. Oh, don’t tell him!”

“You are right,” Alyosha decided. “It is impossible to decide before the verdict of the court. After the trial you will decide yourself; then you will find the new man in yourself, and he will decide.”

“The new man, or a Bernard; and he’ll decide in Bernard’s way! For it seems I am a contemptible Bernard myself!” Mitya grinned bitterly.

“But can it really be, brother, that you have no hope at all of being acquitted?”

Mitya shrugged his shoulders convulsively and shook his head.

“Alyosha, my dear, it’s time for you to go!” he suddenly became hurried. “The governor has shouted in the yard; he’ll be here in a minute. It’s late for us; it’s irregular. Embrace me quickly, kiss me, make the sign of the cross over me, my dear, make the sign of the cross over me for tomorrow’s cross…”

They embraced and kissed.

“And Ivan,” Mitya said suddenly, “proposed that I escape, and yet he believes I killed him!”

A sad smile was squeezed out on his lips.

“Have you asked him whether he believes it or not?” asked Alyosha.

“No, I haven’t asked him. I wanted to ask, but I couldn’t; I hadn’t the strength. It doesn’t matter; I can see it in his eyes. Well, goodbye!”

They kissed once more, hastily, and Alyosha was just going out when Mitya suddenly called him back again:

“Stand in front of me, like this.”

And again he gripped Alyosha tightly by the shoulders with both hands. His face had suddenly turned quite pale, so that even in the near darkness it was terribly noticeable. His lips were twisted; his eyes were fastened on Alyosha.

“Alyosha, tell me the whole truth, as before the Lord God: do you believe that I killed him, or not? You, you yourself, do you believe it or not? The whole truth, don’t lie!” he shouted at him in a frenzy.

Alyosha seemed to sway all over, and in his heart, he felt it, something sharp seemed to pass through.

“Stop, what are you saying…” he began to murmur as if lost.

“The whole truth, the whole, don’t lie!” Mitya repeated.

“Not for a single minute have I believed that you are the murderer,” suddenly broke in a trembling voice from Alyosha’s breast, and he raised his right hand, as if calling on God to witness his words. Bliss lit up Mitya’s whole face in an instant.

“Thank you!” he brought out slowly, as if letting out a sigh after a faint. “Now you have given me new life… Would you believe it: until now I was afraid to ask you, you, you! Well, go, go! You have given me strength for tomorrow; God bless you! Well, go; love Ivan!” broke from Mitya as his last word.

Alyosha went out all in tears. Such a degree of suspiciousness in Mitya, such a degree of distrust even of him, of Alyosha: all this suddenly opened before Alyosha such an abyss of hopeless grief and despair in his unhappy brother’s soul as he had never suspected before. A deep, infinite compassion suddenly seized and tormented him in an instant. His pierced heart ached terribly. “Love Ivan!” Mitya’s words of a moment ago suddenly came back to him. Indeed he was going to Ivan. He had needed terribly to see Ivan since the morning. Ivan tormented him no less than Mitya, and now, after seeing his brother, more than ever.
