## The Song of Melancholy

## 1

When Zarathustra made these speeches, he was standing near the entrance to his cave; but with the last words he slipped away from his guests and fled into the open for a short while.

“O pure smells around me,” he cried, “O blessed stillness around me! But where are my animals? Come here, come here, my eagle and my snake!

Tell me, my animals: these higher men, all of them, do they perhaps not smell good? O pure smells around me! Only now do I know and feel how much I love you, my animals.”

And Zarathustra said again: “I love you, my animals!” But the eagle and the snake pressed close to him as he spoke these words, and looked up at him. So the three of them were together in silence, and sniffed and sipped the good air together. For the air out here was better than among the higher men.

## 2

But hardly had Zarathustra left his cave when the old sorcerer got up, looked around cunningly, and said: “He has gone out!

And already, you higher men (let me tickle you with this name of praise and flattery, as he does himself), already my wicked spirit of deceit and magic is attacking me, my melancholy devil,

who is an adversary of this Zarathustra from the very bottom: forgive him! Now he wants to work his magic before you; just now it is his hour; in vain I wrestle with this evil spirit.

To all of you, whatever honours you may give yourselves with words, whether you call yourselves ‘the free spirits’ or ‘the truthful’ or ‘the penitents of the spirit’ or ‘the unfettered’ or ‘the great longers’:

to all of you who suffer from the great disgust as I do, for whom the old god died and no new god yet lies in cradles and nappies: to all of you my evil spirit and magic devil is well disposed.

I know you, you higher men, I know him; I know this monster too, whom I love against my will, this Zarathustra: he himself often seems to me like a beautiful mask of a saint,

like a new, strange masquerade in which my evil spirit, the melancholy devil, takes pleasure: I love Zarathustra, so it often seems to me, for the sake of my evil spirit.

But already he is attacking me and forcing me, this spirit of melancholy, this devil of the evening twilight: and truly, you higher men, he has a mind to

just open your eyes! he has a mind to come naked; whether male or female I do not yet know: but he is coming, he is forcing me, alas! Open your senses!

The day is dying away; evening is coming now to all things, even the best things; hear now and see, you higher men, what devil, whether man or woman, this spirit of evening melancholy is!”

So spoke the old sorcerer, looked around cunningly, and then reached for his harp.

> with every step secretly

> out of my longings of the day,

> how you thirsted then?
> That I should be banished
> from all truth,
> only a fool! only a poet!
— the old sorcerer’s song

## On Science

So sang the sorcerer; and all who were there together went unnoticed, like birds, into the net of his cunning and melancholy delight. Only the conscientious one of the spirit was not caught: he quickly took the harp away from the sorcerer and cried: “Air! Let good air in! Let Zarathustra in! You make this cave sultry and poisonous, you wicked old sorcerer!

You lead us astray, you false, subtle one, to unknown desires and wildernesses. And woe when such as you make a fuss and a show about truth!

Woe to all free spirits who are not on their guard against such sorcerers! It is all over with their freedom: you teach and lure back into prisons,

you old melancholy devil; out of your lament sounds a decoy whistle; you are like those who with their praise of chastity secretly invite to sensual pleasures!”

So spoke the conscientious one; but the old sorcerer looked around, enjoyed his victory, and swallowed for its sake the annoyance the conscientious one caused him. “Be quiet!” he said in a modest voice. “Good songs want to echo well; after good songs one should be silent for a long time.

That is what all these do, the higher men. But you have understood little of my song, I suppose? There is little of a magic spirit in you.”

“You praise me,” replied the conscientious one, “by separating me from yourself; well then! But you others, what do I see? You are all still sitting there with lustful eyes:

you free souls, where has your freedom gone! You are almost like, it seems to me, those who have long watched wicked naked girls dancing: your souls are dancing themselves!

In you, you higher men, there must be more of what the sorcerer calls his evil spirit of magic and deceit: we must be different.

And truly, we talked and thought together enough before Zarathustra came home to his cave for me not to know that we are different.

We seek different things up here, too, you and I. For I seek more security; that is why I came to Zarathustra. For he is still the firmest tower and will,

today, when everything is shaking, when all the earth quakes. But you, when I see the eyes you make, it almost seems to me that you seek more insecurity,

more shudders, more danger, more earthquake. You long, it almost seems to me so (forgive my presumption, you higher men),

you long for the worst, most dangerous life, which makes me most afraid, for the life of wild animals, for forests, caves, steep mountains and treacherous chasms.

And it is not those who lead you out of danger that please you most, but those who lead you away from all paths, the seducers. But if such longing in you is real, it still seems impossible to me.

For fear is man’s inherited and basic feeling; everything is explained by fear, original sin and original virtue. Out of fear my virtue too grew, and it is called: science.

For fear of wild animals was bred into man longest, including the animal he hides within himself and fears: Zarathustra calls it ‘the inner beast’.

Such long, old fear, at last grown subtle, spiritual, intellectual: today, it seems to me, it is called science.”

So spoke the conscientious one; but Zarathustra, who was just coming back into his cave and had heard and guessed the last speech, threw a handful of roses at the conscientious one and laughed at his “truths”. “What!” he cried. “What did I just hear? Truly, it seems to me you are a fool, or I am one myself: and I will turn your ‘truth’ on its head, quick as a flash.

For fear is our exception. But courage and adventure and delight in the uncertain, in what has not been dared: courage seems to me the whole prehistory of man.

He envied the wildest, most courageous animals all their virtues and robbed them of them: only so did he become man.

This courage, at last grown subtle, spiritual, intellectual, this human courage with eagle’s wings and serpent’s cleverness: that, it seems to me, is called today...”

“Zarathustra!” cried all who sat together, as if from one mouth, and they laughed a great laugh; and something rose from them like a heavy cloud. The sorcerer laughed too, and said cleverly: “Well then! He has gone, my evil spirit!

And did I not warn you against him myself, when I said that he was a deceiver, a spirit of lies and deceit?

Especially when he shows himself naked. But what can I do about his tricks! Did I create him and the world?

Well then! Let us be good again and in good spirits! And even if Zarathustra looks angry (just look at him! he has a grudge against me):

before night comes, he will learn to love and praise me again; he cannot live long without doing such foolish things.

He loves his enemies: he understands this art best of anyone I have seen. But he takes his revenge for it, on his friends!”

So spoke the old sorcerer, and the higher men applauded him: so that Zarathustra went around and shook his friends’ hands with malice and love, as one who has something to make good and to apologise for to all of them. But when he came to the door of his cave as he did so, look, he already longed again for the good air out there and for his animals, and wanted to slip out.

## Among Daughters of the Desert

## 1

“Do not go away!” said the wanderer, who called himself Zarathustra’s shadow. “Stay with us, or the old dull gloom might attack us again.

That old sorcerer has already given us the worst he has, and look, the good pious pope there has tears in his eyes and has set sail again on the sea of melancholy.

These kings may still put on a good face before us: they learned that best of all of us today! But if they had no witnesses, I bet the wicked game would start again with them too,

the wicked game of passing clouds, of damp melancholy, of overcast skies, of stolen suns, of howling autumn winds,

the wicked game of our howling and crying for help: stay with us, O Zarathustra! There is much hidden misery here that wants to speak, much evening, much cloud, much stale air!

You fed us with strong men’s food and powerful sayings: do not let the soft, womanish spirits attack us again for dessert!

You alone make the air around you strong and clear! Did I ever find such good air on earth as with you in your cave?

I have seen many lands, after all; my nose has learned to test and judge many kinds of air: but with you my nostrils taste their greatest delight!

Unless, unless... oh, forgive an old memory! Forgive me an old dessert song that I once composed among daughters of the desert:

for with them there was the same good, bright, oriental air; there I was furthest from cloudy, damp, melancholy old Europe!

Then I loved such oriental girls, and other blue kingdoms of heaven, over which no clouds and no thoughts hang.

You would not believe how nicely they sat there when they were not dancing, deep, but without thoughts, like little secrets, like riddles tied with ribbons, like dessert nuts,

bright and strange indeed! but without clouds: riddles that let themselves be guessed: for the love of such girls I composed a dessert psalm then.”

So spoke the wanderer and shadow; and before anyone answered him, he had already seized the old sorcerer’s harp, crossed his legs, and looked around calmly and wisely; but with his nostrils he slowly and questioningly drew in the air, like one tasting new, strange air in new lands. Then he began to sing with a kind of roar.
