## PART I · CHAPTER I

At the beginning of July, in an exceptionally hot spell, towards evening, a young man came out of the little room he rented from tenants in S. Lane, went down into the street, and slowly, as if undecided, set off towards K. Bridge.

He had managed to avoid meeting his landlady on the stairs. His little room was right under the roof of a tall five-storey building and was more like a cupboard than a place to live. The landlady, from whom he rented this room with meals and service, lived one flight below in a flat of her own, and every time he went out he had to pass her kitchen, whose door almost always stood wide open onto the stairs. And every time he passed it the young man felt a sick, cowardly sensation that made him ashamed and made him wince. He owed his landlady money all round and was afraid of meeting her.

It was not that he was so cowardly and downtrodden; quite the opposite. But for some time he had been in an irritable, strained state, something like hypochondria. He had sunk so deep into himself and shut himself away from everyone that he was afraid of meeting anyone at all, not only his landlady. He was crushed by poverty; but lately even his straitened circumstances had stopped weighing on him. He had stopped attending to his everyday affairs altogether and did not want to attend to them. In fact he was not afraid of any landlady, whatever she might be plotting against him. But to stop on the stairs and listen to all the nonsense about all that everyday rubbish that was nothing to do with him, all the pestering about payment, the threats, the complaints, and to have to wriggle out of it himself, apologise, lie: no, better to slip down the stairs somehow like a cat and steal away so that no one saw him.

This time, however, his fear of meeting his creditor surprised even him once he was out in the street.

“What a thing I mean to attempt, and at the same time what trifles I am afraid of!” he thought with a strange smile. “Hm... yes... everything is in a man’s hands, and he lets it all slip past his nose purely out of cowardice... that is an axiom... I wonder what people are most afraid of? A new step, a new word of their own: that is what they fear most... But I am talking too much. That is why I do nothing: because I talk. Or perhaps it is this: I talk because I do nothing. I learned to talk this last month, lying for days on end in a corner and thinking about... the man in the moon. Well, why am I going there now? Am I capable of that? Is that serious? Not at all serious. I am just amusing myself with a fantasy; toys! Yes, toys, if you like!”

In the street the heat was terrible, and besides there was the stuffiness, the crowds, lime everywhere, scaffolding, bricks, dust, and that special summer stench so familiar to every Petersburger who cannot afford to rent a summer place: all this together jarred unpleasantly on the young man’s already overwrought nerves. The unbearable stench from the taverns, of which there are especially many in that part of town, and the drunks he kept running into, although it was a working day, completed the repulsive and melancholy colouring of the picture. A feeling of the deepest disgust flashed for a moment across the young man’s fine features. He was, by the way, remarkably good-looking, with beautiful dark eyes, dark brown hair, above average height, slim and well built. But soon he fell into something like deep thought, or rather into a kind of trance, and walked on no longer noticing what was around him, nor wanting to notice it. Only now and then did he mutter something to himself, from his habit of talking to himself, which he had just admitted to himself. At that moment he was also aware that his thoughts were sometimes confused and that he was very weak: for two days now he had eaten almost nothing.

He was so badly dressed that another man, even one used to it, would have been ashamed to go out in the street in such rags by day. But the district was such that it was hard to surprise anyone here by one’s clothes. The nearness of the Haymarket, the abundance of certain establishments, and the mainly working and craftsman population crowded into these central Petersburg streets and alleys sometimes speckled the general view with such figures that it would have been odd to be surprised at meeting any of them. But so much spiteful contempt had already gathered in the young man’s soul that, for all his sometimes very youthful touchiness, he was least of all ashamed of his rags in the street. It was another matter when he met certain acquaintances or former fellow students, whom in general he did not like to meet... And yet, when a drunk man who for some reason was being carted along the street just then in a huge cart drawn by a huge dray-horse suddenly shouted at him as he went by, “Hey, you, German hatter!” and bawled at the top of his voice, pointing at him, the young man suddenly stopped and clutched convulsively at his hat. It was a tall round hat from Zimmermann’s, but quite worn out, completely faded to rust, all holes and stains, without a brim, and crushed to one side at the most hideous angle. But it was not shame that seized him, but quite another feeling, even something like fright.

“I knew it!” he muttered in confusion. “I thought so! That is the worst of all! Some stupid thing like this, some most trivial detail, can ruin the whole plan! Yes, the hat is too noticeable... It is ridiculous, and that is why it is noticeable... With my rags I must have a cap, even some old pancake of a thing, not this monstrosity. Nobody wears hats like this; they will notice it a mile off, they will remember it... the main thing is, they will remember it afterwards, and there is your evidence. Here one must be as inconspicuous as possible... Trifles, trifles are what matter!.. It is just such trifles that always ruin everything...”

He did not have far to go; he even knew how many steps it was from the gate of his house: exactly seven hundred and thirty. He had counted them once, when he was very carried away by his dreams. At that time he had not yet believed in these dreams himself, and only provoked himself with their hideous but tempting daring. Now, a month later, he was already beginning to look at it differently, and in spite of all his teasing monologues about his own weakness and indecision, he had somehow, even against his will, got used to regarding the “hideous” dream as an undertaking already, although he still did not believe himself. He was even going now to make a rehearsal of his undertaking, and with every step his agitation grew stronger and stronger.

With a sinking heart and a nervous trembling he went up to an enormous building, one wall of which faced the canal and the other the street. The building was all divided into small flats and was inhabited by all kinds of working people: tailors, locksmiths, cooks, various Germans, young women living on their own, petty clerks and so on. People going in and out kept darting under both gateways and across both courtyards of the building. Three or four caretakers worked there. The young man was very glad not to meet any of them, and slipped unnoticed straight from the gateway to the right, up the stairs. The staircase was dark and narrow, a back staircase, but he already knew all this and had studied it, and he liked these surroundings: in such darkness even a curious glance was not dangerous. “If I am so afraid now, what would it be like if somehow it really came to doing the thing itself?..” he could not help thinking as he went up to the fourth floor. Here his way was blocked by some retired soldiers working as porters, who were carrying furniture out of one of the flats. He already knew that a German official with a family lived in this flat. “So this German is moving out now, and so on the fourth floor, on this staircase and this landing, there will be only the old woman’s flat occupied for a while. That is good... just in case...” he thought again, and rang at the old woman’s flat. The bell gave a faint clank, as if it were made of tin and not of copper. In such small flats in such buildings almost all the bells are like that. He had already forgotten the sound of this little bell, and now its peculiar tinkle seemed suddenly to remind him of something and bring it clearly before him... He gave a start; his nerves were too weak this time. A little later the door opened a tiny crack: the occupant was looking at the visitor through the crack with evident distrust, and all that could be seen were her little eyes glittering out of the darkness. But seeing a lot of people on the landing, she took courage and opened the door wide. The young man stepped over the threshold into the dark entrance hall, which was divided by a partition, behind which was a tiny kitchen. The old woman stood before him in silence and looked at him questioningly. She was a tiny, dried-up old woman of about sixty, with sharp, spiteful little eyes, a small sharp nose, and no headscarf. Her fair hair, only a little grey, was thickly greased with oil. Round her thin, long neck, which looked like a chicken’s leg, some flannel rag was wound, and on her shoulders, in spite of the heat, hung a fur jacket, all worn out and yellowed. The little old woman kept coughing and groaning. The young man must have looked at her with some special look, because the same distrust suddenly flashed in her eyes again.

“Raskolnikov, a student, I was here a month ago,” the young man hastened to mutter with a half-bow, remembering that he ought to be more polite.

“I remember, my dear, I remember very well that you were here,” the old woman said distinctly, still not taking her questioning eyes from his face.

“Well then... and again, on the same sort of little business...” Raskolnikov went on, a little embarrassed and surprised at the old woman’s distrust.

“Perhaps she is always like this, though, and I did not notice it that time,” he thought with an unpleasant feeling.

The old woman was silent, as if thinking it over, then stepped aside and, pointing to the door into the room, said, letting her visitor go ahead:

“Go in, my dear.”

The small room the young man went into, with yellow wallpaper, geraniums and muslin curtains at the windows, was brightly lit at that moment by the setting sun. “And then too, it seems, the sun will be shining just like this!..” flashed through Raskolnikov’s mind as if by chance, and with a quick glance he took in everything in the room, in order to study and remember the arrangement as far as possible. But there was nothing special in the room. The furniture, all very old and made of yellow wood, consisted of a sofa with an enormous curved wooden back, a round table of oval shape in front of the sofa, a dressing table with a little mirror between the windows, chairs along the walls, and two or three cheap pictures in yellow frames showing German young ladies with birds in their hands: that was all the furniture. In the corner a little lamp was burning in front of a small icon. Everything was very clean: the furniture and the floors had been rubbed to a polish; everything shone. “Lizaveta’s work,” thought the young man. Not a speck of dust could be found in the whole flat. “Spiteful old widows always have such cleanliness,” Raskolnikov went on to himself, and glanced curiously at the cotton curtain over the door into the second, tiny room, where the old woman’s bed and chest of drawers stood and into which he had never yet looked. The whole flat consisted of these two rooms.

“What do you want?” the little old woman said sternly, coming into the room and standing as before right in front of him, so as to look him straight in the face.

“I have brought something to pawn, here!” And he took out of his pocket an old flat silver watch. On the back of it a globe was engraved. The chain was steel.

“But the time is up on your last pledge too. It was a month ago the day before yesterday.”

“I will pay you the interest for another month; be patient.”

“That is as I please, my dear, to be patient or to sell your thing right now.”

“How much will you give for the watch, Alyona Ivanovna?”

“You come with trifles, my dear; it is worth next to nothing. For the ring I gave you two notes last time, and you could buy it new from a jeweller for a rouble and a half.”

“Give me four roubles or so, I will redeem it; it was my father’s. I shall be getting money soon.”

“A rouble and a half, and the interest in advance, if you like.”

“A rouble and a half!” cried the young man.

“As you please.” And the old woman held the watch out to him. The young man took it and was so angry that he was about to leave; but he thought better of it at once, remembering that there was nowhere else to go and that he had come for something else as well.

“Give it here!” he said rudely.

The old woman reached into her pocket for her keys and went into the other room behind the curtain. The young man, left alone in the middle of the room, listened curiously and reckoned things up. He could hear her unlock the chest of drawers. “It must be the top drawer,” he reckoned. “So she carries her keys in her right-hand pocket... All on one bunch, on a steel ring... And there is one key there bigger than all the others, three times as big, with a notched bit, not for the chest of drawers, of course... So there is some other box, or a trunk... That is interesting. Trunks always have keys like that... But how vile all this is...”

The old woman came back.

“Here you are, my dear: if it is ten kopecks a month on the rouble, then for a rouble and a half you owe fifteen kopecks, for a month in advance. And for the two roubles before, by the same reckoning, you owe another twenty kopecks in advance. So altogether thirty-five. So what you get now for your watch is one rouble fifteen kopecks. Here you are.”

“What! So now it is a rouble fifteen!”

“Just so.”

The young man did not argue and took the money. He looked at the old woman and was in no hurry to leave, as if there were something more he wanted to say or do, but as if he himself did not know exactly what...

“I may bring you another thing in a day or two, Alyona Ivanovna... silver... a good one... a cigarette case... as soon as I get it back from a friend...” He became confused and fell silent.

“Well, then we shall talk about it, my dear.”

“Goodbye... And you are always at home alone, your sister is not here?” he asked, as casually as he could, as he went out into the hall.

“And what is she to you, my dear?”

“Oh, nothing in particular. I just asked. You are so quick... Goodbye, Alyona Ivanovna!”

Raskolnikov went out in utter confusion. This confusion grew greater and greater. Going down the stairs, he even stopped several times, as if suddenly struck by something. And at last, out in the street, he exclaimed:

“Oh God! How loathsome all this is! And can I really, can I really... no, it is nonsense, it is absurd!” he added firmly. “And could such a horror really have come into my head? What filth my heart is capable of, though! Above all: filthy, foul, vile, vile!.. And for a whole month I...”

But he could not express his agitation in words or exclamations. The feeling of boundless disgust, which had begun to weigh on and sicken his heart even while he was only on his way to the old woman, had now reached such a pitch and become so clear that he did not know where to escape from his anguish. He walked along the pavement like a drunk man, not noticing the passers-by and bumping into them, and came to himself only in the next street. Looking round, he noticed that he was standing beside a tavern, the entrance to which was from the pavement, down some steps into the basement. Just at that moment two drunk men were coming out of the door, supporting each other and swearing at each other as they climbed up to the street. Without thinking long, Raskolnikov went down at once. He had never been into a tavern until now, but now his head was spinning, and besides, a burning thirst was tormenting him. He wanted to drink some cold beer, all the more because he put his sudden weakness down partly to being hungry. He sat down in a dark and dirty corner at a sticky little table, asked for beer, and greedily drank the first glass. At once everything eased, and his thoughts cleared. “All that is nonsense,” he said hopefully, “and there was nothing to be upset about! Simply a physical disorder! One glass of beer, a piece of rusk, and look, in a moment the mind grows strong, the thought grows clear, the intentions grow firm! Pah, how worthless all this is!..” But in spite of this contemptuous spitting, he was already looking cheerful, as if suddenly freed from some terrible burden, and he glanced round in a friendly way at the people there. But even at that moment he had a distant sense that all this readiness to feel better was sick too.

There were few people left in the tavern at that hour. Besides the two drunks he had met on the stairs, a whole band had gone out right after them, five or so, with a girl and a concertina. After they left it became quiet and roomy. Those who stayed were: one man who was tipsy, but not very, sitting over his beer, to look at him a tradesman; his companion, fat, huge, in a Siberian coat and with a grey beard, very drunk, who had dozed off on the bench and now and then, suddenly, as if half asleep, would begin snapping his fingers, spreading his arms wide, and bouncing the upper part of his body without getting up from the bench, singing some nonsense as he did so, trying to remember the words, something like:

> All year long he loved his wife,
> All year lo-ong he lo-oved his wi-ife...

Or suddenly, waking up, again:

> Down Podyacheskaya I went,
> And found my old girl there...

But no one shared his happiness; his silent companion looked at all these outbursts even with hostility and distrust. There was one more man there too, to look at him something like a retired official. He sat apart, in front of his bottle, taking a sip now and then and looking around. He too seemed to be in some agitation.
