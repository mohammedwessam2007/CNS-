## Part Four · Chapter X

Pestsov liked to argue a thing to the end and was not satisfied with Sergei Ivanovich’s words, all the more because he felt the injustice of his own opinion.

“I never meant density of population alone,” he said over the soup, turning to Alexei Alexandrovich, “but in combination with foundations, not with principles.”

“It seems to me,” Alexei Alexandrovich answered unhurriedly and languidly, “that it is one and the same thing. In my opinion only a people that has a higher development can act upon another people, one that…”

“But that is just the question,” Pestsov interrupted in his bass voice, for he was always in a hurry to speak and seemed always to put his whole soul into what he was talking about, “what are we to take higher development to consist of? The English, the French, the Germans: who stands at the highest level of development? Who is to nationalize whom? We see that the Rhine has become French, and yet the Germans are not lower!” he shouted. “There is another law here!”

“It seems to me that influence is always on the side of true education,” said Alexei Alexandrovich, slightly raising his eyebrows.

“But what are we to take as the signs of true education?” said Pestsov.

“I suppose those signs are known,” said Alexei Alexandrovich.

“Are they fully known?” Sergei Ivanovich put in with a subtle smile. “It is now accepted that real education can only be purely classical; but we see fierce disputes on both sides, and it cannot be denied that the opposing camp too has strong arguments in its favour.”

“You are a classicist, Sergei Ivanovich. Will you have some red wine?” said Stepan Arkadyich.

“I am not expressing my opinion on either kind of education,” said Sergei Ivanovich with a smile of indulgence, as to a child, holding out his glass. “I only say that both sides have strong arguments,” he went on, turning to Alexei Alexandrovich. “I am a classicist by education, but personally I can find no place for myself in this dispute. I see no clear reasons why the classical studies should be given preference over the modern ones.”

“The natural sciences have just as great an educational, developing influence,” Pestsov took up. “Take astronomy alone, take botany, or zoology with its system of general laws!”

“I cannot entirely agree with that,” answered Alexei Alexandrovich. “It seems to me that one cannot help admitting that the very process of studying the forms of languages has a particularly beneficial effect on spiritual development. Besides, it cannot be denied that the influence of the classical writers is moral in the highest degree, whereas, unfortunately, with the teaching of the natural sciences are bound up those harmful and false doctrines that are the plague of our time.”

Sergei Ivanovich wanted to say something, but Pestsov interrupted him in his thick bass. He began hotly proving the injustice of this opinion. Sergei Ivanovich calmly waited his turn, evidently with a victorious reply ready.

“But,” said Sergei Ivanovich, smiling subtly and turning to Karenin, “one must agree that it is difficult to weigh fully all the advantages and disadvantages of the one kind of study and the other, and that the question of which to prefer would not have been decided so quickly and finally if there had not been on the side of classical education that advantage you have just expressed: the moral, disons le mot, the anti-nihilist influence.”

“Undoubtedly.”

“If it were not for this advantage of anti-nihilist influence on the side of the classical studies, we should have thought more, weighed the arguments of both sides,” said Sergei Ivanovich with a subtle smile; “we should have given room to both tendencies. But now we know that in these pills of classical education lies the healing power of anti-nihilism, and we boldly prescribe them to our patients… But what if there is no healing power in them?” he concluded, sprinkling his Attic salt.

At Sergei Ivanovich’s pills everyone laughed, and especially loudly and gaily Turovtsyn, who had at last waited for the funny thing that was all he had been waiting for while listening to the conversation.

Stepan Arkadyich had not been mistaken in inviting Pestsov. With Pestsov, clever conversation could not flag for a minute. As soon as Sergei Ivanovich had closed the conversation with his joke, Pestsov at once raised a new one.

“One cannot even agree,” he said, “that the government has this aim. The government is evidently guided by general considerations and remains indifferent to the influences the measures it takes may have. For instance, the question of women’s education ought to have been considered harmful, and yet the government opens courses and universities for women.”

And the conversation at once jumped to the new subject of women’s education.

Alexei Alexandrovich expressed the thought that the education of women is usually confused with the question of the freedom of women, and only for that reason can be considered harmful.

“I, on the contrary, suppose that these two questions are inseparably connected,” said Pestsov. “It is a vicious circle. Woman is deprived of rights because of a lack of education, and the lack of education comes from the absence of rights. We must not forget that the enslavement of women is so great and so old that we often do not want to understand the gulf that separates them from us,” he said.

“You said rights,” said Sergei Ivanovich, waiting until Pestsov was silent, “the rights of serving as jurors, as councillors, as chairmen of boards, the rights of an official, of a member of parliament…”

“Undoubtedly.”

“But if women, as a rare exception, can fill these posts, then, it seems to me, you have used the word ‘rights’ wrongly. It would be more correct to say: duties. Everyone will agree that in performing any office, of juror, of councillor, of telegraph clerk, we feel that we are performing a duty. And so it would be more correct to say that women are seeking duties, and quite legitimately. And one can only sympathize with this wish of theirs to help in the general work of men.”

“Perfectly just,” Alexei Alexandrovich confirmed. “The question, I suppose, consists only in whether they are capable of these duties.”

“They will probably be very capable,” Stepan Arkadyich put in, “when education has spread among them. We see that…”

“And the proverb?” said the old prince, who had long been listening to the conversation, his small mocking eyes shining. “One can say it in front of my daughters: long of hair…”

“Exactly the same was thought of the Negroes before their emancipation!” said Pestsov angrily.

“I only find it strange that women should seek new duties,” said Sergei Ivanovich, “when, unfortunately, we see that men usually avoid them.”

“Duties are bound up with rights: power, money, honours; that is what women are seeking,” said Pestsov.

“It’s as if I were to seek the right to be a wet-nurse and be offended that women are paid for it and nobody will pay me,” said the old prince.

Turovtsyn burst into loud laughter, and Sergei Ivanovich was sorry it was not he who had said it. Even Alexei Alexandrovich smiled.

“Yes, but a man cannot nurse,” said Pestsov, “while a woman…”

“No, there was an Englishman who nursed his baby on board ship,” said the old prince, allowing himself this freedom of conversation in front of his daughters.

“There will be as many women officials as there are such Englishmen,” said Sergei Ivanovich this time.

“Yes, but what is a girl to do who has no family?” Stepan Arkadyich put in, remembering the Chibisova girl, whom he had had in mind all along, sympathizing with Pestsov and supporting him.

“If you look properly into that girl’s story, you will find that she has left a family, her own or her sister’s, where she could have had women’s work,” said Darya Alexandrovna irritably, unexpectedly entering the conversation, probably guessing which girl Stepan Arkadyich had in mind.

“But we are standing up for a principle, for an ideal!” Pestsov objected in his resonant bass. “Woman wants to have the right to be independent, educated. She is constrained, crushed by the awareness that this is impossible.”

“And I am constrained and crushed because they won’t take me as a wet-nurse at the foundling hospital,” said the old prince again, to the great delight of Turovtsyn, who from laughing dropped his asparagus thick end first into the sauce.

## Chapter XI

Everyone took part in the general conversation except Kitty and Levin. At first, when they were talking about the influence one people has on another, there came into Levin’s head, without his wishing it, what he had to say on the subject; but these thoughts, once very important to him, flickered through his head as if in a dream and had not the slightest interest for him now. It even seemed strange to him that they should take such pains to talk about what no one needed. In the same way, it would seem, what they were saying about the rights and education of women ought to have been interesting to Kitty. How many times she had thought about it, remembering her friend from abroad, Varenka, and her hard dependence; how many times she had thought to herself about what would become of her if she did not marry; and how many times she had argued about it with her sister! But now it did not interest her in the least. She and Levin were having a conversation of their own, and not a conversation, but some mysterious communion, which every minute bound them more closely together and produced in both of them a feeling of joyful fear before the unknown into which they were entering.

At first Levin, in answer to Kitty’s question of how he could have seen her the year before in a carriage, told her how he had been walking home from the mowing along the high road and had met her.

“It was very, very early in the morning. You must only just have woken up. Your maman was asleep in her corner. It was a wonderful morning. I was walking along and thinking: who is that with four horses in a carriage? A splendid team of four with bells, and for an instant you flashed past, and I saw you at the window: you were sitting like this, holding the strings of your bonnet with both hands, and thinking terribly hard about something,” he said, smiling. “How I would like to know what you were thinking about then! Something important?”

“Wasn’t my hair untidy?” she thought; but, seeing the rapturous smile that these details called up in his memory, she felt that, on the contrary, the impression she had made had been very good. She blushed and laughed joyfully.

“Really, I don’t remember.”

“How nicely Turovtsyn laughs!” said Levin, admiring his moist eyes and shaking body.

“Have you known him long?” asked Kitty.

“Who doesn’t know him!”

“And I see you think he is a bad man?”

“Not bad, but a nonentity.”

“That’s not true! And stop thinking so at once!” said Kitty. “I too had a very low opinion of him, but he is, he is a very nice and wonderfully kind man. He has a heart of gold.”

“How could you have found out about his heart?”

“He and I are great friends. I know him very well. Last winter, soon after… after you were at our house,” she said with a guilty and at the same time trusting smile, “all Dolly’s children had scarlet fever, and he happened to call on her. And can you imagine,” she said in a whisper, “he felt so sorry for her that he stayed and began helping her look after the children. Yes, and he lived in their house for three weeks and looked after the children like a nurse.”

“I am telling Konstantin Dmitrich about Turovtsyn and the scarlet fever,” she said, leaning over to her sister.

“Yes, it was wonderful, he’s a darling!” said Dolly, glancing at Turovtsyn, who felt that they were talking about him, and smiling gently at him. Levin glanced at Turovtsyn once more and wondered how he had not understood before all the charm of this man.

“I’m sorry, I’m sorry, and I’ll never think badly of people again!” he said gaily, sincerely expressing what he now felt.

## Chapter XII

In the conversation that had been started about the rights of women there were questions, delicate in the presence of ladies, about the inequality of rights in marriage. Several times during dinner Pestsov had swooped down on these questions, but Sergei Ivanovich and Stepan Arkadyich had carefully steered him away.

But when they got up from the table and the ladies went out, Pestsov, instead of following them, turned to Alexei Alexandrovich and began setting out the chief cause of the inequality. The inequality of husband and wife, in his opinion, lay in the fact that a wife’s unfaithfulness and a husband’s unfaithfulness are punished unequally, both by the law and by public opinion.

Stepan Arkadyich hurriedly went up to Alexei Alexandrovich, offering him a cigar.

“No, I don’t smoke,” Alexei Alexandrovich answered calmly, and, as though deliberately wishing to show that he was not afraid of this conversation, he turned to Pestsov with a cold smile.

“I suppose the grounds for such a view lie in the very nature of things,” he said, and was about to go into the drawing-room; but here Turovtsyn suddenly and unexpectedly spoke, turning to Alexei Alexandrovich.

“And have you heard about Pryachnikov?” said Turovtsyn, enlivened by the champagne he had drunk and having long waited for a chance to break the silence that weighed on him. “Vasya Pryachnikov,” he said with his good-natured smile of moist, red lips, addressing himself chiefly to the principal guest, Alexei Alexandrovich, “I was told today that he fought a duel in Tver with Kvytsky and killed him.”

Just as it always seems that one knocks a sore place as if on purpose, so now Stepan Arkadyich felt that, unluckily, the conversation was every minute striking Alexei Alexandrovich’s sore place. He wanted to lead his brother-in-law away again, but Alexei Alexandrovich himself asked with curiosity:

“What did Pryachnikov fight about?”

“About his wife. He acted like a fine fellow! Challenged him and killed him!”

“Ah!” said Alexei Alexandrovich indifferently, and, raising his eyebrows, went into the drawing-room.

“How glad I am you have come,” Dolly said to him with a frightened smile, meeting him in the outer drawing-room. “I must talk to you. Let us sit here.”

Alexei Alexandrovich, with the same expression of indifference that his raised eyebrows gave him, sat down beside Darya Alexandrovna and smiled a feigned smile.

“All the more,” he said, “since I was just going to ask you to excuse me and take my leave at once. I have to go away tomorrow.”

Darya Alexandrovna was firmly convinced of Anna’s innocence, and she felt that she was turning pale and her lips were trembling with anger at this cold, unfeeling man who was so calmly intending to ruin her innocent friend.

“Alexei Alexandrovich,” she said, looking him in the eyes with desperate resolution, “I asked you about Anna, and you did not answer me. How is she?”

“She is well, I believe, Darya Alexandrovna,” answered Alexei Alexandrovich, without looking at her.

“Alexei Alexandrovich, forgive me, I have no right… but I love and respect Anna like a sister; I beg you, I implore you, to tell me what there is between you. What do you accuse her of?”

Alexei Alexandrovich frowned and, almost closing his eyes, lowered his head.

“I suppose your husband has told you the reasons why I consider it necessary to change my former relations with Anna Arkadyevna,” he said, not looking her in the eyes but glancing with displeasure at Shcherbatsky, who was passing through the drawing-room.

“I don’t believe it, I don’t believe it, I can’t believe it!” said Dolly, clasping her bony hands before her with an energetic gesture. She got up quickly and laid her hand on Alexei Alexandrovich’s sleeve. “We shall be interrupted here. Come this way, please.”

Darya Alexandrovna’s agitation had an effect on Alexei Alexandrovich. He got up and followed her submissively into the schoolroom. They sat down at a table covered with oilcloth cut all over by penknives.

“I don’t believe it, I don’t believe it!” said Dolly, trying to catch his eyes, which avoided hers.

“One cannot refuse to believe facts, Darya Alexandrovna,” he said, stressing the word facts.

“But what has she done? What? What?” said Darya Alexandrovna. “What exactly has she done?”

“She has scorned her duties and deceived her husband. That is what she has done,” he said.

“No, no, it cannot be! No, for God’s sake, you are mistaken!” said Dolly, touching her temples with her hands and closing her eyes.

Alexei Alexandrovich smiled coldly with his lips alone, wishing to show her and himself the firmness of his conviction; but this hot defence, though it did not shake him, inflamed his wound. He began to speak with greater animation.

“It is very difficult to be mistaken when the wife herself announces it to the husband. Announces that eight years of life and a son, that all this is a mistake and that she wants to begin living over again,” he said angrily, breathing through his nose.

“Anna and vice: I cannot put them together, I cannot believe it.”

“Darya Alexandrovna!” he said, now looking straight into Dolly’s kind, agitated face and feeling that his tongue was loosening in spite of him. “What I would give for doubt to be still possible! When I doubted, it was hard for me, but easier than now. When I doubted, there was hope; but now there is no hope, and still I doubt everything. I doubt everything, I hate my son, sometimes I don’t believe he is my son. I am very unhappy.”

He did not need to say this. Darya Alexandrovna understood it as soon as he looked into her face; and she felt sorry for him, and her faith in her friend’s innocence was shaken.

“Oh, it is terrible, terrible! But is it really true that you have decided on a divorce?”

“I have decided on the last measure. There is nothing else for me to do.”

“Nothing else to do, nothing else to do…” she said, with tears in her eyes. “No, it is not true that there is nothing else to do!” she said.

“That is just what is terrible in this kind of grief: that one cannot, as in any other, in a loss, in a death, bear one’s cross; here one must act,” he said, as though guessing her thought. “One must get out of the humiliating position in which one has been placed: three cannot live together.”

“I understand, I understand that very well,” said Dolly, and lowered her head. She was silent for a while, thinking of herself, of her own family grief, and suddenly she raised her head with an energetic movement and clasped her hands in a gesture of entreaty. “But wait! You are a Christian. Think of her! What will become of her if you cast her off?”

“I have thought, Darya Alexandrovna, I have thought a great deal,” said Alexei Alexandrovich. His face flushed in patches, and his dull eyes looked straight at her. Darya Alexandrovna now pitied him with all her soul. “That is just what I did after she herself announced my disgrace to me; I left everything as it was. I gave her the chance to mend her ways; I tried to save her. And what happened? She did not fulfil the slightest demand, the observance of propriety,” he said, growing heated. “One can save a person who does not want to perish; but if the whole nature is so corrupted, so depraved, that ruin itself seems salvation to her, what is to be done?”

“Anything, only not divorce!” answered Darya Alexandrovna.

“But what is anything?”

“No, it is terrible. She will be no one’s wife; she will be ruined!”

“What can I do?” said Alexei Alexandrovich, raising his shoulders and eyebrows. The memory of his wife’s last act irritated him so much that he became cold again, as at the beginning of the conversation. “I thank you very much for your sympathy, but it is time for me to go,” he said, getting up.

“No, wait! You must not ruin her. Wait, I will tell you about myself. I married. My husband deceived me; in anger, in jealousy, I wanted to throw everything up, I wanted myself… But I came to my senses; and who did it? Anna saved me. And here I am, living. The children are growing up, my husband is coming back to the family and feels his wrong, he is becoming purer, better, and I live… I forgave, and you must forgive!”

Alexei Alexandrovich listened to her, but her words no longer acted on him. In his soul there rose again all the fury of the day when he had decided on the divorce. He shook himself and began to speak in a shrill, loud voice:

“I cannot forgive, and I do not want to, and I consider it unjust. I have done everything for this woman, and she has trampled it all into the mud that is natural to her. I am not a wicked man, I have never hated anyone, but her I hate with all the strength of my soul, and I cannot even forgive her, because I hate her too much for all the evil she has done me!” he said, with tears of fury in his voice.

“Love those that hate you…” Darya Alexandrovna whispered shyly.

Alexei Alexandrovich smiled contemptuously. That he had long known, but it could not be applied to his case.

“Love those that hate you; but to love those whom you hate is impossible. Forgive me for having upset you. Everyone has grief enough of his own!” And, mastering himself, Alexei Alexandrovich calmly took his leave and went away.

## Chapter XIII

When they got up from the table, Levin wanted to follow Kitty into the drawing-room; but he was afraid it might be unpleasant to her, as making his courting of her too obvious. He stayed in the circle of men, taking part in the general conversation, and, without looking at Kitty, he felt her movements, her glances, and the place where she was in the drawing-room.

Already now, without the slightest effort, he was keeping the promise he had made her: always to think well of everyone and always to love everyone. The conversation turned to the village commune, in which Pestsov saw some special principle, which he called the choral principle. Levin agreed neither with Pestsov nor with his brother, who in some way of his own both did and did not recognize the significance of the Russian commune. But he talked with them, trying only to reconcile them and soften their objections. He was not in the least interested in what he himself was saying, still less in what they were saying, and wished for only one thing: that they and everyone should feel good and pleasant. He knew now the one thing that was important. And that one thing was at first there, in the drawing-room, and then began to move and stopped at the door. Without turning round, he felt a look and a smile directed at him, and he could not help turning round. She was standing in the doorway with Shcherbatsky and looking at him.

“I thought you were going to the piano,” he said, going up to her. “That is what I miss in the country: music.”

“No, we only came to call you out, and thank you,” she said, rewarding him with a smile as if with a present, “for coming. What is the pleasure of arguing? After all, no one ever convinces anyone.”

“Yes, that’s true,” said Levin. “It mostly happens that one argues hotly only because one cannot possibly understand what exactly one’s opponent wants to prove.”

Levin had often noticed in arguments between the cleverest people that, after enormous efforts, an enormous quantity of logical subtleties and words, those arguing came at last to the awareness that what they had long been struggling to prove to each other had been known to them long, long ago, from the beginning of the argument, but that they loved different things and so did not want to name what they loved, so as not to have it disputed. He had often had the experience that sometimes during an argument one understands what one’s opponent loves, and suddenly one comes to love it oneself and at once agrees, and then all the arguments fall away as unnecessary; and sometimes he had experienced the reverse: one at last speaks out what one loves oneself, and for the sake of which one has been inventing arguments, and if it happens that one says it well and sincerely, then suddenly the opponent agrees and stops arguing. This was just what he wanted to say.

She wrinkled her forehead, trying to understand. But he had only begun to explain when she already understood.

“I understand: one must find out what he is arguing for, what he loves, and then one can…”

She had guessed completely, and put into words his badly expressed thought. Levin smiled joyfully: so striking was this passage from the tangled, wordy argument with Pestsov and his brother to this laconic and clear, almost wordless communication of the most complex thoughts.

Shcherbatsky moved away from them, and Kitty, going up to a card-table that had been set out, sat down and, taking a piece of chalk in her hand, began drawing diverging circles on the new green cloth.

They went back to the conversation that had been going on at dinner: about the freedom and occupations of women. Levin agreed with Darya Alexandrovna’s opinion that a girl who did not marry would find woman’s work for herself in a family. He supported this by saying that no family can do without a helper, that in every family, poor or rich, there are and must be nurses, hired or of the family.

“No,” said Kitty, blushing, but looking at him all the more boldly with her truthful eyes, “a girl may be so placed that she cannot enter a family without humiliation, while she herself…”

He understood her from the hint.

“Oh, yes!” he said. “Yes, yes, yes, you are right, you are right!”

And he understood everything Pestsov had been proving at dinner about the freedom of women, only because he saw in Kitty’s heart the fear of spinsterhood and humiliation; and, loving her, he felt that fear and humiliation, and at once renounced his arguments.

A silence followed. She was still drawing with the chalk on the table. Her eyes shone with a quiet brightness. Giving in to her mood, he felt in his whole being an ever-growing tension of happiness.

“Oh, I have scribbled all over the table!” she said, and, putting down the chalk, made a movement as though she meant to get up.

“How can I be left alone… without her?” he thought with horror, and took the chalk. “Wait,” he said, sitting down at the table. “I have long wanted to ask you one thing.”

He looked straight into her kind, though frightened, eyes.

“Please ask it.”

“Here,” he said, and wrote the initial letters: w, y, a, m: t, c, b, d, t, m, n, o, t? These letters meant: “When you answered me: that cannot be, did that mean never, or then?” There was no likelihood that she could understand this complicated sentence; but he looked at her as though his life depended on whether she understood these words.

She looked at him seriously, then leaned her frowning forehead on her hand and began to read. Now and then she glanced at him, asking him with her look: “Is it what I think?”

“I understand,” she said, blushing.

“What is this word?” he said, pointing to the n, which stood for the word never.

“That word means never,” she said, “but it is not true!”

He quickly rubbed out what he had written, gave her the chalk and stood up. She wrote: t, I, c, n, a, o.

Dolly was entirely consoled for the grief caused her by her conversation with Alexei Alexandrovich when she saw these two figures: Kitty, with the chalk in her hand and with a timid and happy smile, looking up at Levin, and his handsome figure bending over the table, with burning eyes turned now on the table, now on her. Suddenly he beamed: he had understood. It meant: “Then I could not answer otherwise.”

He looked at her questioningly, timidly.

“Only then?”

“Yes,” her smile answered.

“And n… And now?” he asked.

“Well, then read this. I will say what I should like. What I should like very much!” She wrote the initial letters: t, y, m, f, a, f, w, h. That meant: “that you might forget and forgive what happened.”

He seized the chalk with tense, trembling fingers and, breaking it, wrote the initial letters of the following: “I have nothing to forget and forgive; I have never stopped loving you.”

She looked at him with a smile that stood still.

“I understand,” she said in a whisper.

He sat down and wrote a long sentence. She understood it all, and, without asking him whether it was right, took the chalk and answered at once.

For a long time he could not understand what she had written, and often looked into her eyes. A dimness of happiness came over him. He could not supply the words she meant; but in her lovely eyes, shining with happiness, he understood everything he needed to know. And he wrote three letters. But he had not yet finished writing when she was already reading behind his hand, and she finished it herself and wrote the answer: Yes.

“Playing at secrétaire?” said the prince, coming up. “Well, we must be going, though, if you want to be in time for the theatre.”

Levin got up and saw Kitty to the door.

In their conversation everything had been said: it had been said that she loved him, and that she would tell her father and mother, and that he would come tomorrow morning.

## Chapter XIV

When Kitty had gone and Levin was left alone, he felt such uneasiness without her and such an impatient desire to live through to tomorrow morning as quickly as possible, when he would see her again and be united with her for ever, that he was afraid, as of death, of the fourteen hours he had to spend without her. He had to be with someone and talk to someone, so as somehow not to be left alone, so as to cheat the time. Stepan Arkadyich would have been the most pleasant companion for him, but he was going, as he said, to an evening party, in reality to the ballet. Levin only had time to tell him that he was happy, and that he loved him and would never, never forget what he had done for him. Stepan Arkadyich’s look and smile showed Levin that he understood this feeling as it should be understood.

“Well, so it’s not yet time to die?” said Stepan Arkadyich, pressing Levin’s hand with emotion.

“N-n-no!” said Levin.

Darya Alexandrovna, taking leave of him, also seemed to congratulate him, saying:

“How glad I am that you and Kitty have met again; one must value old friendships.”

But these words of Darya Alexandrovna’s were unpleasant to Levin. She could not understand how lofty and out of her reach all this was, and she ought not to have dared to mention it.

Levin took leave of them, but, so as not to be left alone, attached himself to his brother.

“Where are you going?”

“To a meeting.”

“Well, I’ll come with you. May I?”

“Why not? Come along,” said Sergei Ivanovich, smiling. “What is the matter with you today?”

“With me? Happiness is the matter with me!” said Levin, letting down the window of the carriage they were driving in. “You don’t mind? It’s stuffy. Happiness is the matter with me! Why have you never married?”

Sergei Ivanovich smiled.

“I am very glad; she seems a splendid gi…” Sergei Ivanovich began.

“Don’t say it, don’t say it, don’t say it!” cried Levin, seizing him with both hands by the collar of his fur coat and wrapping it round him. “She is a splendid girl” were such simple, low words, so out of keeping with his feeling.

Sergei Ivanovich laughed a merry laugh, which was rare with him.

“Well, at any rate one may say that I am very glad of it.”

“That you may say tomorrow, tomorrow, and nothing more! Nothing, nothing, silence!” said Levin, and, wrapping the fur coat round him once more, he added: “I love you very much! Well, may I come into the meeting?”

“Of course you may.”

“What is the discussion about today?” asked Levin, never ceasing to smile.

They arrived at the meeting. Levin listened as the secretary, stumbling, read the minutes, which he evidently did not understand himself; but Levin saw from the secretary’s face what a nice, kind, splendid man he was. This could be seen from the way he got muddled and embarrassed as he read the minutes. Then the speeches began. They were arguing about the allocation of certain sums and the laying of certain pipes, and Sergei Ivanovich stung two members and talked triumphantly for a long time about something; and another member, after writing something on a slip of paper, was timid at first, but then answered him very venomously and charmingly. And then Sviyazhsky (he was there too) also said something so beautifully and nobly. Levin listened to them and saw clearly that there were no allocated sums, no pipes, nothing of the kind, and that they were not angry at all, but were all such kind, splendid people, and that everything was going so nicely and pleasantly among them. They were in no one’s way, and everyone was enjoying it. What was remarkable to Levin was that today he could see through all of them, and by little signs, unnoticed before, he knew the soul of each and saw clearly that they were all good. In particular, they all loved him, Levin, extraordinarily today. That could be seen from the way they talked to him, how kindly, lovingly even all the strangers looked at him.

“Well, are you pleased?” Sergei Ivanovich asked him.

“Very. I never thought it was so interesting! Splendid, wonderful!”

Sviyazhsky came up to Levin and invited him home to tea. Levin could not possibly understand or remember what it was he had been dissatisfied with in Sviyazhsky, what he had been seeking from him. He was a clever and wonderfully kind man.

“Oh, I should be very glad,” he said, and asked after his wife and sister-in-law. And by a strange association of thoughts, since in his imagination the thought of Sviyazhsky’s sister-in-law was connected with marriage, it seemed to him that there was no one better to tell his happiness to than Sviyazhsky’s wife and sister-in-law, and he was very glad to go to them.

Sviyazhsky asked him about his affairs in the country, as always not supposing any possibility of finding anything not already found in Europe, and now this was not in the least unpleasant to Levin. On the contrary, he felt that Sviyazhsky was right, that the whole business was insignificant, and he saw the wonderful gentleness and delicacy with which Sviyazhsky avoided pressing his rightness. Sviyazhsky’s ladies were especially sweet. It seemed to Levin that they already knew everything and sympathized with him, but said nothing only out of delicacy. He sat with them an hour, two, three, talking about various subjects, but meaning only what filled his soul, and did not notice that he was boring them terribly and that it was long past their bedtime. Sviyazhsky saw him out to the hall, yawning and wondering at the strange state his friend was in. It was past one o’clock. Levin went back to his hotel and was frightened at the thought of how he would now spend, alone with his impatience, the ten hours still left to him. The servant on night duty, who was not asleep, lit his candles and was about to go, but Levin stopped him. This servant, Yegor, whom Levin had not noticed before, turned out to be a very intelligent and good and, above all, kind man.

“Well, Yegor, is it hard not sleeping?”

“What can one do! That’s our job. In a gentleman’s house it’s quieter; but here there’s more to be made.”

It turned out that Yegor had a family, three boys and a daughter who was a seamstress, whom he wanted to marry to an assistant in a saddler’s shop.

Levin took this occasion to tell Yegor his thought that in marriage the main thing is love, and that with love one will always be happy, because happiness lies only in oneself.

Yegor listened attentively and evidently understood Levin’s thought completely, but in confirmation of it he made a remark unexpected by Levin: that when he had lived with good masters he had always been satisfied with his masters, and now he was quite satisfied with his employer, though he was a Frenchman.

“A wonderfully kind man,” thought Levin.

“Well, and you, Yegor, when you married, did you love your wife?”

“How could I not love her?” answered Yegor.

And Levin saw that Yegor too was in a rapturous state and was about to pour out all his heartfelt feelings.

“My life has been wonderful too. From a child I…” he began, his eyes shining, evidently infected by Levin’s rapture, as people are infected by yawning.

But at that moment a bell rang; Yegor went away, and Levin was left alone. He had eaten almost nothing at dinner, had refused tea and supper at the Sviyazhskys’, but he could not think of supper. He had not slept the night before, but he could not think of sleep either. The room was cool, but the heat was stifling him. He opened both little window-panes and sat down on the table facing them. Beyond the snow-covered roof could be seen a patterned cross with chains, and above it the rising triangle of the constellation of the Charioteer with the bright yellowish Capella. He looked now at the cross, now at the star, breathed in the fresh frosty air that ran evenly into the room, and, as if in a dream, followed the images and memories that rose up in his imagination. After three o’clock he heard steps in the corridor and looked out of the door. It was the gambler Myaskin, whom he knew, coming back from the club. He was walking gloomily, scowling and coughing. “Poor, unhappy man!” thought Levin, and tears came into his eyes from love and pity for this man. He wanted to talk to him, to comfort him; but, remembering that he was in his shirt, he thought better of it, and sat down again at the window to bathe in the cold air and to look at that wonderfully shaped cross, silent but full of meaning for him, and at the bright yellow star ascending. After six o’clock the floor-polishers began making a noise, bells rang for some service, and Levin felt that he was beginning to be cold. He closed the window, washed, dressed and went out into the street.

## Chapter XV

The streets were still empty. Levin went to the Shcherbatskys’ house. The front doors were locked, and everyone was asleep. He went back, came into his room again, and asked for coffee. The day servant, not Yegor now, brought it to him. Levin wanted to get into conversation with him, but someone rang for the servant and he went away. Levin tried to drink some coffee and put a roll into his mouth, but his mouth decidedly did not know what to do with the roll. Levin spat out the roll, put on his overcoat and went out walking again. It was past nine when he came to the Shcherbatskys’ porch for the second time. In the house they had only just got up, and the cook was going out for provisions. He had to live through at least two more hours.

All that night and morning Levin had lived completely unconsciously and felt himself completely withdrawn from the conditions of material life. He had eaten nothing all day, had not slept for two nights, had spent several hours undressed in the frost, and felt not only fresh and healthy as never before, but he felt completely independent of his body: he moved without any effort of his muscles and felt that he could do anything. He was sure that he could have flown up into the air or moved the corner of a house, if it had been necessary. He spent the remaining time walking about the streets, constantly looking at his watch and gazing about him.

And what he saw then he never saw again afterwards. The children going to school especially touched him, and the bluish-grey pigeons flying down from the roof onto the pavement, and the little loaves sprinkled with flour that an invisible hand had put out. These loaves, these pigeons and two little boys were unearthly beings. It all happened at the same time: a boy ran up to a pigeon and, smiling, glanced at Levin; the pigeon whirred its wings and fluttered away, shining in the sun among the specks of snow quivering in the air; and from a little window came the smell of baked bread, and the loaves were put out. All this together was so extraordinarily good that Levin laughed and cried for joy. After a long round by Gazetny Lane and Kislovka, he went back to the hotel again and, putting his watch in front of him, sat down to wait for twelve o’clock. In the next room they were talking about some machines and some swindle and coughing their morning cough. They did not understand that the hand was already nearing twelve. The hand reached it. Levin went out onto the steps. The cabmen evidently knew everything. With happy faces they surrounded Levin, arguing among themselves and offering their services. Trying not to offend the other cabmen, and promising to drive with them too some time, Levin took one and told him to drive to the Shcherbatskys’. The cabman was lovely in the white collar of his shirt, sticking out from under his coat and drawn tight round his full, red, strong neck. This cabman’s sledge was high and smart, such as Levin never drove in again afterwards, and the horse was a good one and tried to run, but did not move from the spot. The cabman knew the Shcherbatskys’ house and, rounding his arms with particular respect for his fare and saying “Whoa!”, drew up at the entrance. The Shcherbatskys’ hall porter surely knew everything. This could be seen from the smile in his eyes and from the way he said:

“Well, it’s a long time since you were here, Konstantin Dmitrich!”

Not only did he know everything, but he was evidently, as Levin saw, rejoicing and making efforts to hide his joy. Looking into his dear old eyes, Levin understood even something more that was new in his happiness.

“Are they up?”

“Please go in! Or leave it here,” he said, smiling, when Levin was about to turn back to take his hat. That meant something.

“Whom shall I announce you to, sir?” asked the footman.

The footman, though young and one of the new footmen, a dandy, was a very kind and good man, and he too understood everything.

“The princess… The prince… The young princess…” said Levin.

The first person he saw was Mademoiselle Linon. She was crossing the ballroom, and her ringlets and her face were beaming. He had only just begun to speak to her when suddenly steps were heard behind the door, the rustle of a dress, and Mademoiselle Linon vanished from Levin’s eyes, and the joyful terror of the nearness of his happiness came over him. Mademoiselle Linon hurried and, leaving him, went to another door. As soon as she had gone out, quick, quick, light steps sounded over the parquet, and his happiness, his life, himself, what was better than himself, what he had sought and longed for so long, was quickly, quickly coming nearer to him. She was not walking, but was borne towards him by some invisible force.

He saw only her clear, truthful eyes, frightened by the same joy of love that filled his heart too. These eyes shone nearer and nearer, dazzling him with their light of love. She stopped close beside him, touching him. Her hands rose and came down on his shoulders.

She had done all she could: she had run up to him and given herself entirely, shy and rejoicing. He put his arms round her and pressed his lips to her mouth, which was seeking his kiss.

She too had not slept all night, and had been waiting for him all morning. Her mother and father had consented without question and were happy in her happiness. She had been waiting for him. She had wanted to be the first to tell him of her happiness and his. She had been getting ready to meet him alone, and had rejoiced at the thought, and been shy, and ashamed, and had not known herself what she would do. She had heard his steps and voice and had waited behind the door until Mademoiselle Linon went away. Mademoiselle Linon went away. Without thinking, without asking herself how or what, she went up to him and did what she did.

“Let us go to mama!” she said, taking him by the hand. For a long time he could say nothing, not so much because he was afraid of spoiling the height of his feeling with a word, as because every time he wanted to say something, instead of words he felt that tears of happiness would burst out. He took her hands and kissed them.

“Can it really be true?” he said at last in a hollow voice. “I can’t believe that you love me!”

She smiled at his saying “thou” to her, and at the timidity with which he looked at her.

“Yes!” she said significantly, slowly. “I am so happy!”

Without letting go of his hand, she went into the drawing-room. The princess, seeing them, began breathing fast, and at once began to cry, and at once to laugh, and, with a more energetic step than Levin had expected, ran up to them, and, putting her arms round Levin’s head, kissed him and wet his cheeks with her tears.

“So it is all settled! I am glad. Love her. I am glad… Kitty!”

“That didn’t take long!” said the old prince, trying to be indifferent; but Levin noticed that his eyes were moist when he turned to him.

“I have long, always wished for this!” he said, taking Levin by the hand and drawing him towards himself. “Even back then, when this featherbrain took it into her head…”

“Papa!” cried Kitty, and covered his mouth with her hands.

“Well, I won’t!” he said. “I am very, very… gl… Ah! what a fool I am…”

He put his arms round Kitty, kissed her face, her hand, her face again, and made the sign of the cross over her.

And a new feeling of love for this man, the old prince, hitherto a stranger to him, came over Levin as he watched Kitty kiss his fleshy hand long and tenderly.

## Chapter XVI

The princess sat in her armchair in silence and smiled; the prince sat down beside her. Kitty stood by her father’s chair, still not letting go of his hand. Everyone was silent.

The princess was the first to put everything into words and turn all the thoughts and feelings into the questions of life. And at first this seemed equally strange and even painful to everyone.

“When is it to be? We must give our blessing and announce it. And when is the wedding? What do you think, Alexander?”

“Here he is,” said the old prince, pointing to Levin; “he is the principal person here.”

“When?” said Levin, blushing. “Tomorrow. If you are asking me, in my opinion, the blessing today and the wedding tomorrow.”

“Come now, mon cher, that’s nonsense!”

“Well, in a week.”

“He really is mad.”

“No, why?”

“Oh, mercy!” said the mother, smiling joyfully at this haste. “And the trousseau?”

“Is there really to be a trousseau and all that?” thought Levin with horror. “But then, can a trousseau, and a blessing, and all that, can it spoil my happiness? Nothing can spoil it!” He glanced at Kitty and noticed that she was not in the least, not in the least offended by the thought of a trousseau. “So it must be necessary,” he thought.

“You see, I know nothing; I only said what I wished,” he said, apologizing.

“Then we shall think it over. Now we can give our blessing and announce it. That’s right.”

The princess went up to her husband, kissed him and was about to go; but he held her back, embraced her and, tenderly, like a young lover, smiling, kissed her several times. The old people were evidently muddled for a moment and did not quite know whether it was they who were in love again, or only their daughter. When the prince and princess had gone out, Levin went up to his bride and took her hand. He had mastered himself now and could speak, and he had a great deal to say to her. But he said something quite different from what he needed to say.

“How I knew it would be so! I never hoped; but in my heart I was always sure,” he said. “I believe it was predestined.”

“And I?” she said. “Even then…” She stopped and then went on, looking at him resolutely with her truthful eyes: “even then, when I pushed my happiness away from me. I always loved you alone, but I was carried away. I must tell you… Can you forget it?”

“Perhaps it was for the best. You will have to forgive me a great deal. I must tell you…”

This was one of the things he had decided to tell her. He had decided to tell her two things in the very first days: that he was not as pure as she, and the other, that he was an unbeliever. It was tormenting, but he considered that he must tell her both.

“No, not now; later!” he said.

“Very well, later; but be sure to tell me. I am not afraid of anything. I need to know everything. Now it is settled.”

He finished it for her:

“Settled that you will take me, whatever I may be, that you will not give me up? Yes?”

“Yes, yes.”

Their conversation was interrupted by Mademoiselle Linon, who, with a smile that was feigned but tender, came to congratulate her favourite pupil. Before she had gone, the servants came with their congratulations. Then relations arrived, and there began that blissful muddle from which Levin did not emerge until the day after his wedding. Levin felt constantly awkward and bored, but the tension of his happiness kept growing. He constantly felt that a great deal was required of him that he did not know, and he did everything he was told, and all of it gave him happiness. He had thought that his engagement would have nothing in common with others, that the usual conditions of an engagement would spoil his special happiness; but it ended with his doing the same as others, and his happiness only grew from it and became more and more special, such as had never had and has never had anything like it.

“Now we shall eat some sweets,” Mlle Linon would say, and Levin would drive off to buy sweets.

“Well, I am very glad,” said Sviyazhsky. “I advise you to get the bouquets at Fomin’s.”

“Must I?” And he drove to Fomin’s.

His brother told him that he must borrow some money, because there would be a great many expenses, presents…

“Must there be presents?” And he galloped off to Fulde’s.

And at the confectioner’s, and at Fomin’s, and at Fulde’s he saw that he was expected, that they were glad to see him and celebrated his happiness just like everyone he had to do with in those days. What was extraordinary was that everyone not only loved him, but even people who had been unsympathetic before, cold, indifferent, were delighted with him, gave way to him in everything, treated his feeling tenderly and delicately, and shared his conviction that he was the happiest man in the world, because his bride was the height of perfection. Kitty felt the same. When Countess Nordston allowed herself to hint that she had wished for something better, Kitty grew so heated and proved so convincingly that there could be nothing in the world better than Levin that Countess Nordston had to admit it, and in Kitty’s presence never again met Levin without a smile of admiration.

The explanation he had promised was the one painful event of that time. He consulted the old prince and, having got his permission, gave Kitty his diary, in which was written what tormented him. He had written this diary with a future bride in view. Two things tormented him: his lack of innocence and his unbelief. The confession of unbelief passed unnoticed. She was religious, had never doubted the truths of religion, but his outward unbelief did not touch her in the least. Through love she knew all his soul, and in his soul she saw what she wanted; and that such a state of soul was called being an unbeliever was all the same to her. The other confession made her weep bitterly.

Not without an inner struggle had Levin given her his diary. He knew that between him and her there could and should be no secrets, and so he had decided that this was how it must be; but he had not given himself an account of how it might affect her; he had not put himself in her place. Only when that evening he came to them before the theatre, went into her room and saw her poor, sweet face, tear-stained and unhappy from the irreparable grief he had caused, did he understand the gulf that separated his shameful past from her dove-like purity, and he was horrified at what he had done.

“Take them, take these dreadful books!” she said, pushing away the notebooks that lay before her on the table. “Why did you give them to me!… No, it is better after all,” she added, taking pity on his despairing face. “But it is terrible, terrible!”

He lowered his head and was silent. He could say nothing.

“You will not forgive me,” he whispered.

“Yes, I have forgiven you; but it is terrible!”

His happiness, however, was so great that this confession did not break it, but only gave it a new shade. She forgave him; but from then on he considered himself still more unworthy of her, bowed still lower before her morally, and valued his undeserved happiness still more highly.
