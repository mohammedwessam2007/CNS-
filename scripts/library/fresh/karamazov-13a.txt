## Book Five, Chapter VI · Still a Very Obscure One

And Ivan Fyodorovich, having parted from Alyosha, went home, to Fyodor Pavlovich’s house. But, strange to say, an unbearable anguish suddenly came over him, and, what was more, it grew with every step as he came nearer the house. The strangeness was not in the anguish but in the fact that Ivan Fyodorovich could not for the life of him make out what the anguish consisted of. He had often felt anguish before, and it would have been no wonder if it had come at such a moment, when tomorrow, breaking suddenly with everything that had drawn him here, he was getting ready to turn sharply aside once more and set out on a new, completely unknown road, and again quite alone, as before, hoping for much but not knowing what for, expecting much, far too much, from life, but unable himself to define anything either in his expectations or even in his desires. And yet at this moment, though the anguish of the new and unknown really was in his soul, it was not that at all that tormented him. “Is it disgust for my father’s house?” he thought to himself. “It looks like it, I am so sick of it; and though today I shall cross that vile threshold for the last time, it is disgusting all the same…” But no, it was not that either. Was it the parting with Alyosha and the conversation he had had with him? “For so many years I kept silent with the whole world and would not deign to speak, and suddenly I have reeled off such a heap of nonsense.” Indeed, it might have been the youthful vexation of youthful inexperience and youthful vanity, vexation at not having managed to say what he meant, and with such a being as Alyosha, too, on whom his heart undoubtedly had great designs. Of course that was there too, that vexation; it was bound to be there; but that was not it either, none of it was it. “Anguish to the point of nausea, and I can’t say what I want. Perhaps not think…”

Ivan Fyodorovich tried “not thinking”, but that did not help either. The chief thing that made the anguish vexing and irritating was that it had some accidental, entirely external character: he could feel that. Some being or object stood and stuck up somewhere, the way something sometimes sticks up before your eye, and for a long while, busy with work or in heated talk, you don’t notice it, and yet you are visibly irritated, almost tormented, and at last it occurs to you to remove the offending object, often something very trivial and absurd, some thing left out of its place, a handkerchief fallen on the floor, a book not put back in the bookcase, and so on, and so on. At last, in the foulest and most irritated state of mind, Ivan Fyodorovich reached his father’s house, and suddenly, about fifteen paces from the gate, glancing at it, he guessed at once what had been tormenting and disturbing him so.

On the bench at the gate sat the servant Smerdyakov, enjoying the cool of the evening, and Ivan Fyodorovich understood at the first glance at him that the servant Smerdyakov was sitting in his soul too, and that it was precisely this man his soul could not bear. Everything was suddenly lit up and became clear. Earlier, when Alyosha had told him of his meeting with Smerdyakov, something gloomy and repellent had suddenly pierced his heart and at once roused an answering spite in him. Then, in the course of the conversation, Smerdyakov had been forgotten for a while, yet he had stayed in his soul, and no sooner had Ivan Fyodorovich parted from Alyosha and set off alone for the house than the forgotten feeling quickly began to come to the surface again. “Can this worthless scoundrel really trouble me to such a degree?” he thought with unbearable spite.

The fact was that Ivan Fyodorovich had indeed taken a great dislike to this man of late, and especially in the last few days. He had even begun to notice in himself this growing, almost hatred for the creature. Perhaps the hatred had grown so sharp precisely because at first, when Ivan Fyodorovich had only just come to us, something quite different had happened. At that time Ivan Fyodorovich had suddenly taken a kind of special interest in Smerdyakov and even found him very original. He had himself encouraged him to talk to him, always wondering, however, at a certain muddle-headedness, or rather a certain restlessness of his mind, and not understanding what it was that could so constantly and persistently trouble “this contemplative”. They talked about philosophical questions too, and even about why there was light on the first day when the sun, moon and stars were made only on the fourth, and how one ought to understand that. But Ivan Fyodorovich soon became convinced that the point was not the sun, moon and stars at all; that the sun, moon and stars, though a curious subject, were for Smerdyakov entirely third-rate, and that he wanted something quite different. One way or another, at any rate, a boundless vanity began to show and betray itself, a wounded vanity at that. Ivan Fyodorovich did not like it at all. That was where his aversion began. Later, troubles started in the house: Grushenka appeared, the stories with his brother Dmitri began, there was trouble all round. They talked about that too, but though Smerdyakov always spoke of it with great agitation, again it was impossible to get out of him what he himself wanted in it all. One could even wonder at the illogicality and disorder of some of his wishes, which came out in spite of him and were always equally obscure. Smerdyakov kept asking questions, putting some indirect, obviously contrived questions, but what for he did not explain, and usually at the hottest moment of his own questioning he would suddenly fall silent or pass to something quite different. But the main thing, which finally irritated Ivan Fyodorovich beyond bearing and filled him with such aversion, was a sort of disgusting and peculiar familiarity that Smerdyakov had begun to show him more and more, the further it went. Not that he allowed himself to be impolite; on the contrary, he always spoke extremely respectfully; but things had somehow come about so that Smerdyakov evidently began to consider himself, God knows why, at one with Ivan Fyodorovich in something, and always spoke in a tone as if there were already something agreed between the two of them, something secret, something once said on both sides and known only to the two of them, and not even comprehensible to the other mortals bustling about them. For a long time, however, Ivan Fyodorovich did not understand this real cause of his growing aversion either, and only very lately had he managed to guess what it was. With a squeamish, irritable feeling he wanted to go through the gate now in silence, without looking at Smerdyakov; but Smerdyakov rose from the bench, and from that gesture alone Ivan Fyodorovich guessed in an instant that he wished to have a special conversation with him. Ivan Fyodorovich looked at him and stopped, and the fact that he had stopped like that, and not walked past as he had wanted to a minute before, enraged him till he shook. With anger and disgust he looked at Smerdyakov’s gelded, sunken face, with the hair at the temples combed forward and a little tuft whipped up on top. His left eye, slightly screwed up, winked and grinned, as if to say: “Why are you going? You won’t get by; you see that we two clever men have something to talk over.” Ivan Fyodorovich began to tremble.

“Away, you scoundrel, what company am I for you, you fool!” was on the tip of his tongue, but, to his great surprise, something quite different came off it:

“Is my father asleep or awake?” he said quietly and meekly, unexpectedly to himself, and suddenly, also quite unexpectedly, sat down on the bench. For a moment he felt almost frightened; he remembered that afterwards. Smerdyakov stood facing him, his hands behind his back, and looked at him with assurance, almost sternly.

“Still resting, sir,” he said unhurriedly. (“You spoke first, as it were, not me.”) “I wonder at you, sir,” he added after a pause, lowering his eyes somehow affectedly, putting his right foot forward and playing with the toe of his patent-leather boot.

“Why do you wonder at me?” Ivan Fyodorovich said curtly and sternly, restraining himself with all his might, and suddenly understood with disgust that he felt the most intense curiosity and would not leave here on any account until he had satisfied it.

“Why don’t you go to Chermashnya, sir?” Smerdyakov suddenly raised his eyes and smiled familiarly. “And why I smiled you must understand for yourself, if you are a clever man,” his screwed-up left eye seemed to say.

“Why should I go to Chermashnya?” Ivan Fyodorovich was surprised.

Smerdyakov was silent again.

“Fyodor Pavlovich himself begged you to, sir,” he said at last, without hurry and as if he himself did not value his answer: I am getting off with a third-rate reason, he seemed to say, just to say something.

“Oh, damn it, speak plainly, what do you want?” Ivan Fyodorovich cried out at last angrily, passing from meekness to rudeness.

Smerdyakov drew his right foot back to his left, drew himself up straighter, but went on looking with the same calm and the same little smile.

“Nothing of any substance, sir… just by way of conversation…”

Silence again. They were silent for almost a minute. Ivan Fyodorovich knew that he ought to get up at once and be angry, and Smerdyakov stood before him as if waiting: “Now I’ll see whether you get angry or not.” So at least it seemed to Ivan Fyodorovich. At last he swayed forward to get up. Smerdyakov seemed to catch the moment exactly.

“My position is terrible, Ivan Fyodorovich, sir; I don’t even know how to help myself,” he said suddenly, firmly and distinctly, and sighed with his last word. Ivan Fyodorovich at once sat down again.

“Both of them have quite gone out of their minds, sir, both have come down to the merest childishness, sir,” Smerdyakov went on. “I mean your father, sir, and your brother Dmitri Fyodorovich. Now Fyodor Pavlovich will get up and start pestering me every minute: ‘Why hasn’t she come? Why hasn’t she come?’, and so on right up till midnight, and past midnight too. And if Agrafena Alexandrovna doesn’t come (because maybe she doesn’t mean ever to come at all, sir), then he will go for me again tomorrow morning: ‘Why didn’t she come? Why didn’t she come, when will she come?’, as if I were to blame for it somehow before him. On the other hand there’s this, sir: as soon as it gets dark, and earlier too, your brother turns up next door with a weapon in his hands: ‘Look out, you rogue, you soup-maker: if you miss her and don’t let me know she has come, I’ll kill you before anyone.’ The night passes, and in the morning he too, like Fyodor Pavlovich, starts tormenting me cruelly: ‘Why didn’t she come, will she show herself soon?’, and again I am to blame before him, sir, for his lady not coming. And every day and every hour they both get angrier, sir, so that sometimes I think I shall take my own life from fright, sir. I can’t rely on them, sir.”

“Then why did you get mixed up in it? Why did you start carrying tales to Dmitri Fyodorovich?” Ivan Fyodorovich said irritably.

“And how could I not get mixed up in it, sir? And I didn’t get mixed up in it at all, if you want to know with complete exactness, sir. From the very beginning I kept silent, not daring to object, and he himself appointed me to be his servant Lichard. Since then he only knows one word: ‘I’ll kill you, you rogue, if you let her slip past!’ I am quite certain, sir, that tomorrow I shall have a long fit of the falling sickness.”

“What do you mean, a long fit?”

“A long fit, sir, extremely long, sir. It goes on for several hours, sir, or maybe a day or two. Once it went on with me for about three days; I fell from the attic that time. It stops shaking you, and then it starts again; and for all three days I couldn’t come to my senses. Fyodor Pavlovich sent for Herzenstube, the doctor here, that time, and he put ice on the crown of my head and used another remedy besides… I might have died, sir.”

“But they say you can’t know beforehand that a fit of the falling sickness will come at such and such an hour. How can you say it will come tomorrow?” Ivan Fyodorovich asked with a peculiar, irritable curiosity.

“That’s true, sir, you can’t know beforehand.”

“Besides, you fell from the attic that time.”

“I climb into the attic every day, sir; I can fall from the attic tomorrow too. And if not from the attic, I’ll fall into the cellar, sir; I go down into the cellar every day too, sir, on my own business, sir.”

Ivan Fyodorovich gave him a long look.

“You are spinning something, I see, and I don’t quite understand you,” he said quietly, but somehow menacingly. “Do you mean to pretend tomorrow that you are in a fit for three days? Eh?”

Smerdyakov, who had been looking at the ground and again playing with the toe of his right foot, put his right foot back in its place, put his left forward instead, raised his head and, with a smirk, said:

“Even if I could do that very thing, sir, that is, pretend, sir, and since it is not at all hard for an experienced man to do it, then even so I would be fully within my rights to use the means to save my life from death; for while I am lying ill, even if Agrafena Alexandrovna came to his father, he couldn’t ask a sick man: ‘Why didn’t you tell me?’ He would be ashamed himself.”

“Oh, damn it!” Ivan Fyodorovich flared up suddenly, his face twisted with spite. “Why are you always so frightened for your life? All these threats of my brother Dmitri’s are only hot words and nothing more. He won’t kill you; he’ll kill, but not you!”

“He’ll kill me like a fly, sir, and me first of all, sir. And what I fear even more is that I’ll be counted his accomplice when he does something mad to his father.”

“Why would they count you an accomplice?”

“They’ll count me an accomplice because I told him those very signals in great secrecy, sir.”

“What signals? Told whom? Damn you, speak more plainly!”

“I must confess completely,” Smerdyakov drawled with pedantic calm, “that there is a secret here between me and Fyodor Pavlovich. As you are pleased to know yourself (if only you are pleased to know it), for several days now, as soon as night comes, or even evening, he locks himself in from inside at once. Lately you have taken to going back upstairs early every time, and yesterday you didn’t go out anywhere at all, sir, and so perhaps you don’t know how carefully he has begun locking himself in for the night. And even if Grigory Vasilyevich himself came, he would only open to him once he was sure of his voice, sir. But Grigory Vasilyevich doesn’t come, sir, because now I alone wait on him in the rooms, sir; he arranged it so himself from the very moment he started this business with Agrafena Alexandrovna. And for the night I too now go away, by his order, and sleep in the lodge, on condition that I don’t sleep till midnight but keep watch, get up and go round the yard, and wait for Agrafena Alexandrovna to come, sir, since for several days now he has been waiting for her like a madman. This is how he reasons, sir: she is afraid of him, he says, of Dmitri Fyodorovich (he calls him Mitka, sir), and so she will come to me late at night by the back way; and you, he says, watch for her till midnight and later. And if she comes, you run up to the door and knock on my door, or on the window from the garden, with your hand: the first two times softly, like this, one-two, and then at once three times quicker, knock-knock-knock. Then, he says, I shall understand at once that it is she who has come, and I shall open the door to you quietly. Another signal he told me in case something urgent happens: first two quick knocks, knock-knock, and then, after waiting, one more, much harder. Then he will understand that something sudden has happened and that I very much need to see him, and he will open to me too, and I will go in and report. All in case Agrafena Alexandrovna may not come herself but send some message; besides that, Dmitri Fyodorovich may come too, so I am to let him know that he is near. He is very much afraid of Dmitri Fyodorovich, so that even if Agrafena Alexandrovna had already come and they had locked themselves in together, and Dmitri Fyodorovich turned up somewhere near in the meantime, then too I am bound to report it to him at once by knocking three times; so that the first signal, of five knocks, means ‘Agrafena Alexandrovna has come’, and the second signal, of three knocks, means ‘very much needed’. He taught me himself several times by example and explained it. And since in the whole universe only he and I know about these signals, sir, he will open without the least doubt and without calling out (he is very much afraid to call out aloud). And now those very signals have become known to Dmitri Fyodorovich.”

“How are they known? Did you pass them on? How did you dare pass them on?”

“From that very fear, sir. And how could I have dared keep silent before him, sir? Dmitri Fyodorovich kept pressing me every day: ‘You are deceiving me, you are hiding something from me! I’ll break both your legs!’ So I told him those very secret signals, so that at least he would see my servility and so be sure that I am not deceiving him but reporting everything to him in every way.”

“If you think he will use those signals and try to get in, then don’t let him in.”

“And when I am lying in a fit myself, sir, how will I not let him in, sir, even if I could dare not to let him in, sir, knowing how desperate he is, sir?”

“Oh, damn it! Why are you so sure the falling sickness will come, damn you? Are you laughing at me or not?”

“How could I dare laugh at you, and is this any time for laughing, with such fear? I have a feeling there will be a fit; I have such a presentiment; it will come from fright alone, sir.”

“Oh, damn! If you are lying ill, then Grigory will keep watch. Warn Grigory beforehand; he won’t let him in.”

“I wouldn’t dare tell Grigory Vasilyevich about the signals on any account without the master’s orders, sir. And as for Grigory Vasilyevich hearing him and not letting him in, he has just fallen ill today since yesterday, and Marfa Ignatyevna means to treat him tomorrow. They agreed on it earlier. And this treatment of theirs is very curious, sir: Marfa Ignatyevna knows a certain infusion and always keeps it, a strong one, on some herb; she has a secret, sir. And she treats Grigory Vasilyevich with this secret medicine about three times a year, sir, when his lower back gives out completely, as if he had a paralysis, sir, about three times a year, sir. Then she takes a towel, sir, soaks it in this infusion, and Marfa Ignatyevna rubs his whole back for half an hour, sir, till it is dry, sir; it even goes quite red and swollen, sir; and then what is left in the bottle she gives him to drink, sir, with a certain prayer, sir; not all of it, though, because on this rare occasion she keeps a small part for herself, sir, and drinks it too. And both of them, I tell you, being non-drinkers, drop down on the spot, sir, and sleep soundly for a very long time, sir; and when Grigory Vasilyevich wakes up he is nearly always well after it, sir, and when Marfa Ignatyevna wakes up she always has a headache after it, sir. So if tomorrow Marfa Ignatyevna carries out her intention, sir, they are hardly likely to hear anything, sir, or to keep Dmitri Fyodorovich out, sir. They will be asleep, sir.”

“What nonsense! And all this coincides at once as if on purpose: you have a fit, and those two are senseless!” Ivan Fyodorovich shouted. “Aren’t you yourself trying to arrange it so that it coincides?” broke from him suddenly, and he knitted his brows menacingly.

“How could I arrange it so, sir… and why arrange it, when everything here depends on Dmitri Fyodorovich alone, sir, and on his thoughts alone, sir… If he wants to do something, he will do it, sir; and if not, I am not going to bring him on purpose to push him in on his father.”

“And why should he go to his father’s, and secretly at that, if, as you say yourself, Agrafena Alexandrovna won’t come at all?” Ivan Fyodorovich went on, turning pale with spite. “You say so yourself, and all the time I have been living here I have been sure that the old man is only imagining things and that the creature will not come to him. Why then should Dmitri burst in on the old man if she doesn’t come? Speak! I want to know your thoughts.”

“You know yourself why he will come; what do my thoughts matter? He will come out of sheer spite, or out of his suspiciousness, if I happen to be ill, say; he will have his doubts and go impatiently to search the rooms, as he did yesterday: hasn’t she slipped in to him somehow quietly? He also knows perfectly well that Fyodor Pavlovich has a big envelope ready, with three thousand sealed in it, under three seals, sir, tied with a ribbon and inscribed in his own hand: ‘To my angel Grushenka, if she will come’, and then, three days later, he added: ‘and to my little chick’. So that’s what is doubtful, sir.”

“Rubbish!” Ivan Fyodorovich shouted, almost in a frenzy. “Dmitri won’t go to rob money, and kill his father in the bargain. He might have killed him yesterday over Grushenka, like a frantic, spiteful fool, but he won’t go robbing!”

“He needs money very badly now, sir, needs it to the last extremity, Ivan Fyodorovich. You don’t even know how badly,” Smerdyakov explained extremely calmly and with remarkable distinctness. “Besides, he considers those very three thousand, sir, as good as his own, and explained it to me so himself: ‘My father,’ he says, ‘still owes me exactly three thousand.’ And on top of all that, consider some plain truth too, Ivan Fyodorovich, sir: it is almost certainly so, I must say, sir, that Agrafena Alexandrovna, if only she wants to, will certainly make him marry her, the master himself, that is, Fyodor Pavlovich, sir, if only she wants to; and maybe she will want to, sir. I only say that she won’t come, but maybe she will want even more than that, sir, that is, to become a lady outright. I know myself that her merchant Samsonov told her quite frankly that it would be no stupid thing to do, and laughed as he said it. And she is no fool herself, sir. She won’t go and marry a beggar like Dmitri Fyodorovich, sir. So, taking that, consider for yourself, Ivan Fyodorovich, that then neither Dmitri Fyodorovich, nor even you, sir, nor your brother Alexei Fyodorovich would get anything at all after your father’s death, not a rouble, sir, because Agrafena Alexandrovna would marry him precisely to have everything made over to her and to transfer whatever capital there is to herself, sir. But if your father died now, while there is nothing of that yet, sir, each of you would get a sure forty thousand at once, sir, even Dmitri Fyodorovich, whom he hates so, sir, since he has not made a will, sir… Dmitri Fyodorovich knows all this perfectly well…”

Something seemed to twist and quiver in Ivan Fyodorovich’s face. He suddenly flushed.

“Then why,” he suddenly interrupted Smerdyakov, “after all this, do you advise me to go to Chermashnya? What did you mean by it? I go away, and this is what will happen here.” Ivan Fyodorovich was breathing with difficulty.

“Quite right, sir,” Smerdyakov said quietly and reasonably, watching Ivan Fyodorovich intently all the same.

“What do you mean, quite right?” Ivan Fyodorovich asked again, restraining himself with an effort, his eyes flashing menacingly.

“I spoke out of pity for you. In your place, if it were me, I’d throw it all up on the spot… rather than sit by such a business, sir,” answered Smerdyakov, looking with the most open air at Ivan Fyodorovich’s flashing eyes. Both were silent.

“You seem to be a great idiot and, of course… a terrible scoundrel!” Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly got up from the bench. Then he wanted to go straight through the gate, but suddenly stopped and turned to Smerdyakov. Something strange happened: Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly, as if in a spasm, bit his lip, clenched his fists, and in another instant, of course, would have thrown himself on Smerdyakov. Smerdyakov at least noticed it at that same instant, started, and jerked his whole body back. But the instant passed safely for Smerdyakov, and Ivan Fyodorovich, in silence but as if in some bewilderment, turned in at the gate.

“I am leaving for Moscow tomorrow, if you want to know: early tomorrow morning, and that’s all!” he suddenly said spitefully, distinctly and loudly, wondering at himself afterwards why he had needed to say it to Smerdyakov.

“That is the very best thing, sir,” Smerdyakov caught it up, as if he had been waiting for it, “except perhaps that they may trouble you from here by telegraph in Moscow, sir, in some such event, sir.”

Ivan Fyodorovich stopped again and again turned quickly to Smerdyakov. But something seemed to have happened to Smerdyakov too. All his familiarity and nonchalance had dropped away in an instant; his whole face expressed extraordinary attention and expectation, but timid and fawning now: “Won’t you say something more, won’t you add anything?”, so one could read in his intent gaze, fastened on Ivan Fyodorovich.

“And wouldn’t they send for me from Chermashnya too… in some such event?” Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly yelled, raising his voice terribly for no known reason.

“From Chermashnya too, sir… they would trouble you, sir…” Smerdyakov muttered almost in a whisper, as if at a loss, but still looking intently, intently, straight into Ivan Fyodorovich’s eyes.

“Only Moscow is further and Chermashnya nearer; so is it the fare you are sorry about, insisting on Chermashnya, or are you sorry for me, that I should go a long way round?”

“Quite right, sir…” Smerdyakov muttered in a voice that now broke, smiling vilely and again making himself convulsively ready to jump back in time. But Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly, to Smerdyakov’s surprise, laughed and went quickly through the gate, still laughing. Anyone who had looked at his face would surely have concluded that he did not laugh at all because he felt so merry. And he himself could never have explained what was happening to him at that moment. He moved and walked as if in a spasm.
