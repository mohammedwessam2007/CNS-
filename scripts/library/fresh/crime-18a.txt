## CHAPTER II

“These cigarettes!” Porfiry Petrovich began at last, having finished lighting up and caught his breath. “Harm, pure harm, and I can’t give them up! I cough, sir, my throat has begun to tickle, and I get short of breath. I am a coward, you know, sir; I went the other day to B—n; he examines each patient for half an hour at least; he even laughed, looking at me: he tapped me and listened to me; ‘Tobacco,’ he says, ‘among other things, is not good for you; your lungs are enlarged.’ Well, and how am I to give it up? What am I to replace it with? I don’t drink, sir, that’s the whole trouble, he-he-he, that I don’t drink, that’s the trouble! Everything is relative, Rodion Romanych, everything is relative!”

“What, is he taking up his old official tricks again?” Raskolnikov thought with disgust. The whole recent scene of their last meeting suddenly came back to him, and the feeling of that time flooded back to his heart like a wave.

“And I came to see you two days ago, in the evening; didn’t you know?” Porfiry Petrovich went on, looking round the room. “I came into the room, this very one. Just like today, I was passing by: why not, I thought, return his visit. I came in, and the room was wide open; I looked round, waited, and didn’t even announce myself to your servant; I left. You don’t lock up?”

Raskolnikov’s face grew darker and darker. Porfiry seemed to have guessed his thoughts.

“I have come to explain myself, my dear Rodion Romanych, to explain myself, sir! I owe you an explanation, sir, I am bound to give one,” he went on with a little smile, and even gave Raskolnikov’s knee a light slap with his palm; but almost at the same instant his face suddenly took on a serious and concerned expression; it even seemed clouded with sadness, to Raskolnikov’s surprise. He had never yet seen or suspected such a face in him. “A strange scene took place between us last time, Rodion Romanych. Perhaps a strange scene took place between us at our first meeting too; but then... Well, now it all comes to the same thing! Here is the point, sir: I may have turned out very guilty before you; I feel it, sir. Remember how we parted: your nerves were singing and your knees were shaking, and my nerves were singing and my knees were shaking. And you know, somehow it even turned out improper between us then, not gentlemanly. And yet we are gentlemen, after all; that is, in any case, gentlemen first of all; that must be understood, sir. Remember what it came to... it was quite even indecent, sir.”

“What is he doing, what does he take me for?” Raskolnikov asked himself in amazement, raising his head and staring with all his eyes at Porfiry.

“I have decided that it will be better for us to act frankly now,” Porfiry Petrovich went on, tilting his head back a little and lowering his eyes, as if no longer wishing to embarrass his former victim with his look and as if disdaining his former methods and dodges. “Yes, sir, such suspicions and such scenes cannot go on for long. Mikolka resolved things for us then, or I don’t know what it would have come to between us. That damned little tradesman was sitting behind my partition then: can you imagine it? You know that already, of course; and I know myself that he came to see you afterwards; but what you supposed then did not happen: I had not sent for anyone, and I had not yet given any orders about anything. You will ask why I had not? Well, how shall I put it: it had all as it were stunned me myself then. I barely managed to give orders to send for the caretakers. (You noticed the caretakers as you passed, I dare say.) A thought flashed through me then, just one, quickly, like lightning; I was very firmly convinced then, you see, Rodion Romanych. Well, I thought, even if I let one thing slip for a while, I will catch the other by the tail; my own, my own at least, I won’t let slip. You are very irritable by nature, Rodion Romanych, sir; even too much so, sir, with all the other fundamental qualities of your character and heart, which I flatter myself I have partly grasped, sir. Well, of course, even then I could reason that it does not always happen that a man just gets up and blurts out the whole inside story to you. That does happen, especially when you drive a man past the last of his patience, but in any case rarely. That much I could reason too. No, I thought, if only I had one little feature! if only the tiniest little feature, only one, but only such that one could take hold of it with one’s hands, that it would be a thing, and not just this psychology. Because, I thought, if a man is guilty, then of course one can, in any case, wait for something substantial from him; one may even count on the most unexpected result. I was counting on your character then, Rodion Romanych, most of all on your character, sir! I had very great hopes of you then.”

“But you... but why are you talking like this now?” Raskolnikov muttered at last, without even taking in the question properly. “What is he talking about?” he wondered to himself. “Can it be that he really takes me for innocent?”

“Why am I talking like this? I have come to explain myself, sir; I consider it, so to speak, a sacred duty. I want to lay everything out for you to the very ground, how it all was, the whole story of all that, so to speak, darkening of that time. I have made you suffer a great deal, Rodion Romanych. I am not a monster, sir. Why, I understand too what it is for a man to drag all this on himself, a man who is downcast, but proud, masterful and impatient, especially impatient! I regard you, in any case, as a most noble man, sir, and even with the beginnings of magnanimity, sir, though I don’t agree with you in all your convictions, which I consider it my duty to declare beforehand, directly and with complete sincerity, for above all I don’t wish to deceive. Having come to know you, I felt an attachment to you. Perhaps you will laugh at such words of mine? You have the right, sir. I know that you didn’t like me from the first glance, because, essentially, there is nothing to like, sir. But think as you please; now I wish, for my part, by every means to smooth over the impression I produced and to prove that I too am a man with a heart and a conscience. I speak sincerely, sir.”

Porfiry Petrovich paused with dignity. Raskolnikov felt a rush of some new fear. The thought that Porfiry considered him innocent suddenly began to frighten him.

“To tell everything in order, how it suddenly began then, is hardly necessary,” Porfiry Petrovich went on; “I think it is even superfluous. And I could hardly do it, sir. Because how is one to explain it in detail? At first rumours started. What rumours they were, and from whom, and when... and on what occasion, properly speaking, it came to you, that too, I think, is superfluous. With me personally it began with a chance, a completely chance chance, which in the highest degree might have happened and might not have happened. What chance? Hm, I think there is no need to say that either. All this, the rumours and the chances, came together for me then into one thought. I confess frankly, because if one is going to confess, then confess everything: it was I who first fell upon you then. Those marks of the old woman’s on the things, let us say, and so on and so on, all that is nonsense, sir. One can count up a hundred such things. I also had occasion then to find out in detail about the scene in the police office, also by chance, sir, and not just in passing, but from a special narrator, a first-class one, who, without knowing it himself, had mastered that scene wonderfully. It all fits together, sir, it all fits together, Rodion Romanych, my dear! Well, how could I not turn in a certain direction? From a hundred rabbits you will never make a horse, from a hundred suspicions you will never make a proof, as a certain English proverb says, but that is only prudence, sir; but just try to cope with the passions, the passions, because an investigator is a man too, sir. Then I remembered your little article too, in that little journal; remember, we talked about it in detail at your very first visit. I scoffed at it then, but that was in order to draw you out further. I repeat, you are very impatient and very ill, Rodion Romanych. That you are bold, arrogant, serious and... that you have felt, felt a great deal, all that I have known for a long time, sir. I am familiar with all these sensations, and I read your little article as something familiar. It was conceived in sleepless nights and in frenzy, with the heart rising and pounding, with suppressed enthusiasm. And this suppressed, proud enthusiasm in young people is dangerous! I scoffed then, but now I will tell you that I terribly love in general, as an amateur, that is, this first, youthful, ardent trial of the pen. Smoke, mist, a string ringing in the mist. Your article is absurd and fantastic, but such sincerity flashes in it, there is youthful and incorruptible pride in it, the boldness of despair; it is a gloomy article, sir, but that is good, sir. I read your little article and put it aside, and... as I put it aside then, I thought: ‘Well, with this man it won’t pass off like that!’ Well, then, tell me now, after such antecedents, how could I not be carried away by what followed! Ah, Lord! But am I saying anything? Am I asserting anything now? I only noted it then. What is there in it, I thought? There is nothing in it, that is, absolutely nothing, and perhaps in the highest degree nothing. And for me, an investigator, to be carried away like that is not even proper at all: I have Mikolka on my hands, and with facts already; say what you like, but facts! And he too brings in his psychology; one must occupy oneself with them, because this is a matter of life and death. Why am I explaining all this to you now? So that you should know, and with your mind and heart not blame me for my spiteful conduct then. Not spiteful, sir, I speak sincerely, sir, he-he! What do you think: that I didn’t come to search your place then? I did, sir, I did, he-he, I did, sir, when you were lying ill here in your little bed. Not officially and not in my own person, but I did, sir. Everything in your lodging was examined down to the last hair, even while the traces were fresh; but, umsonst, in vain! I thought: now this man will come, he will come himself, and very soon; if he is guilty, he will certainly come. Another would not come, but this one will. And do you remember how Mr Razumikhin began letting things slip to you? We arranged that so as to excite you; that is why we deliberately started the rumour, so that he would let things slip to you, and Mr Razumikhin is the kind of man who cannot hold in his indignation. What struck Mr Zamyotov above all was your anger and your open boldness: well, think of it, in a tavern suddenly to blurt out: ‘I killed her!’ Too bold, sir, too insolent, sir, and if, I thought, he is guilty, then he is a terrible fighter! That is what I thought then, sir. I waited, sir! I waited for you with all my might, and as for Zamyotov, you simply crushed him then and... why, that is just the point, that all this damned psychology cuts both ways! Well, so I wait for you, I watch, and God sends you: here you come! My heart simply thumped. Eh! Well, why did you have to come then? Your laughter, your laughter, as you came in then, remember, why, I guessed everything then as if through glass, and if I had not been waiting for you in such a special way, I would have noticed nothing in your laughter either. That is what it means to be in the mood. And Mr Razumikhin then: ah! the stone, the stone, remember, the stone, the one under which the things are hidden? Why, it is as if I see it somewhere there, in a kitchen garden: it was a kitchen garden you spoke of, wasn’t it, to Zamyotov, and then at my place, the second time? And when we began going over that article of yours then, when you began to set it out, why, one took every word of yours twice over, as if something else were sitting under it! Well, Rodion Romanych, that is how I came to the last posts, and struck my forehead against them, and came to my senses. No, I said, what am I doing! Why, if one wants to, all this, I said, can be explained in the other direction down to the last detail, and it will come out even more naturally. Torment, sir! ‘No,’ I thought, ‘I had better have one little feature!..’ And when I heard about those bells then, I simply froze all over, a trembling even went through me. ‘Well,’ I thought, ‘here is the little feature! This is it!’ And I didn’t even reason then; I simply didn’t want to. I would have given a thousand roubles at that moment, my own money, just to see you with my own eyes: how you walked a hundred paces beside that little tradesman, after he had said ‘murderer’ to your face, and didn’t dare ask him anything for a whole hundred paces!.. Well, and that chill in the spinal cord? Those bells, in your illness, in your half-delirium? And so, Rodion Romanych, why be surprised after that that I played such jokes with you then? And why did you come yourself at that very moment? Why, it was as if someone were pushing you too, by God, and if Mikolka had not separated us, then... and do you remember Mikolka then? Do you remember him well? Why, that was a thunderclap, sir! A thunderclap out of a cloud, a thunderbolt! Well, and how did I receive him? I didn’t believe the thunderbolt this much, you saw for yourself! Not a bit! Afterwards, after you had gone, when he began answering some points very, very coherently, so that I was surprised myself, even then I didn’t believe him a farthing’s worth! That is what it means to be fixed like adamant. No, I thought, morgen früh, not a chance! What Mikolka!”

“Razumikhin told me just now that you are still accusing Nikolai, and that you assured Razumikhin of it yourself...”

His breath caught and he did not finish. He was listening in inexpressible agitation as the man who had seen right through him was disowning himself. He was afraid to believe it and did not believe it. In the still ambiguous words he was greedily seeking and catching at something more precise and final.

“Mr Razumikhin!” cried Porfiry Petrovich, as if glad of a question from Raskolnikov, who had kept silent all this time. “He-he-he! Why, Mr Razumikhin had to be led off the scent: two’s company, three’s a crowd. Mr Razumikhin is not the man, sir, and an outsider besides; he came running to me all pale... Well, God be with him, why mix him up in this! And as for Mikolka, would you like to know what sort of subject he is, as I understand him, that is? First of all, he is still a child, a minor, and not exactly a coward, but rather like some sort of artist. Really, sir, don’t laugh at my describing him like that. Innocent, and receptive to everything. He has a heart; he is a dreamer. He sings, he dances, and they say he tells stories so that people come from other places to listen. He goes to school, and laughs till he drops if you show him your little finger, and drinks himself senseless, not from depravity, but in fits and starts, when they ply him with drink, still like a child. That time he stole, and he doesn’t know it himself; because ‘if I picked it up off the ground, how is that stealing?’ And do you know that he is one of the Old Believers, and not exactly an Old Believer, but simply a sectarian; there were Wanderers in his family, and he himself until recently, for two whole years, was under the spiritual guidance of a certain elder in his village. I found all this out from Mikolka and from his people from Zaraisk. Why, he simply wanted to run off into the wilderness! He had zeal, he prayed to God at night, read the old ‘true’ books and lost himself in them. Petersburg had a strong effect on him, especially the female sex, well, and wine. He is receptive, sir, and he forgot the elder and everything. I know that a certain artist here grew fond of him and began to visit him, and then this incident came along! Well, he lost his nerve: hang himself! Run away! What is to be done with the notion that has spread among the people about our legal system! To some the word ‘they’ll convict you’ is terrifying. Who is to blame! We shall see what the new courts will say. Oh, God grant it! Well, sir, in prison, evidently, he remembered the honest elder again; the Bible appeared again too. Do you know, Rodion Romanych, what ‘to suffer’ means to some of them? It is not for anyone in particular, but simply ‘one must suffer’; to accept suffering, that is, and if from the authorities, so much the better. In my time there was a most humble prisoner who sat in prison for a whole year, reading the Bible on the stove at night, and he read and read himself into it, you know, completely, so that out of the blue he grabbed a brick and threw it at the governor, without any offence on the governor’s part. And how he threw it: he deliberately aimed a yard wide, so as not to do any harm! Well, we know what end awaits a prisoner who attacks the authorities with a weapon: and so ‘he accepted suffering’. So I suspect now that Mikolka wants ‘to accept suffering’, or something of the kind. I know this for certain, even from facts, sir. Only he doesn’t know himself that I know. What, don’t you allow that fantastic people come out of such folk? Why, all the time! The elder has begun to act on him again, especially after the noose, he remembered him. But he will tell me everything himself; he will come. Do you think he will hold out? Wait, he will retract yet! I am expecting him at any hour to come and withdraw his statement. I have grown fond of this Mikolka and am studying him thoroughly. And what do you think! He-he! On some points he answered me very coherently; he has obviously got the necessary information and prepared himself cleverly; but on other points he simply knows not the least little thing, like a man stepping into a puddle, and doesn’t even suspect that he doesn’t know! No, my dear Rodion Romanych, Mikolka is not the one here! This is a fantastic, gloomy business, a modern business, a case of our time, sir, when the human heart has grown troubled; when the phrase is quoted that blood ‘refreshes’; when comfort is preached as the whole of life. Here there are bookish dreams, sir, here is a heart irritated by theory; here one sees resolve for the first step, but resolve of a special kind: he resolved, as if falling off a mountain or flying down from a bell tower, and came to the crime as if not on his own legs. He forgot to shut the door behind him, but he killed, killed two, by a theory. He killed, and he didn’t know how to take the money, and what he managed to snatch he carried off under a stone. It was not enough for him that he endured torment when he was sitting behind the door and they were breaking at the door and the bell was ringing; no, afterwards he goes to the empty flat, in half-delirium, to remember that bell, he needed to feel that spinal chill again... Well, let us say that was in illness; but here is something else: he killed, and yet considers himself an honest man, despises people, walks about like a pale angel; no, what Mikolka, my dear Rodion Romanych, Mikolka is not the one here!”

These last words, after everything said before, which had so resembled a renunciation, were too unexpected. Raskolnikov trembled all over, as if pierced.

“Then... who... killed them?..” he asked, unable to hold back, in a gasping voice. Porfiry Petrovich even recoiled against the back of his chair, as if he too were so unexpectedly amazed by the question.

“What do you mean, who killed them?..” he repeated, as if not believing his ears. “Why, you killed them, Rodion Romanych! It was you who killed them, sir...” he added, almost in a whisper, in a voice of complete conviction.

Raskolnikov jumped up from the sofa, stood for a few seconds and sat down again without saying a word. Small convulsions suddenly passed over his whole face.

“Your lip is trembling again, as it did then,” Porfiry Petrovich muttered, as if even with sympathy. “You have not understood me rightly, I think, Rodion Romanych,” he added after a short silence, “that is why you are so amazed. I have come precisely to say everything now and to bring the matter out into the open.”

“It wasn’t I who killed them,” Raskolnikov whispered, like frightened small children when they are caught in the act.

“No, it was you, sir, Rodion Romanych, you, sir, and no one else, sir,” Porfiry whispered sternly and with conviction.

They both fell silent, and the silence lasted even strangely long, about ten minutes. Raskolnikov leaned his elbows on the table and silently ruffled his hair with his fingers. Porfiry Petrovich sat quietly and waited. Suddenly Raskolnikov looked contemptuously at Porfiry.

“You are at your old tricks again, Porfiry Petrovich! Still the same methods of yours: don’t you get sick of it, really?”

“Eh, come now, what do I want with methods now! It would be another matter if there were witnesses here; but as it is we are whispering one to one. You see yourself that I have not come to chase you and catch you like a hare. Whether you confess or not is all the same to me at this moment. For my own part I am convinced without you.”

“And if so, why have you come?” Raskolnikov asked irritably. “I put my former question to you: if you consider me guilty, why don’t you take me to prison?”

“Well, there’s a question! I will answer you point by point: in the first place, it is not to my advantage to put you under arrest just like that.”

“How, not to your advantage! If you are convinced, then you must...”

“Eh, what if I am convinced? For the time being all this is my dreams, sir. And why should I put you away there to rest? You know yourself, if you are asking for it. Suppose I bring the little tradesman to confront you, and you say to him: ‘Are you drunk or not? Who saw me with you? I simply took you for a drunk, and you were drunk’; well, what shall I say to you then, all the more since your story is even more plausible than his, because in his statement there is nothing but psychology, which is not even proper for a mug like his, while you hit the very point, because he drinks like a fish, the scoundrel, and is far too well known for it. And I myself have frankly admitted to you, several times already, that this psychology cuts both ways and that the second end is the bigger and much more plausible one, and that apart from this I have nothing against you so far. And though I will still put you away, and have even come myself (quite unlike ordinary people) to announce everything to you beforehand, I tell you straight out (also unlike ordinary people) that it will not be to my advantage. Well, sir, in the second place, I have come to you because...”

“Well, yes, in the second place?” (Raskolnikov was still gasping.)

“Because, as I declared just now, I consider myself obliged to give you an explanation. I don’t want you to regard me as a monster, all the more since I am sincerely well disposed towards you, believe it or not. Consequently, in the third place, I have come to you with an open and direct proposal: to make a voluntary confession. It will be incalculably more advantageous for you, and more advantageous for me too, because it will be off my shoulders. Well, is that frank on my part or not?”

Raskolnikov thought for a minute.

“Listen, Porfiry Petrovich, you say yourself: nothing but psychology, and yet you have gone off into mathematics. Well, what if you yourself are mistaken now?”

“No, Rodion Romanych, I am not mistaken. I have a little feature. I found that little feature even then, sir; the Lord sent it!”

“What little feature?”

“I won’t say what, Rodion Romanych. And in any case I have no right to put it off any longer now; I shall put you away, sir. So consider: now it is all the same to me, and therefore I am doing this solely for you. By God, it will be better, Rodion Romanych!”

Raskolnikov smiled maliciously.

“Why, this is not only ridiculous, it is even shameless. Well, even if I were guilty (which I am not saying at all), why on earth should I come to you with a confession, when you yourself already say that I shall be sitting there with you at rest?”

“Eh, Rodion Romanych, don’t believe words entirely; perhaps it won’t be entirely at rest! That is only a theory, and mine at that, sir, and what sort of authority am I for you? Perhaps I am hiding something from you even now, sir. I can’t just take everything and lay it out for you, he-he! The second point: what advantage, you ask? But do you know what reduction of sentence will follow for you? When will you come, at what moment? Just consider that! When another man has already taken the crime on himself and muddled the whole case? And I swear to you by God himself that I will fix and arrange things ‘there’ so that your confession will come out as if quite unexpected. We will destroy all this psychology completely; I will turn all suspicions against you into nothing, so that your crime will appear as a kind of darkening of the mind, because, in conscience, that is what it is. I am an honest man, Rodion Romanych, and I will keep my word.”

Raskolnikov fell sadly silent and hung his head; he thought for a long time and at last smiled again, but his smile was gentle and sad now:

“Eh, I don’t want it!” he said, as if no longer hiding anything from Porfiry. “It isn’t worth it! I don’t want your reduction at all!”

“There, that is just what I was afraid of!” Porfiry exclaimed hotly and as if involuntarily. “That is just what I was afraid of, that you would not want our reduction.”

Raskolnikov looked at him sadly and impressively.

“Eh, don’t scorn life!” Porfiry went on. “There is a great deal of it still ahead. How can you not want a reduction, how can you not! You are an impatient man!”

“A great deal of what still ahead?”

“Of life! What sort of prophet are you, how much do you know? Seek and ye shall find. Perhaps this is just what God was waiting for you for. And it is not for ever, the chain...”

“There will be a reduction...” laughed Raskolnikov.

“What, are you afraid of bourgeois shame, perhaps? It may be that you are afraid, and don’t know it yourself, because you are young! But all the same, it is not for you to be afraid or ashamed of making a confession.”

“E-eh, I spit on it!” Raskolnikov whispered contemptuously and with disgust, as if not wishing even to speak. He half rose again, as if he wanted to go out somewhere, but sat down again in visible despair.

“That’s just it, you spit on it! You have lost your faith, and you think I am flattering you crudely; but how long have you lived yet? How much do you understand? You invented a theory, and you were ashamed that it failed, that it turned out very unoriginal! It turned out base, that is true, but all the same you are not a hopeless scoundrel. Not that kind of scoundrel at all! At least you didn’t fool yourself for long; you went all the way to the last posts at once. Do you know what I take you for? I take you for one of those men who, even if you cut out his guts, will stand and look at his torturers with a smile, if only he finds faith or God. Well, find them, and you will live. In the first place, you have needed a change of air for a long time. Well, suffering is a good thing too. Suffer. Mikolka perhaps is right to want suffering. I know that you don’t believe, but don’t be cunning and clever about it; give yourself to life directly, without reasoning; don’t worry: it will carry you straight to the shore and set you on your feet. What shore? How should I know? I only believe that you have a great deal of life still ahead. I know that you take my words now as a lecture learned by heart; but perhaps you will remember them later, they may be of use some day; that is why I say them. It is still a good thing that you killed only an old woman. If you had invented another theory, you might perhaps have done something a hundred million times more hideous! Perhaps you ought even to thank God; how do you know: perhaps God is keeping you for something. And you have a great heart, and fear less. Have you lost your nerve before the great fulfilment that lies ahead? No, it is shameful to lose your nerve here. If you have taken such a step, then brace yourself. This is justice now. Go and fulfil what justice demands. I know you don’t believe, but by God, life will carry you through. You will come to love it yourself later. All you need now is air, air, air!”

Raskolnikov even started.

“But who are you,” he cried, “what sort of prophet are you? From the height of what majestic calm do you deliver these wise prophecies to me?”

“Who am I? I am a finished man, nothing more. A man, perhaps, who feels and sympathises, who perhaps knows a thing or two, but a completely finished man. But you are another matter: God has prepared life for you (and who knows, perhaps with you too it will pass like smoke and nothing will come of it). Well, what if you pass into another category of people? You are not going to regret comfort, you, with your heart? What if perhaps no one sees you for too long? It is not a matter of time, but of you yourself. Become a sun, and everyone will see you. A sun must first of all be a sun. Why are you smiling again: because I am such a Schiller? And I bet you suppose that I am trying to flatter you now! Well, perhaps I really am, he-he-he! Perhaps you had better not take my word for it, Rodion Romanych, perhaps never believe me entirely; that is my nature, I agree; only I will add this: how low a man I am and how honest, you can judge for yourself, I think!”

“When do you intend to arrest me?”

“Why, I can still give you a day and a half or two to walk about. Think it over, my dear, pray to God. And it is more advantageous, by God, more advantageous.”

“And what if I run away?” Raskolnikov asked, smiling somehow strangely.

“No, you won’t run away. A peasant will run away, a fashionable sectarian will run away, the lackey of someone else’s thought, because you need only show him the tip of your little finger, like Midshipman Dyrka, and he will believe in anything you like for the rest of his life. But you no longer believe in your theory; what would you run away with? And what would you do as a fugitive? The life of a fugitive is nasty and hard, and what you need above all is life and a definite position, the air that suits you; well, is the air there yours? You will run away and come back of your own accord. You cannot do without us. And if I lock you up in the prison castle, well, you will sit there a month, two, three, and then suddenly, mark my words, you will come of your own accord, and how, perhaps, quite unexpectedly to yourself. Even an hour before you will not know that you are coming to confess. I am even sure that you will ‘think it over and decide to accept suffering’; you don’t take my word for it now, but you will settle on it yourself. Because suffering, Rodion Romanych, is a great thing; don’t look at the fact that I have grown fat; no matter, I know all the same; don’t laugh at it: there is an idea in suffering. Mikolka is right. No, you won’t run away, Rodion Romanych.”

Raskolnikov got up from his seat and took his cap. Porfiry Petrovich got up too.

“Going for a walk? It will be a fine evening, if only there isn’t a storm. Though it would be better if it freshened things up...”

He too reached for his cap.

“Please, Porfiry Petrovich, don’t take it into your head,” Raskolnikov said with stern insistence, “that I have confessed to you today. You are a strange man, and I listened to you out of mere curiosity. I have confessed nothing to you... Remember that.”

“Why, yes, I know, I will remember; look at that, he is even trembling. Don’t worry, my dear; your will be done. Walk about a little; only you mustn’t walk about too much. Just in case, I have one more little request for you,” he added, lowering his voice. “It is a ticklish little one, but important: if, that is, just in case (which, however, I don’t believe and consider you quite incapable of), if by chance, well, just in case, the wish should come to you during these forty or fifty hours to end the business somehow differently, in some fantastic way, to raise your hands against yourself like that (an absurd supposition, but you will forgive me for it), then leave a short but detailed little note. Just two lines, just two little lines, and mention the stone: it will be more noble, sir. Well, sir, goodbye... Good thoughts, good beginnings!”

Porfiry went out, somehow stooping and as if avoiding looking at Raskolnikov. Raskolnikov went to the window and waited with irritable impatience for the moment when, by his reckoning, the other would come out into the street and walk some distance away. Then he hurriedly left the room himself.
