## I

In the big building of the law courts, during a break in the hearing of the Melvinsky case, the members of the court and the public prosecutor met in Ivan Yegorovich Shebek’s office, and the conversation turned to the famous Krasov case. Fyodor Vasilyevich grew heated, arguing that it was outside the court’s jurisdiction; Ivan Yegorovich held his ground; while Pyotr Ivanovich, who had not joined the argument at the start, took no part in it and was looking through the *Gazette*, which had just been brought in.

“Gentlemen!” he said. “Ivan Ilyich has died.”

“Really?”

“Here, read it,” he said to Fyodor Vasilyevich, handing him the fresh issue, still smelling of ink.

Inside a black border was printed: “Praskovya Fyodorovna Golovina, with deep sorrow, informs relatives and friends of the death of her beloved husband, Ivan Ilyich Golovin, Member of the Court of Justice, which took place on the 4th of February of this year 1882. The funeral will be on Friday at one o’clock in the afternoon.”

Ivan Ilyich had been a colleague of the gentlemen gathered there, and they had all liked him. He had been ill for several weeks; it was said his illness was incurable. His post had been kept for him, but there had been talk that, in the event of his death, Alekseyev might be appointed to his place, and to Alekseyev’s place either Vinnikov or Shtabel. So that on hearing of Ivan Ilyich’s death, the first thought of each of the gentlemen in the office was what this death might mean for the transfers or promotions of the members themselves or their acquaintances.

“Now I’m sure to get Shtabel’s post, or Vinnikov’s,” thought Fyodor Vasilyevich. “It was promised me long ago, and the promotion means eight hundred roubles more a year for me, not counting the allowance for the office.”

“Now I must ask for my brother-in-law to be transferred from Kaluga,” thought Pyotr Ivanovich. “My wife will be very pleased. Now she won’t be able to say I’ve never done anything for her family.”

“I thought he would never get up again,” Pyotr Ivanovich said aloud. “It’s a pity.”

“But what exactly was wrong with him?”

“The doctors couldn’t decide. That is, they decided, but differently. When I saw him last, I thought he would get better.”

“And I never went to see him after the holidays. I kept meaning to.”

“Did he have any money?”

“I think his wife has something very small. But a trifle.”

“Yes, we must go. They lived terribly far away.”

“Far from you, you mean. Everything is far from you.”

“He can never forgive me for living across the river,” said Pyotr Ivanovich, smiling at Shebek. And they talked about the great distances in the city, and went back into the court.

Apart from the thoughts about transfers and possible changes at work that this death set off in each of them, the very fact of the death of a close acquaintance aroused in all who heard of it, as always, a feeling of gladness that it was he who had died and not I.

“Well, he’s dead; but I’m not,” each of them thought or felt. And his close acquaintances, Ivan Ilyich’s so-called friends, could not help thinking too that they would now have to carry out the very tedious duties of decency: go to the funeral service and pay the widow a visit of condolence.

The closest of all were Fyodor Vasilyevich and Pyotr Ivanovich.

Pyotr Ivanovich had been at the School of Law with Ivan Ilyich and considered himself indebted to him.

Having told his wife at dinner the news of Ivan Ilyich’s death and his thoughts about the possibility of getting his brother-in-law transferred to their district, Pyotr Ivanovich did not lie down for his usual rest, but put on his tailcoat and drove to Ivan Ilyich’s.

At the entrance to Ivan Ilyich’s flat stood a carriage and two cabs. Downstairs in the hall, by the coat stand, the lid of the coffin, covered in gold brocade, with tassels and braid polished with powder, was leaning against the wall. Two ladies in black were taking off their fur coats. One, Ivan Ilyich’s sister, he knew; the other lady he did not. Pyotr Ivanovich’s colleague Schwartz was coming downstairs and, seeing him come in from the top step, stopped and winked at him, as if to say: “Ivan Ilyich has made a mess of things; not like you and me.”

Schwartz’s face with its English side-whiskers, and his whole lean figure in a tailcoat, had, as always, an elegant solemnity, and this solemnity, which always contradicted Schwartz’s playful character, had a special piquancy here. So Pyotr Ivanovich thought.

Pyotr Ivanovich let the ladies go ahead of him and slowly followed them up the stairs. Schwartz did not come down but stopped at the top. Pyotr Ivanovich understood why: he obviously wanted to arrange where they would play cards that evening. The ladies went up the stairs to the widow, while Schwartz, his strong lips seriously pressed together and a playful look in his eyes, pointed Pyotr Ivanovich with a movement of his eyebrows to the right, to the dead man’s room.

Pyotr Ivanovich went in, as always happens, uncertain what he would have to do there. One thing he knew: in such cases crossing yourself never hurts. As to whether one ought also to bow, he was not quite sure, and so he chose the middle course: on entering the room he began to cross himself and, as it were, to bow slightly. As far as the movements of his hands and head allowed, he looked around the room at the same time. Two young men, one a schoolboy, nephews probably, were crossing themselves as they left the room. An old woman stood motionless. And a lady with strangely raised eyebrows was saying something to her in a whisper. A church reader in a frock coat, brisk and resolute, was reading something aloud in a tone that excluded all contradiction. The servant Gerasim, passing in front of Pyotr Ivanovich with light steps, was sprinkling something on the floor. Seeing this, Pyotr Ivanovich at once caught a faint smell of a decomposing body. On his last visit to Ivan Ilyich, Pyotr Ivanovich had seen this peasant in the study; he had been acting as a nurse, and Ivan Ilyich had been particularly fond of him. Pyotr Ivanovich kept crossing himself and bowing slightly in a direction halfway between the coffin, the reader and the icons on the table in the corner. Then, when this movement of crossing himself began to seem to him to have gone on too long, he stopped and began to look at the dead man.

The dead man lay, as dead men always lie, especially heavily, in a dead man’s way, his stiffened limbs sunk into the lining of the coffin, his head bent forever on the pillow, and he displayed, as dead men always do, his yellow, waxen forehead with bald patches on the sunken temples, and his protruding nose, which seemed to press down on his upper lip. He had changed a great deal, and grown even thinner since Pyotr Ivanovich had last seen him, but, as with all dead men, his face was more handsome, and above all more significant, than it had been when he was alive. On the face was an expression that what had needed to be done had been done, and done rightly. Besides this, there was in the expression a reproach, or a reminder, to the living. This reminder seemed to Pyotr Ivanovich out of place, or at least not to concern him. Something felt unpleasant to him, and so Pyotr Ivanovich hastily crossed himself once more and, too hastily as it seemed to him, not quite in keeping with decency, turned and went to the door. Schwartz was waiting for him in the next room, his legs planted wide apart and both hands behind his back playing with his top hat. One look at Schwartz’s playful, well-groomed and elegant figure refreshed Pyotr Ivanovich. Pyotr Ivanovich understood that he, Schwartz, was above all this and did not give in to depressing impressions. His very appearance said: the incident of Ivan Ilyich’s funeral service cannot possibly be considered sufficient grounds for declaring the order of the session disrupted; that is, nothing can prevent us from breaking open a new pack of cards with a snap this very evening, while the footman sets out four fresh candles; in general there is no reason to suppose that this incident could stop us spending a pleasant evening today as well. And he said so in a whisper to Pyotr Ivanovich as he passed, proposing that they meet for a game at Fyodor Vasilyevich’s. But it was evidently not Pyotr Ivanovich’s fate to play cards that evening. Praskovya Fyodorovna, a short, fat woman who, in spite of all her efforts to the contrary, still widened from the shoulders downwards, all in black, her head covered with lace and with the same strangely raised eyebrows as the lady standing by the coffin, came out of her rooms with some other ladies and, showing them to the door of the dead man’s room, said:

“The service will begin in a moment; please go in.”

Schwartz, bowing vaguely, stood still, evidently neither accepting nor declining the invitation. Praskovya Fyodorovna, recognising Pyotr Ivanovich, sighed, came right up to him, took his hand and said:

“I know you were a true friend of Ivan Ilyich’s...” and looked at him, waiting for him to act in a way that matched these words.

Pyotr Ivanovich knew that just as in there one had to cross oneself, here one had to press her hand, sigh and say, “Believe me!” And so he did. And having done it, he felt that the desired result had been achieved: that he was touched and she was touched.

“Come, before it begins in there; I need to talk to you,” said the widow. “Give me your arm.”

Pyotr Ivanovich gave her his arm, and they went towards the inner rooms, past Schwartz, who winked sadly at Pyotr Ivanovich: “So much for our cards! Don’t hold it against us if we find another partner. Perhaps you can make a fifth, when you get away,” said his playful look.

Pyotr Ivanovich sighed still more deeply and sadly, and Praskovya Fyodorovna gratefully pressed his arm. Going into her drawing room, upholstered in pink cretonne and lit by a dim lamp, they sat down at the table: she on the sofa, and Pyotr Ivanovich on a low pouffe whose springs had gone and which gave way unevenly under him. Praskovya Fyodorovna had meant to warn him to sit on another chair, but decided that such a warning did not suit her position, and changed her mind. As he sat down on this pouffe, Pyotr Ivanovich remembered how Ivan Ilyich had furnished this drawing room and had consulted him about this very pink cretonne with green leaves. Going past the table to sit on the sofa (the whole drawing room was crammed with knick-knacks and furniture), the widow caught the black lace of her black shawl on the carving of the table. Pyotr Ivanovich half rose to free it, and the pouffe, released, began to heave and push him. The widow began to free her lace herself, and Pyotr Ivanovich sat down again, crushing the pouffe rebelling beneath him. But the widow had not freed it all, and Pyotr Ivanovich rose again, and again the pouffe rebelled and even creaked. When all this was over, she took out a clean cambric handkerchief and began to weep. But the episode with the lace and the struggle with the pouffe had cooled Pyotr Ivanovich, and he sat frowning. This awkward situation was broken by Sokolov, Ivan Ilyich’s butler, who came to report that the plot in the cemetery that Praskovya Fyodorovna had chosen would cost two hundred roubles. She stopped weeping and, looking at Pyotr Ivanovich with the air of a victim, said in French that it was very hard for her. Pyotr Ivanovich made a silent sign expressing his complete conviction that it could not be otherwise.

“Please do smoke,” she said in a voice that was at once generous and crushed, and turned to discussing with Sokolov the price of the plot. Lighting his cigarette, Pyotr Ivanovich heard her ask in great detail about the various prices of the ground and settle on the one she would take. Besides that, when she had finished with the plot, she gave orders about the choir. Sokolov left.

“I do everything myself,” she said to Pyotr Ivanovich, moving the albums on the table to one side; and, noticing that his ash was threatening the table, she promptly pushed an ashtray towards him and said: “I think it is pretence to claim that grief prevents one from attending to practical matters. On the contrary, if anything can... not console me, but distract me, it is looking after things for him.” She took out her handkerchief again, as if about to weep, and suddenly, as if mastering herself, shook herself and began to speak calmly:

“But I have some business with you.”

Pyotr Ivanovich bowed, not allowing the springs of the pouffe, which at once began to stir under him, to get out of hand.

“In the last days he suffered terribly.”

“Did he suffer very much?” asked Pyotr Ivanovich.

“Oh, terribly! For the last hours, not minutes, hours, he screamed without stopping. For three days and nights on end he screamed without pausing for breath. It was unbearable. I cannot understand how I bore it; you could hear it through three doors. Oh, what I have been through!”

“And was he really conscious?” asked Pyotr Ivanovich.

“Yes,” she whispered, “to the last minute. He said goodbye to us a quarter of an hour before he died, and he asked us to take Volodya away.”

The thought of the suffering of a man he had known so closely, first as a cheerful boy, a schoolboy, then as a grown-up partner at cards, in spite of the unpleasant awareness of his own pretence and this woman’s, suddenly horrified Pyotr Ivanovich. He saw again that forehead, that nose pressing down on the lip, and he felt afraid for himself.

“Three days and nights of terrible suffering, and death. Why, that could come for me too, at once, at any minute,” he thought, and for a moment he was afraid. But at once, he did not know how, the usual thought came to his rescue: that this had happened to Ivan Ilyich and not to him, that it ought not to and could not happen to him; that by thinking like this he was giving in to a gloomy mood, which one ought not to do, as was evident from Schwartz’s face. And having reasoned like this, Pyotr Ivanovich calmed down and began to ask with interest about the details of Ivan Ilyich’s end, as if death were an adventure peculiar to Ivan Ilyich and not at all peculiar to himself.

After various conversations about the details of the truly terrible physical sufferings that Ivan Ilyich had endured (details that Pyotr Ivanovich learned only from the way Ivan Ilyich’s torments had affected Praskovya Fyodorovna’s nerves), the widow evidently found it necessary to get down to business.

“Oh, Pyotr Ivanovich, how hard it is, how terribly hard, how terribly hard,” and she began to weep again.

Pyotr Ivanovich sighed and waited for her to blow her nose. When she had blown her nose, he said:

“Believe me...” and again she began to talk and told him what was evidently her chief business with him; and this business consisted of questions about how, on the occasion of her husband’s death, she could get money from the government. She pretended to ask Pyotr Ivanovich’s advice about a pension; but he saw that she already knew, down to the smallest details, things he did not know himself: everything that could be got out of the government on the occasion of this death; and that what she wanted to find out was whether some way could be found of getting still more money. Pyotr Ivanovich tried to think of such a way, but after thinking a little and, for decency’s sake, abusing our government for its stinginess, he said he thought nothing more could be got. Then she sighed and evidently began to think of a way to get rid of her visitor. He understood this, put out his cigarette, stood up, pressed her hand and went out into the hall.

In the dining room with the clock that Ivan Ilyich had been so pleased to find in an antique shop, Pyotr Ivanovich met the priest and several more acquaintances who had come for the service, and saw Ivan Ilyich’s daughter, a pretty young woman he knew. She was all in black. Her very slender waist seemed slenderer still. She had a gloomy, determined, almost angry look. She bowed to Pyotr Ivanovich as if he were somehow to blame. Behind the daughter, with the same offended look, stood a rich young man Pyotr Ivanovich knew, an examining magistrate, her fiancé, as he had heard. He bowed to them dejectedly and was about to go into the dead man’s room when from under the stairs appeared the little figure of the son, a schoolboy, terribly like Ivan Ilyich. He was the little Ivan Ilyich whom Pyotr Ivanovich remembered from the School of Law. His eyes were tear-stained, and also the kind of eyes impure boys of thirteen or fourteen have. Seeing Pyotr Ivanovich, the boy began to frown, sternly and shamefacedly. Pyotr Ivanovich nodded to him and went into the dead man’s room. The service began: candles, groans, incense, tears, sobs. Pyotr Ivanovich stood frowning, looking at the feet in front of him. He did not once look at the dead man, did not give in to the weakening influences to the very end, and was one of the first to leave. There was nobody in the hall. Gerasim, the servant, darted out of the dead man’s room, tossed all the fur coats about with his strong hands to find Pyotr Ivanovich’s, and handed it to him.

“Well, Gerasim, my friend?” said Pyotr Ivanovich, to say something. “It’s sad, isn’t it?”

“It’s God’s will. We shall all come to it,” said Gerasim, showing his white, even, peasant’s teeth, and, like a man in the thick of hard work, he briskly opened the door, called the coachman, helped Pyotr Ivanovich in, and sprang back to the porch, as if wondering what else he could do.

It was especially pleasant for Pyotr Ivanovich to breathe the clean air after the smell of incense, the corpse and carbolic acid.

“Where to, sir?” asked the coachman.

“It isn’t late. I’ll call on Fyodor Vasilyevich after all.”

And Pyotr Ivanovich drove there. And indeed he found them finishing the first rubber, so that it was convenient for him to come in as a fifth.
