## Chapter X · “It Was He Who Said That!”

Coming in, Alyosha told Ivan Fyodorovich that a little over an hour before, Marya Kondratyevna had run to his lodgings and announced that Smerdyakov had taken his own life. “I went in to him to clear away the samovar, and there he was hanging on a little nail by the wall.” To Alyosha’s question, “Have you reported it to the proper person?”, she answered that she had not reported it to anyone, but “rushed straight to you first and ran all the way.” She was like a madwoman, Alyosha said, and trembling all over like a leaf. And when Alyosha had run back with her to their cottage, he found Smerdyakov still hanging. On the table lay a note: “I destroy my life of my own will and desire, so as to blame no one.” Alyosha left the note on the table and went straight to the police captain and reported everything to him, “and from there straight to you,” Alyosha concluded, looking intently into Ivan’s face. And all the time he was telling it, he did not take his eyes off him, as if struck by something in the expression of his face.

“Brother,” he suddenly cried, “you must be terribly ill! You are looking at me and seem not to understand what I am saying.”

“It is good that you have come,” Ivan said as if thoughtfully, and as if he had not heard Alyosha’s exclamation at all. “And I knew he had hanged himself.”

“From whom?”

“I don’t know from whom. But I knew. Did I know? Yes, he told me. He was telling me just now…”

Ivan was standing in the middle of the room and still speaking thoughtfully, looking at the ground.

“Who is he?” asked Alyosha, involuntarily looking round.

“He has slipped away.”

Ivan raised his head and smiled quietly.

“He was afraid of you, of you, a dove. You are a ‘pure cherub’. Dmitri calls you a cherub. Cherub… The thunderous cry of rapture of the seraphim! What is a seraph? Perhaps a whole constellation. And perhaps the whole constellation is only some chemical molecule… There is a constellation of the Lion and the Sun; don’t you know it?”

“Brother, sit down!” said Alyosha in alarm. “Sit down on the sofa, for God’s sake. You are delirious; lie back on the pillow, like that. Would you like a wet towel on your head? Perhaps it will make you better?”

“Give me the towel; it’s here on the chair; I threw it here just now.”

“It isn’t here. Don’t worry, I know where it is; here it is,” said Alyosha, finding in another corner of the room, by Ivan’s dressing table, a clean towel, still folded and unused. Ivan looked strangely at the towel; his memory seemed to come back to him in an instant.

“Wait,” he half rose from the sofa. “Just now, an hour ago, I took this very towel from there and wetted it. I put it on my head and threw it here… How is it dry? There was no other.”

“You put this towel on your head?” asked Alyosha.

“Yes, and walked about the room, an hour ago… Why have the candles burned down so? What time is it?”

“Nearly twelve.”

“No, no, no!” Ivan cried suddenly. “It was not a dream! He was here; he was sitting here, there on that sofa. When you knocked at the window I threw a glass at him… this one… Wait, I was asleep before, but this dream is not a dream. It has happened before. I have dreams now, Alyosha… but they are not dreams; they are real: I walk, talk and see… and I am asleep. But he was sitting here, he was here, on that sofa… He is terribly stupid, Alyosha, terribly stupid,” Ivan suddenly laughed and began pacing the room.

“Who is stupid? Whom are you talking about, brother?” Alyosha asked again in anguish.

“The devil! He has taken to coming to me. He has been here twice, almost three times. He teased me by saying I was angry that he was simply a devil and not Satan with scorched wings, in thunder and lightning. But he is not Satan; he lies about that. He is an impostor. He is simply a devil, a paltry, petty devil. He goes to the baths. Undress him and you will surely find a tail, long, smooth, like a Great Dane’s, a yard long, brown… Alyosha, you are chilled; you have been in the snow; will you have some tea? What? Cold? Shall I have it heated? C’est à ne pas mettre un chien dehors…”

Alyosha ran quickly to the washstand, wetted the towel, persuaded Ivan to sit down again and wrapped the wet towel round his head. He sat down beside him.

“What were you telling me this evening about Lise?” Ivan began again. (He was becoming very talkative.) “I like Lise. I said something nasty to you about her. I lied; I like her… I am afraid for Katya tomorrow; that is what I am most afraid of. For the future. Tomorrow she will throw me over and trample me underfoot. She thinks I am ruining Mitya out of jealousy of her! Yes, she thinks that! Well, I am not! Tomorrow the cross, but not the gallows. No, I won’t hang myself. Do you know that I can never take my own life, Alyosha? From baseness, perhaps? I am not a coward. From thirst for life! How did I know that Smerdyakov had hanged himself? Yes, it was he who told me…”

“And you are firmly convinced that someone was sitting here?” asked Alyosha.

“There on that sofa, in the corner. You would have driven him away. Why, you did drive him away: he vanished as you appeared. I love your face, Alyosha. Did you know that I love your face? And he is I, Alyosha, I myself. All that is low in me, all that is vile and contemptible! Yes, I am a ‘romantic’; he noticed that… though it is a slander. He is terribly stupid, but that is how he gets the better of me. He is cunning, animal cunning; he knew how to infuriate me. He kept teasing me that I believed in him, and so he made me listen to him. He fooled me like a little boy. He told me a great deal of truth about myself, though. I should never have said it to myself. You know, Alyosha, you know,” Ivan added terribly seriously and as if confidentially, “I should very much like him really to be he, and not I!”

“He has tormented you,” said Alyosha, looking at his brother with compassion.

“He teased me! And cleverly, you know, cleverly: ‘Conscience! What is conscience? I make it myself. Why do I torment myself, then? From habit. From the universal human habit of seven thousand years. So let us lose the habit, and we shall be gods.’ It was he who said that, it was he who said that!”

“And not you, not you?” Alyosha cried irresistibly, looking clearly at his brother. “Well, let him be; throw him off and forget him! Let him take with him everything that you curse now, and never come again!”

“Yes, but he is malicious. He laughed at me. He was impudent, Alyosha,” Ivan said, shuddering with offence. “But he slandered me; he slandered me in many things. He lied to me about myself to my face. ‘Oh, you are going to perform a great act of virtue; you will declare that you killed your father, that the lackey killed your father at your instigation…’”

“Brother,” Alyosha interrupted, “restrain yourself: it was not you who killed him. That is not true!”

“That is what he says, he, and he knows it: ‘You are going to perform a great act of virtue, and you don’t believe in virtue; that is what angers and torments you; that is why you are so vindictive.’ That is what he told me about myself, and he knows what he is saying…”

“It is you who are saying it, not he!” Alyosha exclaimed sorrowfully. “And you are saying it in illness, in delirium, tormenting yourself!”

“No, he knows what he is saying. ‘You are going out of pride,’ he says; ‘you will stand up and say: “It was I who killed him; why are you writhing with horror? You are lying! I despise your opinion; I despise your horror.”’ That is what he says about me, and suddenly he says: ‘And you know, you want them to praise you: a criminal, a murderer, but what magnanimous feelings he has; he wanted to save his brother, and confessed!’ Now that is a lie, Alyosha!” Ivan suddenly cried, his eyes flashing. “I don’t want the rabble to praise me! He lied, Alyosha, he lied, I swear to you! I threw the glass at him for that, and it smashed against his ugly face.”

“Brother, calm yourself; stop!” Alyosha begged.

“No, he knows how to torment; he is cruel,” Ivan went on, not listening. “I always had a presentiment of why he comes. ‘Granted,’ he says, ‘that you were going out of pride; still there was a hope too that they would convict Smerdyakov and send him to penal servitude, that Mitya would be acquitted, and you would be condemned only morally’ (do you hear, he laughed at that!) ‘and others would even praise you. But now Smerdyakov is dead; he has hanged himself; and who will believe you alone at the trial now? And yet you are going, you are going; you will go all the same; you have decided to go. Why are you going, after this?’ It is terrible, Alyosha; I can’t bear such questions. Who dares to ask me such questions?”

“Brother,” Alyosha interrupted, sinking with fear, but still as if hoping to bring Ivan to reason, “how could he have spoken to you of Smerdyakov’s death before I came, when no one yet knew of it, and there was no time for anyone to find out?”

“He did speak of it,” Ivan said firmly, not admitting any doubt. “It was all he spoke about, if you like. ‘And it would be all very well,’ he says, ‘if you believed in virtue: let them not believe me; I go for the principle of it. But you are a little pig, like Fyodor Pavlovich, and what is virtue to you? Why will you drag yourself there, if your sacrifice will serve no purpose? Because you don’t know yourself why you are going! Oh, you would give a great deal to know yourself why you are going! And have you really decided? You have not decided yet. You will sit up all night deciding whether to go or not. But you will go all the same, and you know you will go; you know yourself that however you decide, the decision no longer depends on you. You will go because you dare not not go. Why you dare not, guess that yourself; there’s a riddle for you!’ He got up and went. You came, and he went. He called me a coward, Alyosha! Le mot de l’énigme (the answer to the riddle) is that I am a coward! ‘It is not for such eagles to soar above the earth!’ He added that, he added that! And Smerdyakov said the same. He must be killed! Katya despises me; I have seen it for a month; and Lise will begin to despise me too! ‘You are going so that they will praise you’: that is a bestial lie! And you despise me too, Alyosha. Now I shall hate you again. And I hate the monster, I hate the monster! I don’t want to save the monster; let him rot in penal servitude! He has started singing a hymn! Oh, tomorrow I shall go, stand before them and spit in all their faces!”

He jumped up in a frenzy, threw off the towel and began pacing the room again. Alyosha remembered his words of a little while ago: “It is as though I were asleep awake… I walk, talk and see, and I am asleep.” It was exactly as if that were happening now. Alyosha did not leave him. The thought flashed through his mind of running for a doctor and bringing him, but he was afraid to leave his brother alone: there was absolutely no one to leave him with. At last Ivan little by little began to lose consciousness entirely. He kept on talking, talking without stopping, but quite incoherently now. He even pronounced his words badly, and suddenly he swayed heavily where he stood. But Alyosha managed to hold him up. Ivan let himself be led to the bed; Alyosha undressed him somehow and put him to bed. He sat over him for another two hours. The sick man slept soundly, without moving, breathing quietly and evenly. Alyosha took a pillow and lay down on the sofa without undressing. As he fell asleep he prayed for Mitya and for Ivan. Ivan’s illness was becoming clear to him: “The torments of a proud decision, a deep conscience!” God, in whom he did not believe, and his truth were overcoming a heart that still did not want to submit. “Yes,” ran through Alyosha’s head as it lay on the pillow, “yes, now that Smerdyakov is dead, no one will believe Ivan’s evidence; but he will go and give it!” Alyosha smiled quietly. “God will conquer!” he thought. “Either he will rise up in the light of truth, or… he will perish in hatred, taking revenge on himself and on everyone for having served what he does not believe in,” Alyosha added bitterly, and prayed for Ivan again.
