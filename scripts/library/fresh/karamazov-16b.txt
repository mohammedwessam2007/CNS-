## Part Three · Book Seven: Alyosha · Chapter I · The Odour of Corruption

The body of the departed hieroschemamonk Father Zosima was prepared for burial according to the established rite. Dead monks and schemamonks, as is well known, are not washed. “When any of the monks departs unto the Lord” (it is said in the great Book of Rites), “the appointed monk” (that is, the one designated for this) “wipeth his body with warm water, first making with the sponge” (that is, a Greek sponge) “a cross on the forehead of the departed, on the breast, on the hands and on the feet and on the knees, and nothing more.” All this Father Paisy performed himself over the departed. After the wiping he dressed him in the monastic habit and wrapped him in the mantle, cutting it a little for this, according to the rule, so as to wrap it crosswise. On his head he put the cowl with the eight-pointed cross. The cowl was left open, and the face of the departed was covered with a black veil. In his hands they laid an icon of the Saviour. In this form, towards morning, they moved him into the coffin (which had been made ready long before). The coffin they decided to leave in the cell (in the first, large room, the very one in which the late elder had received the brethren and laymen) all day. Since the departed was by rank a hieroschemamonk, hieromonks and hierodeacons had to read over him not the Psalter but the Gospel. Father Iosif began the reading, immediately after the requiem; and Father Paisy, who wished himself to read afterwards all day and all night, was for the time being very busy and anxious, together with the head of the hermitage. For something extraordinary began suddenly to show itself, more and more as time went on, both among the monastery brethren and among the laymen who came in crowds from the monastery guesthouses and from the town: some unheard-of and even “unseemly” agitation and impatient expectation. Both the head of the hermitage and Father Paisy did all they could to calm those so fussily excited. When it was full day, people began arriving from the town, some even bringing their sick with them, especially children, as if they had been waiting on purpose for this moment, evidently hoping for an immediate power of healing, which, by their faith, could not be slow to show itself. And only now did it become clear to what degree everyone among us had grown used to regarding the late elder, even in his lifetime, as an undoubted and great saint. And those arriving were by no means only of the common people. This great expectation of the believers, shown so hastily and so nakedly, and even impatiently, almost as a demand, seemed to Father Paisy an undoubted temptation; and though he had foreseen it long before, in reality it went beyond his expectations. Meeting excited monks, Father Paisy even began to rebuke them: “Such an immediate expectation of something great,” he said, “is a frivolity possible only among people of the world, and unseemly for us.” But they paid him little heed, and Father Paisy noticed it with uneasiness, even though he himself (if everything is to be remembered truthfully), though indignant at the too impatient expectations and finding frivolity and vanity in them, secretly, within himself, in the depths of his soul, was expecting almost the same as these excited ones, as he could not help admitting to himself. Nevertheless certain encounters were particularly unpleasant to him and roused great doubts in him from a kind of foreboding. In the crowd that thronged the dead man’s cell he noticed with an inward disgust (for which he immediately reproached himself) the presence, for instance, of Rakitin, or of the distant visitor, the monk from Obdorsk, who was still staying at the monastery; and both of them Father Paisy suddenly, for some reason, considered suspicious, though they were not the only ones who could be noticed in this sense. The monk from Obdorsk stood out as the most fussy of all the excited ones; he could be seen everywhere, in all places: everywhere he was asking questions, everywhere listening, everywhere whispering with a peculiar, mysterious air. And his face had the most impatient expression, as if he were already irritated that what had been expected so long had not happened. As for Rakitin, he, as it turned out later, had come to the hermitage so early on a special commission from Madame Khokhlakova. This kind but weak-willed woman, who could not be admitted to the hermitage herself, had no sooner woken and heard of the death than she was seized with so violent a curiosity that she at once dispatched Rakitin to the hermitage in her place, with instructions to observe everything and immediately report to her in writing, about every half-hour, everything that happened. She considered Rakitin the most pious and believing of young men, so well did he know how to get on with everyone and present himself to each as that person wished, if only he saw the least advantage to himself in it. The day was clear and bright, and many of the pilgrims who had come crowded round the graves of the hermitage, which were most closely packed round the church, though they were also scattered all over the hermitage. Going round the hermitage, Father Paisy suddenly remembered Alyosha, and that he had not seen him for a long time, almost since the night. And no sooner had he remembered him than he caught sight of him in the farthest corner of the hermitage, by the wall, sitting on the gravestone of a monk who had died long ago and was famous for his ascetic labours. He was sitting with his back to the hermitage, facing the wall, as if hiding behind the monument. Coming right up, Father Paisy saw that he had covered his face with both hands and, though without a sound, was weeping bitterly, his whole body shaking with sobs. Father Paisy stood over him for a while.

“Enough, my dear son, enough, my friend,” he said at last with feeling. “What is it? Rejoice and do not weep. Or don’t you know that this day is the greatest of his days? Only think where he is now, at this moment!”

Alyosha glanced up at him, uncovering his face, swollen with tears like a little child’s, but at once, without a word, turned away and covered it again with both hands.

“But perhaps it is right,” Father Paisy said thoughtfully. “Weep, perhaps; Christ has sent you these tears. ‘Your tender little tears are only a rest for your soul and will serve to gladden your dear heart,’” he added to himself, moving away from Alyosha and thinking of him lovingly. He moved away quickly, though, for he felt that, looking at him, he might weep himself. Meanwhile time went on; the monastery services and requiems for the departed continued in order. Father Paisy again took Father Iosif’s place at the coffin and again took over the reading of the Gospel from him. But it was not yet three in the afternoon when something happened that I mentioned at the end of the last book, something so unexpected by anyone among us and so contrary to the general hope that, I repeat, the detailed and frivolous story of this occurrence is remembered with extraordinary vividness to this day in our town and in all the country round. Here I will add once more from myself personally: it is almost repugnant to me to recall this frivolous and scandalous event, in essence a most trivial and natural one, and I would of course have left it out of my story altogether, had it not influenced in the strongest and most definite way the soul and heart of the chief, though future, hero of my story, Alyosha, making as it were a crisis and turning point in his soul, which shook but also strengthened his mind finally, for his whole life and towards a definite goal.

And so to the story. Before dawn, when they had laid the elder’s body, prepared for burial, in the coffin and carried it into the first room, the former reception room, a question arose among those at the coffin: should the windows in the room be opened? But this question, put by someone in passing, briefly, remained unanswered and almost unnoticed; only a few of those present noticed it, and then to themselves, in this sense only: that to expect corruption and an odour of corruption from the body of such a departed man was an utter absurdity, deserving even pity (if not a smile) for the little faith and frivolity of the one who had asked the question. For they were expecting the exact opposite. And then, soon after midday, something began which at first those who came in and went out received only in silence and to themselves, each with an evident fear of telling anyone the thought that was beginning in him; but by three in the afternoon it had shown itself so clearly and undeniably that the news of it flew instantly all over the hermitage and to all the pilgrims visiting it, at once reached the monastery too and threw everyone in the monastery into amazement, and finally, in the shortest time, reached the town and stirred up everyone there, believers and unbelievers. The unbelievers rejoiced; and as for the believers, some of them rejoiced even more than the unbelievers themselves, for “men love the fall of the righteous and his disgrace,” as the late elder himself had said in one of his teachings. The fact was that from the coffin there began to come, little by little, but more and more noticeably as time went on, an odour of corruption, which by three in the afternoon had become all too evident and was gradually growing stronger. And never for a long time, nor could anyone remember it in all the past life of our monastery, had there been such a scandal, coarsely unbridled, and in any other case even impossible, as broke out immediately after this event even among the monks themselves. Afterwards, even after many years, some sensible monks of ours, recalling that whole day in detail, wondered and were horrified at how the scandal could have reached such a pitch then. For before this too it had happened that monks of very righteous life died, whose righteousness was plain to all, God-fearing elders, and yet from their humble coffins too there came an odour of corruption, appearing naturally, as with all the dead; but that had caused no scandal, nor even the slightest agitation. Of course there were some among us, departed long ago, whose memory was still vividly kept in the monastery, and whose remains, according to tradition, had shown no corruption, which had a moving and mysterious effect on the brethren and was kept in their memory as something seemly and miraculous, and as a promise of still greater glory from their tombs in the future, if only by God’s will the time should come for it. Among these the memory was especially kept of the elder Iov, who lived to a hundred and five, a famous ascetic, a great faster and keeper of silence, who died long ago, back in the second decade of this century, and whose grave was shown with special and extraordinary reverence to all pilgrims arriving for the first time, with mysterious hints at certain great hopes. (It was the very grave on which Father Paisy had found Alyosha sitting that morning.) Besides this elder, departed long ago, a similar memory was alive of the great father hieroschemamonk, the elder Varsonofy, who had died comparatively recently, the very one from whom Father Zosima had received the eldership, and whom in his lifetime all the pilgrims who came to the monastery regarded simply as a holy fool. Of both of these it was preserved in tradition that they lay in their coffins as if alive and were buried quite incorrupt, and that even their faces seemed to grow bright in the coffin. And some even insisted that a fragrance could be plainly felt from their bodies. But despite even such impressive memories, it would still be hard to explain the direct cause by which so frivolous, absurd and spiteful a scene could take place at Elder Zosima’s coffin. For my own part, I suppose that many other things came together at the same time, many different causes acting together. Among them, for instance, was that same inveterate hostility to the eldership as a pernicious innovation, which still lay deeply hidden in the minds of many monks in the monastery. And then, of course, and chiefly, there was envy of the dead man’s holiness, so firmly established in his lifetime that it seemed forbidden even to object to it. For though the late elder had drawn many to himself, and not so much by miracles as by love, and had raised up round himself a whole world, as it were, of those who loved him, nevertheless, and even all the more, by that very thing he had bred envious ones too, and after them bitter enemies, open and secret, and not only among the monks but even among laymen. He had done no one any harm, for instance; but there it was: “Why do they think him so holy?” And that one question alone, repeated little by little, bred at last a whole abyss of the most insatiable spite. That is why I think that many, hearing of the odour of corruption from his body, and so soon too (for not even a day had passed since his death), were immeasurably glad; just as among those devoted to the elder and who had honoured him until then there were at once some who were almost insulted and personally offended by this event. And the thing went on by degrees as follows.

As soon as the corruption began to show itself, one could tell from the mere look of the monks who came into the dead man’s cell why they were coming. One comes in, stands a little, and goes out to confirm the news quickly to the others waiting outside in a crowd. Some of those waiting shook their heads sorrowfully, but others no longer even wanted to hide their joy, which shone plainly in their spiteful eyes. And no one reproached them any more, no one raised a kindly voice, which was even strange, for those devoted to the late elder were still the majority in the monastery; but evidently the Lord himself allowed the minority to get the upper hand for a time on this occasion. Soon laymen too began appearing in the cell, as spies of the same kind, mostly from among the educated visitors. Few of the common people came in, though many of them crowded at the gates of the hermitage. It is certain that precisely after three o’clock the influx of lay visitors greatly increased, and precisely because of the scandalous news. People who would perhaps not have come at all that day and had not meant to come now came on purpose, among them some persons of considerable rank. Outward decorum, however, was not yet broken, and Father Paisy, firmly and distinctly, with a stern face, went on reading the Gospel aloud, as though not noticing what was happening, though he had long since noticed something extraordinary. But now voices began to reach him too, very quiet at first, but gradually growing firmer and bolder. “So God’s judgement is not as man’s!” Father Paisy suddenly heard. The first to say this was a layman, an official from the town, a man already elderly and, as far as was known of him, very devout; but in saying it aloud he only repeated what the monks had long been repeating in each other’s ears. They had long since said this hopeless word, and worst of all was that with almost every minute a kind of triumph showed itself and grew in that word. Soon, however, even decorum itself began to be broken, and it was as if everyone felt that they now even had some right to break it. “And why should this have happened?” some of the monks said, at first as if with regret. “He had a small body, dry, his skin grown to his bones; where could the odour come from?” “It means God wanted to show us on purpose,” others hastily added, and their opinion was accepted at once and without dispute; for again they pointed out that even if there were bound to be an odour naturally, as from any departed sinner, still it would have come later, not with such obvious haste, after a day and a night at least, whereas “this one has forestalled nature”; therefore it was none other than God and his deliberate finger. He wanted to show something. This judgement struck irresistibly. The meek Father Iosif, the hieromonk and librarian, a favourite of the deceased, began objecting to some of the slanderers that “it is not so everywhere”, and that the incorruptibility of the bodies of the righteous is not a dogma of Orthodoxy but only an opinion, and that in the most Orthodox lands, on Athos for instance, they are not so troubled by an odour of corruption, and that there bodily incorruptibility is not considered the chief sign of the glorification of the saved, but the colour of their bones, when their bodies have lain many years in the earth and even decayed in it: “and if the bones are found yellow as wax, that is the chief sign that the Lord has glorified the righteous departed; but if they are found not yellow but black, it means the Lord has not deemed him worthy of such glory; that is how it is on Athos, a great place, where from of old Orthodoxy has been kept inviolate and in the brightest purity,” Father Iosif concluded. But the humble father’s words passed without effect and even provoked a mocking rebuff: “That is all learning and innovations; no need to listen,” the monks decided among themselves. “We keep to the old ways; there are all sorts of innovations coming out now; are we to copy them all?” others added. “We have had no fewer holy fathers than they. They sit there under the Turk and have forgotten everything. Their Orthodoxy has long been muddied, and they haven’t even any bells,” the most mocking ones put in. Father Iosif went away in sorrow, all the more since he himself had stated his opinion not very firmly, as if he had little faith in it himself. But he foresaw with dismay that something very unseemly was beginning and that even disobedience itself was raising its head. Little by little, after Father Iosif, all the sensible voices fell silent too. And somehow it came about that all who had loved the late elder and accepted the institution of the eldership with tender obedience were suddenly terribly frightened of something and, meeting one another, only glanced timidly into each other’s faces. And the enemies of the eldership, as an innovation, proudly raised their heads. “From the late elder Varsonofy there was not only no odour, but a fragrance came from him,” they reminded people gloatingly; “but he did not earn it by being an elder, but because he was righteous himself.” And after this, censures and even outright accusations began to pour down on the newly departed elder: “He taught wrongly; he taught that life is a great joy and not tearful humility,” said some of the most muddle-headed. “He believed in the modern way; he didn’t recognise material fire in hell,” added others still more muddle-headed. “He wasn’t strict about fasting; he allowed himself sweets; he ate cherry jam with his tea; he was very fond of it; ladies used to send it to him. Is it for a schemamonk to be drinking tea?” could be heard from some of the envious ones. “He sat in pride,” the most gloating recalled cruelly. “He thought himself a saint; people fell on their knees before him, and he accepted it as his due.” “He abused the sacrament of confession,” the fiercest opponents of the eldership added in a spiteful whisper, and these were even some of the oldest monks, most severe in their devotions, true fasters and keepers of silence, who had kept silent in the dead man’s lifetime but now suddenly opened their mouths, which was terrible indeed, for their words had a strong effect on the young monks who were not yet settled. The visitor from Obdorsk, the little monk from St Sylvester’s, listened hard to all this, sighing deeply and nodding his head: “No, it seems Father Ferapont judged rightly yesterday,” he thought to himself. And just then Father Ferapont appeared, as if he had come out precisely to make the shock worse.

I have mentioned already that he rarely came out of his wooden cell at the apiary, and even stayed away from church for long periods, and that he was allowed this as a supposed holy fool, without being bound by the rule common to all. But to tell the whole truth, all this was allowed him even from a kind of necessity. For it seemed somehow even shameful to burden with the common rule, against his will, so great a faster and keeper of silence, who prayed day and night (he even fell asleep on his knees), if he did not want to submit himself. “He is holier than all of us and does harder things than the rule requires,” the monks would have said then, “and if he doesn’t come to church, it means he knows himself when to come; he has his own rule.” It was for the sake of this likely murmuring and scandal that Father Ferapont was left in peace. Father Ferapont, as everyone knew, disliked Elder Zosima extremely; and now even to him, in his little cell, the news suddenly came that “God’s judgement is not as man’s, it seems, and that he has even forestalled nature.” It must be supposed that one of the first to run and tell him the news was the visitor from Obdorsk, who had visited him the day before and had left him in horror. I mentioned too that Father Paisy, standing firm and unshaken and reading over the coffin, though he could not hear or see what was happening outside the cell, foresaw unerringly in his heart all the main things, for he knew his surroundings through and through. He was not troubled, but awaited without fear all that might still happen, following with a piercing eye the coming outcome of the agitation, which already presented itself to his mind’s eye. Suddenly an extraordinary noise in the passage, plainly breaking decorum now, struck his ear. The door was flung wide open, and on the threshold appeared Father Ferapont. Behind him, as could be seen and even clearly made out from the cell, many monks who had come with him were crowding at the foot of the steps, and laymen among them. Those who had come with him, however, did not go in or climb the steps, but stopped and waited for what Father Ferapont would say and do next, for they felt, even with a certain fear, in spite of all their boldness, that he had not come for nothing. Stopping on the threshold, Father Ferapont raised his arms, and from under his right arm peeped the sharp, curious little eyes of the visitor from Obdorsk, the only one who had not been able to restrain himself and had run up the steps after Father Ferapont out of his great curiosity. The rest of them, on the contrary, as soon as the door was flung open with a noise, drew back still further in sudden fear. Raising his hands on high, Father Ferapont suddenly howled:

“Casting out I cast out!” And at once, turning to all four sides in turn, he began making the sign of the cross with his hand at the walls and all four corners of the cell. Those who had come with him understood this action of Father Ferapont’s at once, for they knew he always did so wherever he went in, and that he would not sit down or say a word until he had driven out the unclean spirit.

“Satan, begone! Satan, begone!” he repeated with each cross. “Casting out I cast out!” he howled again. He was in his coarse cassock, belted with a rope. From under his hempen shirt his bare chest, overgrown with grey hair, showed. His feet were quite bare. As soon as he began to wave his arms, the cruel chains he wore under his cassock began to shake and clank. Father Paisy broke off his reading, stepped forward and stood before him, waiting.

“Why have you come, honoured father? Why do you break decorum? Why do you trouble the humble flock?” he said at last, looking at him sternly.

“Why hast thou come? What dost thou ask? How dost thou believe?” Father Ferapont shouted, playing the holy fool. “I have hastened here to drive out your guests, the foul devils. I’m looking to see how many you have stored up without me. I want to sweep them out with a birch broom.”

“You drive out the unclean one, and perhaps you serve him yourself,” Father Paisy went on fearlessly. “And who can say of himself, ‘I am holy’? Can you, father?”

“I am foul, not holy. I won’t sit in an armchair and want to be bowed down to like an idol!” thundered Father Ferapont. “Nowadays folk are destroying the holy faith. The dead man, your saint,” he turned to the crowd, pointing his finger at the coffin, “rejected devils. He gave purges against devils. So they have bred here among you like spiders in the corners. And now he stinks himself. In this we see a great sign from the Lord.”

And this really had happened once in Father Zosima’s lifetime. One of the monks began to dream of the unclean spirit, and at last to see it when awake. When he disclosed this in the greatest fear to the elder, the elder advised him to pray unceasingly and to fast more strictly. But when even this did not help, he advised him, while not giving up fasting and prayer, to take a certain medicine. Many were scandalised by this at the time and talked about it among themselves, shaking their heads, and Father Ferapont most of all, to whom certain slanderers had hastened at once to report this “extraordinary” order of the elder’s in so special a case.

“Begone, father!” said Father Paisy commandingly. “It is not men who judge, but God. Perhaps we see here a ‘sign’ that neither you nor I nor anyone has the power to understand. Begone, father, and do not trouble the flock!” he repeated insistently.

“He didn’t keep the fasts according to the rule of his schema; that’s why the sign has come. That is plain, and it is a sin to hide it!” the fanatic would not calm down, carried away by a zeal that went beyond reason. “He was tempted by sweets; ladies brought them to him in their pockets; he sweetened himself with tea; he made offerings to his belly, filling it with sweet things, and his mind with proud thoughts… And so he has suffered shame…”

“Your words are frivolous, father!” Father Paisy too raised his voice. “I marvel at your fasting and your austerity, but your words are frivolous, as if a young man in the world had spoken, fickle and green. Begone, father, I command you!” Father Paisy thundered in conclusion.

“I’ll go!” said Father Ferapont, as if somewhat taken aback, but not giving up his spite. “You learned men! With your great reason you have exalted yourselves above my nothingness. I came here hardly able to read and write, and here I have forgotten what I did know; the Lord God himself has protected little me from your wisdom…”

Father Paisy stood over him and waited firmly. Father Ferapont was silent for a while, and suddenly, growing mournful and putting his right palm to his cheek, he said in a sing-song voice, looking at the coffin of the departed elder:

“Tomorrow they will sing ‘Helper and Protector’ over him, a glorious canon, but over me, when I peg out, only ‘What earthly joy’, a little hymn,” he said tearfully and ruefully. (At the carrying out of the body of a monk or schemamonk, from the cell to the church and after the funeral service from the church to the cemetery, the hymn “What earthly joy…” is sung. If the departed was a hieroschemamonk, the canon “Helper and Protector…” is sung. Author’s note.) “You have grown proud and puffed up; this place is desolate!” he suddenly howled like a madman, and, with a wave of his hand, quickly turned and quickly went down the steps. The crowd waiting below swayed; some went after him at once, but some lingered, for the cell was still open, and Father Paisy, who had come out onto the steps after Father Ferapont, stood watching. But the old man, once roused, had not finished yet. Having gone some twenty paces, he suddenly turned towards the setting sun, raised both arms above him, and, as if someone had cut his legs from under him, crashed to the ground with a great cry:

“My Lord has conquered! Christ has conquered the setting sun!” he shouted frantically, raising his arms to the sun, and, falling face down on the ground, he sobbed aloud like a little child, shaking all over with his tears and stretching out his arms on the ground. At that everyone rushed to him; there were exclamations, answering sobs… A kind of frenzy took hold of them all.

“Here is one who is holy! Here is one who is righteous!” the cries rang out, no longer fearfully. “Here is one who should be an elder,” others added, now spitefully.

“He won’t be an elder… He’ll refuse it himself… He won’t serve the cursed innovation… He won’t copy their foolery,” other voices at once took up; and how far this would have gone it is hard even to imagine, but just at that moment the bell rang for the service. Everyone suddenly began crossing themselves. Father Ferapont got up too and, protecting himself with the sign of the cross, went to his cell without looking back, still exclaiming, but now something quite incoherent. A few, a small number, began to stream after him, but most began to disperse, hurrying to the service. Father Paisy handed the reading over to Father Iosif and went down. The frenzied cries of the fanatics could not shake him, but his heart suddenly grew sad and anguished about something in particular, and he felt it. He stopped and suddenly asked himself: “Why is this sadness of mine so great as to make me lose heart?” And with surprise he understood at once that this sudden sadness of his came, it seemed, from a very small and particular cause: in the crowd that had just been thronging at the entrance to the cell he had noticed Alyosha too among the other excited ones, and he remembered that on seeing him he had at once felt a kind of pain in his heart. “Can this young man mean so much in my heart now?” he suddenly asked himself in surprise. At that moment Alyosha happened to be passing him, as if hurrying somewhere, but not towards the church. Their eyes met. Alyosha quickly looked away and dropped his eyes to the ground, and from the young man’s look alone Father Paisy guessed what a powerful change was taking place in him at this moment.

“Have you too been led astray?” Father Paisy suddenly exclaimed. “Can you too be with those of little faith?” he added sorrowfully.

Alyosha stopped and looked somehow vaguely at Father Paisy, but again quickly looked away and again dropped his eyes to the ground. He stood sideways and did not turn his face to the one who questioned him. Father Paisy watched attentively.

“Where are you hurrying to? They are ringing for the service,” he asked again, but again Alyosha gave no answer.

“Or are you leaving the hermitage? How can you, without asking leave, without a blessing?”

Alyosha suddenly gave a crooked smile, raised his eyes strangely, very strangely, to the father who was questioning him, to the one to whom his former guide, the former master of his heart and mind, his beloved elder, had entrusted him as he was dying; and suddenly, still without answering, he waved his hand, as if not caring even about respectfulness, and with quick steps went towards the gate, out of the hermitage.

“You will come back!” whispered Father Paisy, looking after him with sorrowful surprise.
