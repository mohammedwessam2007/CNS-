## CHAPTER VI

Later on Raskolnikov happened to find out why the tradesman and his wife had invited Lizaveta to their place. It was the most ordinary business and there was nothing special about it. A family that had come to town and fallen on hard times was selling things, clothes and so on, all women’s things. Since it was not profitable to sell at the market, they were looking for a dealer, and Lizaveta did that kind of work: she took commissions, went about on business and had a large practice, because she was very honest and always named the lowest price: whatever price she named, that was how it was. In general she spoke little, and, as has already been said, she was so humble and timid...

But Raskolnikov had lately become superstitious. Traces of the superstition remained in him for a long time afterwards, almost indelibly. And in this whole affair he was always inclined afterwards to see something strange, as it were, mysterious, as if particular influences and coincidences were present. As far back as the winter, a student he knew, Pokorev, when leaving for Kharkov, had happened to mention to him in conversation the address of the old woman Alyona Ivanovna, in case he should ever need to pawn anything. For a long time he did not go to her, because he had lessons and was managing somehow. About six weeks ago he remembered the address; he had two things fit for pawning: his father’s old silver watch and a little gold ring with three little red stones of some kind, given him by his sister as a keepsake when they parted. He decided to take the ring; having found the old woman, at the very first glance, before he knew anything special about her, he felt an irresistible loathing for her; he took two “little notes” from her and on the way went into a wretched little tavern. He asked for tea, sat down and fell into deep thought. A strange thought was pecking its way out in his head, like a chick out of an egg, and it very, very much absorbed him.

Almost beside him, at another little table, sat a student whom he did not know at all and did not remember, and a young officer. They had played billiards and begun to drink tea. Suddenly he heard the student telling the officer about the moneylender, Alyona Ivanovna, a collegiate secretary’s widow, and giving him her address. This alone seemed somehow strange to Raskolnikov: he had just come from there, and here they were talking about her. Of course it was chance; but he could not shake off one most unusual impression, and here someone seemed to be doing him a service: the student suddenly began telling his companion various details about this Alyona Ivanovna.

“She’s a fine one,” he was saying. “You can always get money from her. She’s as rich as a Jew, she can hand out five thousand at once, and she doesn’t turn up her nose at a pledge worth a rouble either. Lots of our fellows have been to her. Only she’s a terrible bitch...”

And he began to describe how spiteful she was, how capricious, how if you were a single day late with a pledge the thing was lost. She gave a quarter of what a thing was worth, and took five and even seven per cent a month in interest, and so on. The student chattered away and told him besides that the old woman had a sister, Lizaveta, whom she, so small and nasty as she was, beat every minute and kept in complete slavery, like a little child, though Lizaveta was at least six foot tall...

“There’s a phenomenon for you too!” cried the student, and roared with laughter.

They began to talk about Lizaveta. The student talked about her with a kind of special pleasure and kept laughing, and the officer listened with great interest and asked the student to send this Lizaveta to him to mend his linen. Raskolnikov did not miss a single word and learned everything at once: Lizaveta was the younger half-sister of the old woman (from different mothers), and she was already thirty-five. She worked for her sister day and night, served in the house as cook and laundress, and besides sewed for sale, even hired herself out to wash floors, and gave everything to her sister. She dared not take on any order or any work without the old woman’s permission. The old woman had already made her will, which Lizaveta herself knew, and by the will she was not to get a kopeck, except the movable property, the chairs and so on; all the money was left to a monastery in N. province, for the eternal repose of her soul. Lizaveta was of the tradesman class, not an official’s widow, unmarried, and terribly ungainly to look at, remarkably tall, with long, as if twisted, great feet, always in trodden-down goatskin shoes, and she kept herself clean. But what the student was most surprised at and laughed at was that Lizaveta was constantly pregnant...

“But you say she’s a fright?” the officer remarked.

“Yes, she’s so dark, like a soldier in disguise, but you know, she’s not a fright at all. She has such a kind face and eyes. Very much so. The proof is, lots of men like her. She’s so quiet, gentle, uncomplaining, willing, willing for anything. And her smile is really very nice.”

“So you like her too?” laughed the officer.

“For her strangeness. No, I’ll tell you what. I would kill that damned old woman and rob her, and I assure you, without the least prick of conscience,” the student added warmly.

The officer roared with laughter again, and Raskolnikov started. How strange it was!

“Wait, I want to ask you a serious question,” the student grew heated. “I was joking just now, of course, but look: on one side a stupid, senseless, worthless, spiteful, sick old woman, no use to anyone and, on the contrary, harmful to everyone, who does not know herself what she is living for, and who will die of herself tomorrow anyway. Do you understand? Do you understand?”

“Well, I understand,” answered the officer, staring attentively at his heated companion.

“Listen further. On the other side, young, fresh forces going to waste for lack of support, and that by the thousand, and that everywhere! A hundred, a thousand good deeds and undertakings that could be set up and put right with the old woman’s money, which is doomed to a monastery! Hundreds, perhaps thousands of lives put on the right road; dozens of families saved from destitution, from decay, from ruin, from depravity, from the venereal hospitals; and all with her money. Kill her and take her money, so as to devote yourself afterwards, with its help, to the service of all mankind and the common cause: what do you think, would not one tiny little crime be wiped out by thousands of good deeds? For one life, thousands of lives saved from rotting and decay. One death and a hundred lives in exchange: why, it’s arithmetic! And what does the life of this consumptive, stupid and spiteful old woman mean on the general scales? No more than the life of a louse, a cockroach, and not even that, because the old woman is harmful. She is eating up other people’s lives: the other day she bit Lizaveta’s finger out of spite; they nearly had to cut it off!”

“Of course she doesn’t deserve to live,” remarked the officer, “but there it is, it’s nature.”

“Eh, brother, but nature is corrected and guided, and without that we should drown in prejudices. Without that there would never have been a single great man. They say ‘duty, conscience’; I don’t want to say anything against duty and conscience; but how do we understand them? Wait, I’ll ask you another question. Listen!”

“No, you wait; I’ll ask you a question. Listen!”

“Well!”

“Here you are talking and making speeches, but tell me: will you kill the old woman yourself or not?”

“Of course not! I’m speaking of justice... It’s not a question of me...”

“Well, in my opinion, if you won’t dare do it yourself, then there’s no justice in it at all! Let’s have another game!”

Raskolnikov was in extraordinary agitation. Of course, all this was the most ordinary and most frequent young people’s talk and thought, which he had heard more than once already, only in other forms and on other subjects. But why should he have happened to hear precisely such talk and such thoughts just now, when exactly the same thoughts had just been born in his own head? And why, just now, as soon as he had carried away the germ of his thought from the old woman’s, should he hit precisely on a conversation about the old woman?.. This coincidence always seemed strange to him. This trivial tavern conversation had an extraordinary influence on him as the matter developed further: as if there really were some predestination here, some sign...

Coming back from the Haymarket, he threw himself on the sofa and sat for a whole hour without moving. Meanwhile it grew dark; he had no candle, and it did not even occur to him to light one. He could never remember whether he had been thinking about anything at that time. At last he felt the fever of earlier, the chill, and realised with pleasure that he could also lie down on the sofa. Soon a heavy, leaden sleep came down on him as if it were crushing him.

He slept an unusually long time and without dreams. Nastasya, who came in at ten o’clock the next morning, could hardly push him awake. She had brought him tea and bread. The tea was again used once already, and again in her own teapot.

“My, how he sleeps!” she cried indignantly. “He’s always asleep!”

He raised himself with an effort. His head ached; he stood up, turned round in his little room, and fell back on the sofa.

“Asleep again!” cried Nastasya. “Are you ill, or what?”

He made no answer.

“Do you want some tea?”

“Later,” he said with an effort, closing his eyes again and turning to the wall. Nastasya stood over him.

“Perhaps he really is ill,” she said, turned and went out.

She came in again at two o’clock with soup. He was lying as before. The tea stood untouched. Nastasya even took offence and began to push him angrily.

“Why are you snoring away!” she cried, looking at him with disgust. He raised himself and sat up, but said nothing to her and looked at the floor.

“Are you ill or not?” asked Nastasya, and again got no answer.

“You might at least go out into the street,” she said after a pause. “You might at least get some air. Are you going to eat, or what?”

“Later,” he said weakly. “Go away!” And he waved his hand.

She stood a little longer, looked at him with compassion, and went out.

A few minutes later he raised his eyes and looked for a long time at the tea and the soup. Then he took the bread, took the spoon and began to eat.

He ate a little, without appetite, three or four spoonfuls, as if mechanically. His head ached less. When he had eaten, he stretched out on the sofa again, but he could not sleep now; he lay without moving, face down, his face buried in the pillow. He kept dreaming, and they were such strange dreams: most often it seemed to him that he was somewhere in Africa, in Egypt, in some oasis. The caravan is resting, the camels lie quietly; palms grow all around in a whole circle; everyone is eating. But he keeps drinking water, straight from a stream which flows and murmurs right there beside him. And it is so cool, and the water is so wonderful, wonderfully blue, cold, running over stones of many colours and over such clean sand with golden sparkles... Suddenly he clearly heard a clock striking. He started, came to himself, raised his head, looked at the window, worked out the time, and suddenly jumped up, completely himself again, as if someone had torn him from the sofa. He went on tiptoe to the door, opened it softly a little way and began to listen down the stairs. His heart was beating terribly. But on the stairs everything was quiet, as if everyone were asleep... It seemed wild and strange to him that he could have slept in such oblivion since the day before and had done nothing yet, prepared nothing... And meanwhile perhaps it had struck six... And instead of sleep and stupor, an unusual, feverish and somehow bewildered bustle suddenly seized him. There were not many preparations, however. He strained every effort to think of everything and forget nothing; and his heart kept beating, thumping so that it became hard for him to breathe. First, he had to make a loop and sew it into his coat: a minute’s work. He reached under the pillow and found among the linen stuffed under it an old, unwashed shirt of his that was completely falling apart. From its rags he tore a strip about an inch and a half wide and fourteen inches long. He folded this strip in two, took off his wide, strong summer coat made of some thick cotton material (his only outer garment) and began to sew both ends of the strip under the left armpit on the inside. His hands shook as he sewed, but he managed it, and so that nothing could be seen from outside when he put the coat on again. The needle and thread had been ready for a long time and lay in the table drawer, in a piece of paper. As for the loop, it was a very clever invention of his own: the loop was meant for the axe. One could not carry an axe in one’s hands along the street. And if one hid it under one’s coat, one would still have to hold it with one’s hand, which would be noticeable. But now, with the loop, he had only to put the blade of the axe into it, and it would hang quietly, under his arm on the inside, all the way. And by putting his hand into the side pocket of his coat, he could hold the end of the axe handle too, so that it would not swing; and since the coat was very wide, a real sack, it could not be noticed from outside that he was holding something with his hand through the pocket. He had thought of this loop too, two weeks ago.

Having finished this, he pushed his fingers into a little crack between his “Turkish” sofa and the floor, felt about near the left corner and pulled out the pledge he had prepared long ago and hidden there. This pledge, however, was not a pledge at all, but simply a smoothly planed little piece of wood, no bigger or thicker than a silver cigarette case might be. He had found this piece of wood by chance on one of his walks, in a courtyard where there was some workshop in an outbuilding. Later he added to the piece of wood a smooth, thin strip of iron, probably broken off something, which he had also found in the street at the same time. Putting the two pieces together, of which the iron one was smaller than the wooden, he tied them together tightly, crosswise, with thread; then he wrapped them neatly and elegantly in clean white paper and tied it with thin tape, also crosswise, and arranged the knot so that it would be harder to untie. This was to distract the old woman’s attention for a while, when she began fiddling with the knot, and so to seize the moment. The iron plate had been added for weight, so that the old woman should not guess, at least at first, that the “thing” was wooden. All this had been kept under the sofa until the time came. He had only just taken out the pledge when suddenly someone shouted somewhere in the yard:

“It’s long past six!”

“Long past! My God!”

He rushed to the door, listened, seized his hat and began to go down his thirteen steps, carefully, soundlessly, like a cat. The most important thing lay ahead: to steal the axe from the kitchen. That the thing must be done with an axe he had decided long ago. He also had a folding garden knife; but he did not trust a knife, and especially not his own strength, and so he had settled finally on the axe. Let us note, by the way, one peculiarity of all the final decisions he had already made in this business. They had one strange property: the more final they became, the more hideous and absurd they at once became in his own eyes too. In spite of all his tormenting inner struggle, he could never, for a single moment, believe in the practicability of his plans, all this time.

And even if it had ever happened that everything had been worked out by him to the last point and finally decided, and no doubts of any kind remained, then, it seems, he would have given it all up as absurd, monstrous and impossible. But there still remained a whole abyss of unresolved points and doubts. As for where to get the axe, that trifle did not worry him at all, because nothing was easier. The point was that Nastasya, especially in the evenings, was constantly out: she would either run off to the neighbours or to the shop, and she always left the door wide open. That was the only thing the landlady quarrelled with her about. So he had only to go quietly into the kitchen when the time came and take the axe, and then, an hour later (when it was all over), go in and put it back. But doubts presented themselves too: suppose he came back an hour later to put it back, and there was Nastasya, back again. Of course he would have to walk past and wait until she went out again. But what if in the meantime she missed the axe, began to look for it, made an outcry: there was suspicion, or at least a reason for suspicion.

But these were still trifles, which he had not even begun to think about, and there was no time for them. He thought about the main thing, and put off the trifles until he had convinced himself about everything. But that seemed absolutely unattainable. So, at least, it seemed to him. He could not, for instance, imagine at all that he would one day finish thinking, get up and simply go there... Even his recent rehearsal (that is, the visit with the intention of finally looking over the place) he had only tried to make, and far from in earnest, but just like that: “Come, I’ll go and try it, why go on dreaming!” And at once he could not bear it, spat and ran away, furious with himself. And yet, it would seem, the whole analysis, in the sense of the moral resolution of the question, had already been finished by him: his casuistry had been sharpened like a razor, and he no longer found any conscious objections in himself. But in this last case he simply did not trust himself, and stubbornly, slavishly, searched for objections on all sides and by touch, as if someone were forcing him and drawing him to it. And the last day, which had come so unexpectedly and decided everything at once, acted on him almost entirely mechanically: as if someone had taken him by the hand and pulled him along, irresistibly, blindly, with unnatural force, without objections. As if he had caught a corner of his clothing in the wheel of a machine, and it had begun to draw him into it.

At first (long before, in fact) he had been occupied by one question: why are almost all crimes so easily detected and exposed, and why are the traces of almost all criminals so plainly marked? He came little by little to various and curious conclusions, and in his opinion the chief reason lay not so much in the material impossibility of hiding the crime as in the criminal himself: the criminal himself, almost every one, at the moment of the crime undergoes a kind of failure of will and reason, replaced, on the contrary, by a childish, phenomenal heedlessness, and precisely at the moment when reason and caution are most needed. By his conviction, it turned out that this eclipse of reason and failure of will take hold of a man like an illness, develop gradually, and reach their highest point shortly before the crime is committed; they continue in the same form at the very moment of the crime and for some time after it, depending on the individual; then they pass, as every illness passes. But the question whether the illness gives rise to the crime itself, or whether the crime itself, by some peculiarity of its nature, is always accompanied by something like an illness, he did not yet feel able to resolve.

Having reached such conclusions, he decided that with him personally, in his case, there could be no such sick upheavals, that his reason and will would remain with him, inalienably, all the time he was carrying out what he had planned, for the sole reason that what he had planned was “not a crime”... We pass over the whole process by which he arrived at this last decision; we have run too far ahead as it is... Let us only add that the actual, purely material difficulties of the business in general played a most secondary part in his mind. “One has only to keep all one’s will and all one’s reason over them, and they will all be overcome in their time, when one has to get to know all the details of the business down to the finest point...” But the business did not begin. He continued to believe least of all in his final decisions, and when the hour struck, everything turned out quite differently, as if by chance, even almost unexpectedly.

One most trivial circumstance stopped him dead before he was even down the stairs. As he came level with the landlady’s kitchen, open wide as always, he glanced cautiously into it, to see beforehand whether the landlady herself was there in Nastasya’s absence, and if not, whether the doors of her room were properly shut, so that she should not somehow look out from there when he went in for the axe. But what was his astonishment when he suddenly saw that Nastasya was not only at home this time, in her kitchen, but was even busy with work: taking linen out of a basket and hanging it on lines! Seeing him, she stopped hanging it, turned to him and looked at him the whole time he was passing. He turned his eyes away and walked past, as if noticing nothing. But it was all over: no axe! He was terribly struck.

“And what made me think,” he thought as he went down under the gateway, “what made me think that she would certainly not be at home at this moment? Why, why, why did I decide it so surely?” He was crushed, even somehow humiliated. He wanted to laugh at himself out of spite... A dull, brutal rage boiled up in him.

He stopped under the gateway, thinking. To go out into the street, just for appearances, to walk about, was loathsome to him; to go back home was more loathsome still. “And what a chance I have lost for ever!” he muttered, standing aimlessly under the gateway, right opposite the caretaker’s dark little room, which was also open. Suddenly he started. From the caretaker’s room, two steps away from him, from under the bench on the right, something gleamed in his eyes... He looked all around: no one. On tiptoe he went up to the caretaker’s room, went down two steps and called the caretaker in a weak voice. “Just so, he’s not in! Somewhere near, though, in the yard, because the door is wide open.” He rushed headlong at the axe (it was an axe) and pulled it out from under the bench, where it lay between two logs; right there, without going out, he fixed it in the loop, put both hands in his pockets and came out of the caretaker’s room; no one noticed! “If not reason, then the devil!” he thought, smiling strangely. This chance encouraged him extraordinarily.

He walked along quietly and sedately, without hurrying, so as not to give rise to any suspicion. He hardly looked at the passers-by, even tried not to look at faces at all and to be as inconspicuous as possible. Then he remembered his hat. “My God! And I had the money the day before yesterday, and I couldn’t change it for a cap!” A curse burst from his soul.

Glancing by chance, with one eye, into a little shop, he saw that by the clock on the wall it was already ten past seven. He had to hurry, and at the same time make a detour: come up to the house the long way round, from the other side...

Before, when he had happened to picture all this in his imagination, he had sometimes thought that he would be very afraid. But he was not very afraid now, even not afraid at all. At that moment he was even occupied by some irrelevant thoughts, only none of them for long. Passing the Yusupov Garden, he was even very much taken with the thought of building high fountains, and how well they would freshen the air in all the squares. Little by little he came to the conviction that if the Summer Garden were extended over the whole of the Field of Mars, and even joined to the garden of the Mikhailovsky Palace, it would be a splendid and most useful thing for the city. Then he was suddenly interested: why exactly, in all big cities, are people inclined, not simply from necessity but somehow particularly, to live and settle in precisely those parts of the city where there are no gardens and no fountains, where there is dirt and stench and every kind of filth? Then he remembered his own walks in the Haymarket, and for a moment he came to himself. “What nonsense,” he thought. “No, better not think about anything at all!”

“So, I suppose, those who are being led to execution fasten their thoughts on every object they meet on the way,” flashed through his head, but it only flashed like lightning; he himself quickly put out the thought... But now it was near, here was the house, here was the gateway. Somewhere a clock suddenly struck once. “What, can it be half past seven? It can’t be; it must be fast!”

Luckily for him, all went well again at the gateway. More than that, as if on purpose, at that very moment a huge load of hay had just driven in at the gate in front of him, completely screening him all the time he was passing under the archway, and as soon as the load had driven through the gateway into the yard, he slipped instantly to the right. There, on the other side of the load, several voices could be heard shouting and arguing, but no one noticed him and no one came towards him. Many windows looking onto that huge square courtyard were open at that moment, but he did not raise his head: he had not the strength. The staircase to the old woman’s was near, straight from the gateway on the right. He was already on the stairs...

Getting his breath and pressing his hand on his thumping heart, at the same time feeling for the axe and setting it straight once more, he began to climb the stairs carefully and quietly, listening every moment. But the staircase too was quite empty at that hour; all the doors were shut; he met no one. On the second floor one empty flat, it is true, stood wide open, and painters were working in it, but they did not even look. He stood still, thought, and went on. “Of course, it would be better if they were not here at all, but... there are two floors more above them.”

But here was the fourth floor, here was the door, here was the flat opposite; the empty one. On the third floor, by every sign, the flat directly under the old woman’s was empty too: the visiting card nailed to the door with little nails had been taken down: they had moved out!.. He was gasping for breath. For one instant the thought flashed through his mind: “Shouldn’t I go away?” But he gave himself no answer and began to listen at the old woman’s flat: a dead silence. Then he listened once more down the stairs, listened long and attentively... Then he looked round for the last time, pulled himself together, set himself straight and once more tried the axe in the loop. “Am I not pale... very?” he thought. “Am I not especially agitated? She is distrustful... Shouldn’t I wait a little longer... until my heart stops?..”

But his heart did not stop. On the contrary, as if on purpose, it beat harder, harder, harder... He could not bear it; he slowly stretched out his hand to the bell and rang. Half a minute later he rang again, louder.

No answer. There was no point in ringing for nothing, and it did not suit him either. The old woman, of course, was at home, but she was suspicious and alone. He knew her habits in part... and once more he put his ear close to the door. Whether his senses were so sharpened (which is hard to suppose in general), or whether it really was very audible, he suddenly made out something like a cautious rustle of a hand at the door handle and something like the rustle of a dress against the door itself. Someone was standing unnoticed right by the lock and, exactly as he was here outside, was listening, hiding inside and, it seemed, also with an ear pressed to the door...

He stirred on purpose and muttered something rather loudly, so as not to give any sign that he was hiding; then he rang a third time, but quietly, solidly and without any impatience. Remembering this afterwards, brightly, clearly (this minute was stamped on him for ever), he could not understand where he had got so much cunning, all the more since his mind seemed to grow dim for moments, and he hardly felt his body on him at all... A moment later he heard the bolt being drawn back.
