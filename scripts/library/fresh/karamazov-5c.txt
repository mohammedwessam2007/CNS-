## Chapter IV · The Confession of a Passionate Heart, in Anecdotes

“I caroused there. Father said earlier that I paid several thousand at a time for seducing girls. That is a swinish phantom, and it never happened; and what did happen did not, properly speaking, need money for ‘that’. With me money is an accessory, the fever of the soul, the setting. Today this is my lady; tomorrow a little street girl is in her place. I amuse both the one and the other, I throw money about by the handful, music, din, gypsy girls. If need be, I give it to her too, because they take it, they take it eagerly, that must be admitted, and they are pleased and grateful. Fine ladies loved me; not all, but it happened, it happened; but I always loved the little back lanes, the remote and dark little corners behind the square: there are adventures there, surprises there, nuggets in the mud. I am speaking allegorically, brother. In our little town there were no such lanes in the material sense, but there were moral ones. But if you were what I am, you would understand what these mean. I loved debauchery, and I loved the shame of debauchery too. I loved cruelty: am I not a bedbug, a wicked insect? It has been said: a Karamazov! Once the whole town went on a picnic; we went in seven troikas; in the dark, in winter, in the sledge, I began to press the hand of a neighbouring girl, and I forced kisses on that girl, an official’s daughter, poor, sweet, meek, uncomplaining. She allowed it; she allowed a lot in the dark. She thought, poor thing, that I would come the next day and make her an offer (they valued me above all as a suitor, you see); and after that I never said a word to her, not half a word for five months. I saw how her little eyes followed me from a corner of the ballroom whenever there was dancing (and with us there is nothing but dancing); I saw how they burned with a little flame, a flame of meek indignation. This game amused only the insect’s sensuality which I was feeding in myself. Five months later she married an official and went away... angry, and still loving, perhaps. Now they live happily. Note that I told no one, did not disgrace her; though I am low in my desires and love baseness, I am not dishonourable. You are blushing; your eyes have flashed. Enough of this filth for you. And all this was still only so-so, little Paul de Kock flowers, though the cruel insect was already growing, already spreading in my soul. Here, brother, there is a whole album of memories. God send them health, the darlings. When I broke off with them, I liked not to quarrel. And I never gave one away, never disgraced a single one. But enough. Did you really think I called you here only for this rubbish? No, I will tell you something more curious; but don’t be surprised that I am not ashamed before you, and even seem glad.”

“You say that because I blushed,” Alyosha remarked suddenly. “I didn’t blush at your words or for your deeds, but because I am the same as you.”

“You? Well, that’s going a bit far.”

“No, not far,” said Alyosha warmly. (Evidently the thought had long been in him.) “It is all the same steps. I am on the very lowest, and you are above, somewhere on the thirteenth. That is how I see the matter, but it is all one and the same, quite of the same kind. Whoever has stepped on the lowest step will certainly step on the highest all the same.”

“So one shouldn’t step at all?”

“Whoever can, should not step at all.”

“And can you?”

“I think not.”

“Be quiet, Alyosha, be quiet, my dear; I want to kiss your little hand, just like that, out of tenderness. That rogue Grushenka is a connoisseur of people; she told me once that some day she would eat you up. I’m quiet, I’m quiet! From the abominations, from the field fouled by flies, let us pass to my tragedy, also a field fouled by flies, that is, by every baseness. The point is that though the old man lied about the seducing of innocents, yet essentially, in my tragedy, that is how it was, though it happened only once, and even then it didn’t come off. The old man, who reproached me with a fable, doesn’t know of this very thing: I never told anyone; I’ll tell you first now, except Ivan of course; Ivan knows everything. He knew it long before you. But Ivan is a grave.”

“Ivan is a grave?”

“Yes.”

Alyosha was listening extremely attentively.

“In that line battalion, you see, though I was an ensign, I was all the same as if under supervision, like some exile. And the little town received me terribly well. I threw a lot of money about; they believed I was rich, and I believed it myself. But then I must have pleased them with something else too. Though they shook their heads, really they loved me. My lieutenant colonel, an old man already, suddenly took a dislike to me. He found fault with me; but I had a hand to back me, and besides, the whole town stood up for me, so he couldn’t find much fault. I was to blame myself too: I deliberately did not show him proper respect. I was proud. This stubborn old man, not at all a bad man and the most good-natured of hosts, had once had two wives, both dead. One, the first, was of some simple family and left him a daughter, simple too. In my time she was already a girl of about twenty-four and lived with her father together with an aunt, her late mother’s sister. The aunt was speechless simplicity, and the niece, the lieutenant colonel’s elder daughter, was lively simplicity. I like to say a good word when I remember: never, my dear, have I known a more charming woman’s character than that girl’s; her name was Agafya, just imagine, Agafya Ivanovna. And she was not at all bad-looking, in the Russian taste: tall, buxom, full-bodied, with beautiful eyes; her face, let us say, a little coarse. She did not marry, though two men proposed; she refused, and did not lose her cheerfulness. I became friends with her; not in that way, no, it was pure there, but just as friends. I often made friends with women quite sinlessly, as friends. I chatter such frank things with her that, ooh! and she only laughs. Many women love frankness, note that, and besides, she was a maiden, which amused me very much. And here is something else: she could not possibly have been called a young lady. They lived with her father, she and her aunt, somehow voluntarily lowering themselves, not putting themselves on a level with the rest of society. Everyone loved her and needed her, because she was an excellent dressmaker: she had a talent; she did not ask money for her services, she did it out of kindness, but when she was given presents she did not refuse to accept them. The lieutenant colonel, though, he was something else! The lieutenant colonel was one of the very first persons in our place. He lived in style, received the whole town, suppers, dances. When I arrived and joined the battalion, the whole little town was talking about how the lieutenant colonel’s second daughter would soon be coming to us from the capital, a beauty of beauties, who had only just left a certain aristocratic institute in the capital. This second daughter is this very Katerina Ivanovna, and she was by the lieutenant colonel’s second wife. And this second wife, already dead, came from a distinguished family, some big general’s house, though, as I know for certain, she too brought the lieutenant colonel no money at all. So she came with relations, and that was all; perhaps some expectations, but nothing in hand. And yet, when the institute girl arrived (on a visit, not for good), our whole little town seemed to come to life again; our most distinguished ladies, two Excellencies, a colonel’s wife, and everyone, everyone after them, at once took her up, snatched at her, began to entertain her; queen of balls, of picnics; they cooked up tableaux vivants for the benefit of some governesses. I am silent, I carouse; just then I pulled off a trick that set the whole town buzzing. I see that once she measured me with her eyes; it was at the battery commander’s; but I didn’t go up to her then: I disdain to make her acquaintance, so to speak. I went up to her a little later, also at a party; I began to talk; she barely looked at me, pursed her contemptuous little lips; well, I thought, wait, I’ll have my revenge! I was a most dreadful boor on most occasions then, and I felt it myself. Above all I felt that ‘Katenka’ was not exactly an innocent institute girl, but a person of character, proud and truly virtuous, and above all with intelligence and education, and I had neither one nor the other. You think I meant to make her an offer? Not in the least; I simply wanted revenge, because I was such a fine fellow and she didn’t feel it. And meanwhile, carousing and havoc. At last the lieutenant colonel put me under arrest for three days. Just at that time Father sent me six thousand, after I had sent him a formal renunciation of everything and anything, that is, we are ‘quits’, so to speak, and I won’t demand anything more. I understood nothing then: I, brother, until my very arrival here, and even until these very last days, and perhaps even until today, understood nothing about all these money disputes between Father and me. But to the devil with that; that comes later. And then, having received these six thousand, I suddenly learned for certain, through a little letter from a friend, of a most curious thing for me: namely, that people were displeased with our lieutenant colonel, that they suspected him of irregularities; in a word, that his enemies were preparing a little treat for him. And indeed the divisional commander arrived and gave him a dressing down like nothing on earth. Then a little later he was ordered to send in his resignation. I won’t tell you how it all came about in detail; he really did have enemies; only suddenly there was an excessive coolness in the town towards him and his whole family; everyone suddenly drew back, as it were. And here my first trick came off: I meet Agafya Ivanovna, with whom I had always kept up our friendship, and I say: ‘But you know, your papa is short of four thousand five hundred roubles of government money.’ ‘What are you saying, why do you say that? The general was here not long ago, it was all there...’ ‘It was then, but now it isn’t.’ She was terribly frightened: ‘Don’t frighten me, please; whom did you hear it from?’ ‘Don’t worry,’ I say, ‘I won’t tell anyone, and you know that on this score I am a grave; but here is what I wanted to add on this score, also by way, so to speak, of “just in case”: when they demand the four thousand five hundred from your papa and he doesn’t have it, then rather than go to trial and then be sent to serve as a private in his old age, better send me your institute girl then, secretly; money has just been sent to me; I’ll fork out the four thousand to her, perhaps, and keep the secret sacredly.’ ‘Ah, what a scoundrel you are,’ she says (she said just that), ‘what a wicked scoundrel! How dare you!’ She went away in terrible indignation, and I shouted after her once more that the secret would be kept sacredly and inviolably. These two women, that is, Agafya and her aunt, I will say in advance, turned out in this whole story to be pure angels, and they truly adored this sister, this proud girl, Katya; they humbled themselves before her, they were her maids... Only Agafya went and passed this thing on to her then, that is, our conversation. I learned all this afterwards as well as I know my five fingers. She didn’t hide it, and of course that was just what I wanted.

“Suddenly a new major arrives to take over the battalion. He takes it over. The old lieutenant colonel suddenly falls ill, can’t move, stays at home two days, doesn’t hand over the government money. Our doctor Kravchenko insisted that he really was ill. Only here is what I knew thoroughly, in secret, and for a long time: that every time the authorities had inspected the money, after that, and this had gone on for about four years running, the sum vanished for a while. The lieutenant colonel lent it to a most reliable man, a merchant of ours, an old widower, Trifonov, a bearded man in gold spectacles. He would go to the fair, do whatever business he needed to there, and at once return the money intact to the lieutenant colonel, and at the same time bring back presents from the fair, and with the presents a little interest. Only this time (I learned all this then quite by chance from a youngster, Trifonov’s slobbering little son, his son and heir, the most depraved boy the world ever produced), this time, I say, Trifonov, coming back from the fair, returned nothing. The lieutenant colonel rushed to him: ‘I never received anything from you, and could not have received anything’: that was the answer. Well, so our lieutenant colonel sits at home, his head tied up in a towel; all three of them are putting ice on the crown of his head; suddenly an orderly arrives with the book and an order: ‘Hand over the government money, at once, immediately, within two hours.’ He signed; I saw that signature in the book afterwards; he got up, said he was going to put on his uniform, ran to his bedroom, took his double-barrelled hunting gun, loaded it, rolled in a soldier’s bullet, took the boot off his right foot, braced the gun against his chest and began feeling for the trigger with his foot. But Agafya already suspected; she had remembered my words of that time; she stole up and spied on him in time: she burst in, threw herself on him from behind, embraced him; the gun went off upwards into the ceiling; no one was hurt; the others ran in, seized him, took away the gun, held him by the arms... I learned all this afterwards down to the last detail. I was sitting at home then; it was twilight, and I was just about to go out; I had dressed, combed my hair, scented my handkerchief, taken my cap, when suddenly the door opens and there before me, in my lodging, is Katerina Ivanovna.

“Strange things do happen: no one in the street noticed then how she came to me, so in the town it simply vanished without a trace. I was renting a lodging from two officials’ widows, very ancient old women; they waited on me; respectful women; they obeyed me in everything, and at my order they both kept silent afterwards like cast-iron posts. Of course I understood everything at once. She came in and looked straight at me; her dark eyes looked resolutely, insolently even, but in her lips and around her lips, I see, there is irresolution.

“‘My sister told me that you would give four thousand five hundred roubles if I came for them... to you myself. I have come... give me the money!..’ She couldn’t hold out; she choked, took fright, her voice broke, and the corners of her lips and the lines around her lips trembled. Alyoshka, are you listening or asleep?”

“Mitya, I know you will tell the whole truth,” Alyosha said in agitation.

“That is just what I will tell. If it’s the whole truth, then this is how it was; I won’t spare myself. My first thought was a Karamazov one. Once, brother, a phalanx spider bit me, and I lay in a fever from it for two weeks; well, so now too, suddenly, I feel the spider bite me in the heart, the wicked insect, do you understand? I measured her with my eye. Have you seen her? She is a beauty, you know. But it was not for that that she was beautiful then. She was beautiful at that moment because she was noble and I was a scoundrel, because she was in the grandeur of her magnanimity and her sacrifice for her father, and I was a bedbug. And on me, a bedbug and a scoundrel, she wholly depends, wholly, all round, soul and body. She is hemmed in. I will tell you straight: this thought, the thought of the spider, so seized my heart that it almost bled away from sheer languor. It would seem there could be no struggle at all: I should act precisely like a bedbug, like a wicked tarantula, without any pity... It even took my breath away. Listen: of course I would have gone the very next day to ask for her hand, so as to end all this in the noblest way, so to speak, and so that no one would know of it or could know. Because, though I am a man of low desires, I am honest. And suddenly at that same second someone whispered in my ear: ‘But tomorrow, when you come with an offer of your hand, such a girl will not even come out to you, but will tell the coachman to throw you out of the yard. “Go and disgrace me through the whole town, I’m not afraid of you!”’ I glanced at the girl; my voice had not lied: of course, that is just how it will be. They will throw me out by the scruff of the neck; one can judge by her face already. Spite boiled up in me; I wanted to play the vilest, piggish, shopkeeper’s trick: to look at her with a sneer, and right there, while she is standing before you, to stun her with the intonation that only a little shopkeeper knows how to put into it:

“‘Four thousand, is it! Why, I was joking, what are you thinking of? You have reckoned too credulously, madam. A couple of hundred, perhaps, even with pleasure and willingly, but four thousand is not the kind of money, miss, to be thrown away on such frivolity. You have been pleased to trouble yourself for nothing.’

“You see, I would have lost everything, of course; she would have run away; but it would have come out infernal, vengeful; it would have been worth all the rest. I would have howled all my life afterwards from remorse, but only to pull off that little trick now! Would you believe it, never has this happened to me with any woman, not a single one, that at such a moment I looked at her with hatred; and here I swear by the cross: I looked at that one then for three or five seconds with terrible hatred, with that very hatred from which to love, to the maddest love, is only a hair’s breadth! I went to the window, put my forehead to the frozen glass, and I remember that the ice burned my forehead like fire. I didn’t keep her long, don’t worry; I turned round, went to the table, opened the drawer and took out a five-thousand five-per-cent bond made out to bearer (it was lying in my French dictionary). Then I silently showed it to her, folded it, gave it to her, opened the door to the passage for her myself, and, stepping back a pace, bowed to her from the waist with a most respectful, most heartfelt bow, believe me! She trembled all over, looked at me intently for a second, turned terribly pale, well, white as a tablecloth, and suddenly, also without saying a word, not impulsively but so softly, deeply, quietly, she bent right down and fell straight at my feet, her forehead to the ground, not like an institute girl, but like a Russian! She jumped up and ran. When she ran out I was wearing my sword; I drew the sword and wanted to stab myself right there; why, I don’t know; it was terrible foolishness, of course, but it must have been from rapture. Do you understand that one can kill oneself from a certain kind of rapture? But I didn’t stab myself; I only kissed the sword and put it back in its sheath, which, by the way, I need not have mentioned to you. And even now, I think, in telling about all the struggles, I have laid it on a bit thick, to praise myself. But let it be, let it be so, and the devil take all spies of the human heart! That is the whole of my former ‘incident’ with Katerina Ivanovna. So now brother Ivan knows about it, and you, and that’s all!”

Dmitry Fyodorovich got up, took a step and another in agitation, took out his handkerchief, wiped the sweat from his forehead, then sat down again, but not in the place where he had sat before, but in another, on the bench opposite, by the other wall, so that Alyosha had to turn right round to him.
