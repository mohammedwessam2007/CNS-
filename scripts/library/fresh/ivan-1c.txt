## III

So Ivan Ilyich’s life went on for seventeen years after his marriage. He was already an experienced prosecutor who had turned down several transfers while waiting for a more desirable post, when unexpectedly an unpleasant circumstance occurred that almost destroyed the calm of his life. Ivan Ilyich was expecting the post of presiding judge in a university town, but somehow Goppe got ahead of him and obtained it. Ivan Ilyich became irritated, began to make reproaches, and quarrelled with Goppe and with his immediate superiors; they became cold towards him, and at the next appointment he was passed over again.

This was in 1880. That year was the hardest of Ivan Ilyich’s life. That year it turned out, on the one hand, that his salary was not enough to live on, and on the other, that everyone had forgotten him, and that what seemed to him the greatest and cruellest injustice towards him seemed to others a perfectly ordinary matter. Even his father did not consider it his duty to help him. He felt that everyone had abandoned him, regarding his position, with a salary of 3,500 roubles, as perfectly normal and even fortunate. He alone knew that, with the awareness of the injustices done to him, with his wife’s eternal nagging, and with the debts he had begun to run up by living beyond his means, he alone knew that his position was far from normal.

That summer, to ease his finances, he took leave and went with his wife to spend the summer in the country, at Praskovya Fyodorovna’s brother’s.

In the country, without his work, Ivan Ilyich for the first time felt not just boredom but an unbearable anguish, and decided that he could not go on living like this and that some decisive steps had to be taken.

After a sleepless night, which Ivan Ilyich spent pacing the terrace, he decided to go to Petersburg to make efforts on his own behalf and, to punish those who had failed to appreciate him, to transfer to another ministry.

The next day, in spite of all his wife’s and brother-in-law’s attempts to dissuade him, he left for Petersburg.

He went for one thing: to beg for a post with a salary of five thousand. He no longer cared about any particular ministry, political direction or kind of work. All he needed was a post, a post with five thousand, in the administration, in the banks, on the railways, in the Empress Maria’s charitable institutions, even in the customs, but it must be five thousand, and he must get out of the ministry where they had failed to appreciate him.

And this journey of Ivan Ilyich’s was crowned with amazing, unexpected success. At Kursk an acquaintance, F. S. Ilyin, got into the first-class carriage and told him of a fresh telegram received by the governor of Kursk: there was to be an upheaval in the ministry in a few days; Pyotr Ivanovich was to be replaced by Ivan Semyonovich.

The expected upheaval, besides its significance for Russia, had a special significance for Ivan Ilyich, because by bringing forward a new man, Pyotr Petrovich, and evidently his friend Zakhar Ivanovich, it was in the highest degree favourable to Ivan Ilyich. Zakhar Ivanovich was a colleague and friend of Ivan Ilyich’s.

In Moscow the news was confirmed. And on arriving in Petersburg Ivan Ilyich found Zakhar Ivanovich and received the promise of a sure post in his old Ministry of Justice.

A week later he telegraphed his wife:

“Zakhar in Miller’s place. I receive appointment at first report.”

Thanks to this change of personnel, Ivan Ilyich unexpectedly received an appointment in his old ministry which placed him two grades above his colleagues: five thousand in salary and three thousand five hundred for the expenses of moving. All his annoyance with his former enemies and with the whole ministry was forgotten, and Ivan Ilyich was completely happy.

Ivan Ilyich returned to the country cheerful and contented, as he had not been for a long time. Praskovya Fyodorovna cheered up too, and a truce was made between them. Ivan Ilyich told how everyone had honoured him in Petersburg, how all those who had been his enemies were put to shame and now fawned on him, how they envied him his position, and especially how much everyone in Petersburg loved him.

Praskovya Fyodorovna listened to this and pretended to believe it, and contradicted him in nothing, but only made plans for their new life in the town they were moving to. And Ivan Ilyich saw with joy that these plans were his plans, that they agreed, and that his life, which had stumbled, was once again taking on its true and natural character of cheerful pleasantness and decency.

Ivan Ilyich had come back only for a short time. On the 10th of September he had to take up his post, and besides, he needed time to settle into the new place, to move everything from the provinces, to buy and order a great many more things; in a word, to arrange things as they had been decided in his mind, and almost exactly as they had been decided in Praskovya Fyodorovna’s heart too.

And now, when everything had turned out so well, and when he and his wife agreed in their aims and, besides, spent little time together, they got on so well as they had not done since the first years of their married life. Ivan Ilyich had thought of taking his family with him at once, but the insistence of his sister-in-law and brother-in-law, who had suddenly become especially gracious and affectionate towards Ivan Ilyich and his family, meant that Ivan Ilyich left alone.

Ivan Ilyich left, and the cheerful state of mind produced by his success and by his harmony with his wife, each strengthening the other, did not leave him the whole time. He found a charming flat, exactly what husband and wife had dreamed of. Spacious, lofty reception rooms in the old style, a comfortable and magnificent study, rooms for his wife and daughter, a schoolroom for his son: all as if designed on purpose for them. Ivan Ilyich took charge of the furnishing himself, chose the wallpaper, bought furniture, especially antiques, to which he gave a particularly *comme il faut* style, and upholstery; and everything grew and grew and approached the ideal he had set himself. When he was halfway through, the result exceeded his expectations. He understood the *comme il faut*, elegant and not vulgar character that everything would take on when it was finished. Falling asleep, he pictured the reception room as it would be. Looking at the unfinished drawing room, he already saw the fireplace, the screen, the whatnot, and those little chairs scattered about, those dishes and plates on the walls, and the bronzes, when they were all in their places. He was delighted at the thought of how he would astonish Pasha and Lizanka, who also had taste in these things. They would never expect this. He had been particularly successful in finding and buying cheaply old things that gave everything an especially noble character. In his letters he deliberately made everything sound worse than it was, so as to astonish them. All this absorbed him so much that even his new work, though he loved it, absorbed him less than he had expected. During sessions of the court he had moments of absent-mindedness: he would be thinking about what kind of cornices to have for the curtains, straight or gathered. He was so taken up with it that he often did things himself, even moved the furniture and rehung the curtains himself. Once he climbed a ladder to show an upholsterer who did not understand how he wanted the curtains draped; he missed his footing and fell, but, being a strong and agile man, he caught himself, and only knocked his side against the handle of the window frame. The bruise hurt for a while, but soon passed. Ivan Ilyich felt especially cheerful and well all this time. He wrote: “I feel as if fifteen years had fallen off me.” He had expected to finish in September, but it dragged on until the middle of October. But it was charming: not only did he say so, but everyone who saw it told him so.

In reality it was just what is found among all people who are not quite rich, but who want to resemble the rich, and so only resemble one another: damask, ebony, flowers, carpets and bronzes, everything dark and gleaming, everything that all people of a certain kind do in order to resemble all people of a certain kind. And his was so like all the others that it could not even attract attention; but to him it all seemed something special. When he met his family at the railway station, brought them to his lit-up, finished flat, and a footman in a white tie opened the door into the hall decorated with flowers, and then they went into the drawing room and the study and gasped with pleasure, he was very happy, took them everywhere, drank in their praise and beamed with pleasure. That same evening, when Praskovya Fyodorovna asked him over tea, among other things, how he had fallen, he laughed and acted out how he had flown off the ladder and frightened the upholsterer.

“It’s not for nothing I’m an athlete. Anyone else would have been killed, but I only bumped myself here; it hurts when you touch it, but it’s passing already; it’s just a bruise.”

And they began to live in their new home, in which, as always happens once one has properly settled in, they lacked only one more room, and on their new income, which, as always, fell just a little short, some five hundred roubles; and it was very good. It was especially good at first, when not everything had been arranged and there was still arranging to do: something to buy, something to order, something to move, something to adjust. Although there were some disagreements between husband and wife, they were both so pleased and had so much to do that everything ended without big quarrels. When there was nothing left to arrange, it became a little boring and something seemed to be missing, but by then acquaintances had been made, habits formed, and life was full.

Ivan Ilyich, having spent the morning in court, came home to dinner, and at first his mood was good, although it suffered a little precisely because of the flat. (Every stain on the tablecloth or the damask, every broken curtain cord irritated him: he had put so much work into the furnishing that any damage pained him.) But in general Ivan Ilyich’s life went on as, by his creed, life ought to flow: easily, pleasantly and decently. He got up at nine, drank his coffee, read the newspaper, then put on his uniform and drove to the court. There the harness in which he worked had already been worn to shape; he slipped into it at once. Petitioners, inquiries at the office, the office itself, the sessions, public and administrative. In all this one had to know how to exclude everything raw and alive that always disturbs the regular course of official business: one must not admit any relations with people except official ones, and the reason for relations must be only official, and the relations themselves only official. For example, a man comes and wants to find out something. Ivan Ilyich, as a man not on duty, cannot have any relations with such a man; but if there is a relation to this man as to a member of the court, one that can be expressed on headed paper, then within the limits of that relation Ivan Ilyich does everything, absolutely everything, that can be done, and while doing so he keeps up the semblance of human, friendly relations, that is, politeness. As soon as the official relation ends, every other relation ends too. Ivan Ilyich possessed this skill of separating the official side, without mixing it with his real life, to the highest degree, and by long practice and talent he had developed it to such a degree that, like a virtuoso, he sometimes even allowed himself, as if in jest, to mix human and official relations. He allowed himself this because he felt he had the strength, whenever he needed to, to separate out the official part again and throw away the human. All this went on for Ivan Ilyich not only easily, pleasantly and decently, but even with virtuosity. In between he smoked, drank tea, talked a little about politics, a little about general affairs, a little about cards, and most of all about appointments. And tired, but with the feeling of a virtuoso who has played his part cleanly, one of the first violins in the orchestra, he came home. At home his daughter and her mother had gone out somewhere, or had someone visiting; his son was at school, or doing his lessons with tutors, diligently learning what is taught at school. Everything was good. After dinner, if there were no guests, Ivan Ilyich sometimes read a book that people were talking about, and in the evening sat down to work, that is, read papers, looked up the laws, compared statements and brought them under the laws. This was neither boring nor amusing to him. It was boring when he could have been playing cards; but if there was no game, it was still better than sitting alone or with his wife. Ivan Ilyich’s pleasures were little dinners, to which he invited ladies and gentlemen of social importance, and spending time with them in a way that resembled the usual way such people spend their time, just as his drawing room resembled all drawing rooms.

Once they even gave a party, with dancing. And Ivan Ilyich enjoyed himself, and everything went well, except that there was a big quarrel with his wife over the cakes and sweets: Praskovya Fyodorovna had her own plan, but Ivan Ilyich insisted on getting everything from an expensive confectioner, and ordered a great many cakes, and the quarrel was because cakes were left over and the confectioner’s bill came to forty-five roubles. The quarrel was big and unpleasant, so that Praskovya Fyodorovna said to him, “Fool, sourpuss!” And he clutched his head and in his anger said something about divorce. But the party itself was cheerful. The best society was there, and Ivan Ilyich danced with Princess Trufonova, the sister of the one known for founding the society “Bear My Sorrow Away”. The joys of his work were the joys of self-esteem; the joys of society were the joys of vanity; but Ivan Ilyich’s real joys were the joys of playing cards. He confessed that after everything, after any events at all, however joyless, in his life, the joy that burned like a candle before all the others was to sit down to a game of vint with good players and partners who did not shout, and it must be four (with five it was very painful to sit out, though you pretend you love it), and to play an intelligent, serious game (when the cards are running), then to have supper and drink a glass of wine. And after cards, especially when he had won a little (a lot was unpleasant), Ivan Ilyich went to bed in a particularly good mood.

So they lived. Their circle was made up of the very best people; important people came, and young people.

In their view of their circle of acquaintances, husband, wife and daughter were in complete agreement, and, without any discussion, they all alike edged away and freed themselves from all sorts of shabby friends and relatives who came flying in with endearments to the drawing room with Japanese plates on the walls. Soon these shabby friends stopped flying in, and only the very best society remained with the Golovins. Young men paid court to Lizanka, and Petrishchev, an examining magistrate, the son of Dmitry Ivanovich Petrishchev and the sole heir to his fortune, began to court Liza, so that Ivan Ilyich was already discussing with Praskovya Fyodorovna whether they should arrange a troika ride for them or get up some private theatricals. So they lived. And everything went on like this, without changing, and everything was very good.
