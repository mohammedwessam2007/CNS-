## Book Five: Pro and Contra · Chapter I · The Engagement

Madame Khokhlakova again met Alyosha first. She was in a hurry: something important had happened: Katerina Ivanovna’s hysterics had ended in a fainting fit, then had come “a terrible, dreadful weakness; she lay down, rolled up her eyes and began to rave. Now there is fever; they have sent for Herzenstube, sent for the aunts. The aunts are here already, but Herzenstube isn’t yet. Everyone is sitting in her room and waiting. Something will happen, and she is unconscious. And what if it is brain fever!”

Exclaiming this, Madame Khokhlakova had a seriously frightened look: “This is serious now, serious!” she added to every word, as if everything that had happened to her before had not been serious. Alyosha listened to her with sorrow; he began to tell her his own adventures, but she interrupted him at the first words: she had no time; she asked him to sit with Lise and wait for her at Lise’s.

“Lise, dearest Alexei Fyodorovich,” she whispered to him almost in his ear, “Lise surprised me strangely just now, but she moved me too, and so my heart forgives her everything. Imagine, as soon as you had gone, she suddenly began sincerely repenting that she had supposedly laughed at you yesterday and today. But she didn’t laugh, she was only joking. But she repented so seriously, almost to tears, that I was surprised. She never repented seriously before when she laughed at me, it was always in jest. And you know, she laughs at me every minute. And now she is serious; now everything has turned serious. She values your opinion extremely, Alexei Fyodorovich, and if you can, don’t be offended with her and don’t hold it against her. I do nothing but spare her myself, because she is so clever; would you believe it? She was saying just now that you were the friend of her childhood, ‘the most serious friend of my childhood’; imagine that, the most serious; and what about me? She has extremely serious feelings on that score, and even memories, and above all these phrases and little words, these most unexpected little words, so that you never expect it, and suddenly out it jumps. Not long ago about a pine tree, for instance: there was a pine tree in our garden in her early childhood, perhaps it is still standing, so there is no need to speak in the past tense. Pine trees are not people, they don’t change for a long time, Alexei Fyodorovich. ‘Mama,’ she says, ‘I remember that pine as if in a dream’, that is, ‘a pine tree from a dream’; she put it somehow differently, because there is a muddle here; ‘pine’ is a silly word, but only she told me something so original about it that I absolutely won’t undertake to repeat it. And I’ve forgotten it all anyway. Well, goodbye; I am very shaken, and I am surely going out of my mind. Ah, Alexei Fyodorovich, twice in my life I have gone out of my mind, and they treated me. Go to Lise. Cheer her up, as you always do so charmingly. Lise,” she called, going up to her door, “here I have brought you Alexei Fyodorovich, whom you insulted so, and he is not in the least angry, I assure you; on the contrary, he is surprised that you could think so!”

“Merci, maman; come in, Alexei Fyodorovich.”

Alyosha went in. Lise looked somehow embarrassed and suddenly blushed all over. She was evidently ashamed of something, and, as always happens in such cases, began very, very quickly to talk about something quite irrelevant, as if that irrelevant thing were all that interested her at that moment.

“Mama has just suddenly told me, Alexei Fyodorovich, the whole story about those two hundred roubles and about your errand... to that poor officer... and told me the whole of that dreadful story of how he was insulted, and, you know, though Mama tells things very muddle-headedly... she keeps skipping about... I listened and cried. Well, what happened, did you give him the money, and how is that unhappy man now?..”

“That’s just it, I didn’t give it to him, and there is a whole story to it,” answered Alyosha, as if for his part most concerned of all precisely that he had not given the money; and yet Lise noticed perfectly well that he too was looking aside and evidently also trying to talk about something irrelevant. Alyosha sat down at the table and began to tell her, but from the very first words he completely stopped being embarrassed and in his turn carried Lise away. He was speaking under the influence of strong feeling and of a recent extraordinary impression, and he managed to tell it well and in detail. Even before, back in Moscow, in Lise’s childhood, he had liked to come to her and tell her now what had just happened to him, now something he had read, now memories of the childhood he had lived. Sometimes they even dreamed together and made up whole stories between the two of them, but mostly merry and funny ones. Now it was as if they had both suddenly been carried back to the old Moscow days, two years before. Lise was extremely moved by his story. Alyosha managed to paint the image of “Ilyushechka” before her with warm feeling. And when he had finished, in every detail, the scene of how that unhappy man trampled the money, Lise clasped her hands and cried out with uncontrollable feeling:

“So you didn’t give him the money, so you just let him run away! My God, you should at least have run after him yourself and caught him up...”

“No, Lise, it is better that I didn’t run,” said Alyosha; he got up from his chair and walked about the room with a preoccupied air.

“How better, why better? Now they are without bread and will perish!”

“They won’t perish, because the two hundred roubles won’t pass them by all the same. He will take them tomorrow anyway. Tomorrow he will certainly take them,” said Alyosha, pacing in thought. “You see, Lise,” he went on, suddenly stopping in front of her, “I made a mistake myself here, but even the mistake turned out for the better.”

“What mistake, and why for the better?”

“Here is why: he is a cowardly man and weak in character. He is so worn out, and very kind. Now I keep thinking: why did he suddenly take such offence and trample the money? Because, I assure you, until the very last moment he did not know that he would trample it. And it seems to me that he took offence at a lot of things here... and it could not have been otherwise in his position... In the first place, he took offence just because he rejoiced too much over the money in front of me and did not hide it from me. If he had rejoiced but not very much, had not shown it, had begun to put on airs, like others, when taking money, to make faces, well, then he might still have borne it and accepted it; but he rejoiced too truthfully, and that is what is offensive. Ah, Lise, he is a truthful and kind man; that is the whole trouble in such cases! All the time he was talking then, his voice was so weak, so faint, and he talked so very quickly, kept giggling a sort of little laugh, or else was crying... really, he was crying, he was in such raptures... and he talked about his daughters... and about the post that would be given him in another town... And as soon as he had poured out his soul, he suddenly felt ashamed of having shown me his whole soul like that. So he immediately came to hate me. And he is one of those terribly shamefaced poor people. Above all, he took offence because he had accepted me as his friend too quickly and surrendered to me too quickly; first he flew at me, frightened me, and then suddenly, as soon as he saw the money, he began to embrace me. Because he did embrace me; he kept touching me with his hands. It was precisely in that position that he must have felt all that humiliation, and just then I made that mistake, a very important one: I suddenly told him that if he didn’t have enough money to move to another town, he would be given more, and that I myself would even give him as much as he liked out of my own money. That suddenly struck him: why, he thinks, did I too jump forward to help him? You know, Lise, it is terribly hard for an injured man when everyone starts looking at him as his benefactors... I have heard this; the elder told me so. I don’t know how to express it, but I have often seen it myself. And I feel exactly the same way myself, you know. And the main thing is that though he did not know until the very last moment that he would trample the notes, he still had a presentiment of it, certainly he did. That is why his rapture was so strong, because he had a presentiment... And so, though all this is so bad, still it is for the better. I even think it is for the very best; it could not have been better...”

“Why, why could it not have been better?” exclaimed Lise, looking at Alyosha in great surprise.

“Because, Lise, if he had not trampled the money but had taken it, then, coming home, within an hour or so he would have wept over his humiliation; that is certainly what would have happened. He would have wept, and perhaps tomorrow he would have come to me at dawn and thrown the notes at me, perhaps, and trampled them as he did earlier. But now he has gone away terribly proud and triumphant, though he knows that he has ‘ruined himself’. And so now nothing is easier than to make him accept those same two hundred roubles no later than tomorrow, because he has already proved his honour, thrown down the money, trampled it... He could not have known when he trampled it that I would bring it to him again tomorrow. And yet he needs that money terribly, you know. Though he is proud now, still, even today he will be thinking about what help he has lost. At night he will think about it even more, he will dream about it, and by tomorrow morning he will perhaps be ready to run to me and ask forgiveness. And that is just when I shall appear: ‘Here,’ I’ll say, ‘you are a proud man, you have proved it; well, now take it, forgive us.’ And that is when he will take it!”

Alyosha said “And that is when he will take it!” with a kind of rapture. Lise clapped her hands.

“Ah, that’s true, ah, I have suddenly understood it terribly! Ah, Alyosha, how do you know all this? So young, and he already knows what is in the soul... I would never have thought of it...”

“The main thing now is to convince him that he is on an equal footing with all of us, in spite of his taking money from us,” Alyosha went on in his rapture, “and not only on an equal footing, but even on a higher footing...”

“‘On a higher footing’, charming, Alexei Fyodorovich, but go on, go on!”

“That is, I didn’t put it right... about a higher footing... but that doesn’t matter, because...”

“Ah, it doesn’t matter, it doesn’t matter, of course it doesn’t matter! Forgive me, Alyosha, dear... You know, until now I hardly respected you... that is, I respected you, but on an equal footing, and now I shall respect you on a higher footing... Dear, don’t be angry that I am ‘being witty’,” she took up at once with strong feeling. “I am silly and little, but you, you... Listen, Alexei Fyodorovich, isn’t there in all this reasoning of ours, that is, yours... no, better ours... isn’t there contempt for him, for that unhappy man... in our analysing his soul like this now, as if from above, eh? In our having decided so surely now that he will take the money, eh?”

“No, Lise, there is no contempt,” Alyosha answered firmly, as if he had already been prepared for this question. “I thought about it myself on the way here. Consider, what contempt can there be, when we ourselves are just the same as he is, when everyone is just the same as he is? Because we are just the same, no better. And even if we were better, we would still be the same in his place... I don’t know about you, Lise, but I consider that in many ways I have a petty soul. And his is not petty; on the contrary, it is very delicate... No, Lise, there is no contempt for him here at all! You know, Lise, my elder once said: one must look after people all the time as one looks after children, and some of them as one looks after the sick in hospitals...”

“Ah, Alexei Fyodorovich, ah, my dear, let us look after people as one looks after the sick!”

“Let us, Lise; I am ready, only I am not quite ready myself; sometimes I am very impatient, and at other times I have no eyes. Now you are different.”

“Ah, I don’t believe it! Alexei Fyodorovich, how happy I am!”

“How good that you say that, Lise.”

“Alexei Fyodorovich, you are wonderfully good, but sometimes you are a sort of pedant... and yet, you look, and you are not a pedant at all. Go and look at the door, open it quietly and see whether Mama is listening,” Lise whispered suddenly in a kind of nervous, hurried whisper.

Alyosha went, opened the door a little and reported that no one was listening.

“Come here, Alexei Fyodorovich,” Lise went on, blushing more and more, “give me your hand, like this. Listen, I must make you a great confession: I did not write you yesterday’s letter in jest, but seriously...”

And she covered her eyes with her hand. It was evident that she was very ashamed to make this confession. Suddenly she seized his hand and impetuously kissed it three times.

“Ah, Lise, that’s splendid,” Alyosha exclaimed joyfully. “And I was quite sure that you wrote it seriously.”

“Sure, imagine that!” She suddenly pushed his hand away, without letting it go out of hers, however, blushing terribly and laughing a little happy laugh. “I kiss his hand, and he says ‘that’s splendid’.” But she reproached him unjustly: Alyosha too was in great confusion.

“I would like always to please you, Lise, but I don’t know how to do it,” he muttered somehow, blushing too.

“Alyosha, dear, you are cold and impudent. You see, sir? He has been pleased to choose me as his wife and has left it at that! He was already sure that I wrote it seriously; what do you think of that! But that is impudence, that’s what!”

“But is it bad that I was sure?” Alyosha suddenly laughed.

“Ah, Alyosha, on the contrary, it is terribly good,” Lise looked at him tenderly and happily. Alyosha stood there, still holding his hand in hers. Suddenly he bent down and kissed her right on the lips.

“What’s this? What’s the matter with you?” cried Lise. Alyosha was completely at a loss.

“Well, forgive me if it was wrong... Perhaps it was terribly stupid of me... You said I was cold, so I went and kissed you... Only I see it came out stupid...”

Lise laughed and covered her face with her hands.

“And in that dress!” broke from her between laughs; but suddenly she stopped laughing and became quite serious, almost stern.

“Well, Alyosha, we will wait a while with kisses, because neither of us knows how to do it yet, and we still have a very long time to wait,” she concluded suddenly. “Tell me instead why you are taking me, such a fool, a sick little fool, you, so clever, so thoughtful, so observant? Ah, Alyosha, I am terribly happy, because I am not worthy of you at all!”

“You are, Lise. In a few days I shall leave the monastery altogether. Going out into the world, one must marry; that much I know. That is what he told me to do too. Whom could I take better than you... and who would take me, except you? I have already thought it over. In the first place, you have known me since childhood, and in the second, you have very many gifts that I don’t have at all. Your soul is merrier than mine; above all, you are more innocent than I, and I have already touched on much, on very much... Ah, you don’t know, but I too am a Karamazov! What does it matter that you laugh and joke, and at me too; on the contrary, laugh, I am so glad of it... But you laugh like a little girl, and inside yourself you think like a martyr...”

“Like a martyr? How so?”

“Yes, Lise; your question earlier, whether there is contempt in us for that unhappy man, because we are dissecting his soul like that: that is a martyr’s question... you see, I can’t express it at all, but whoever has such questions is capable of suffering himself. Sitting in your chair, you must have thought a great deal already...”

“Alyosha, give me your hand; why are you taking it away?” Lise said in a little voice weakened by happiness, somehow fallen. “Listen, Alyosha, what will you wear when you leave the monastery, what kind of suit? Don’t laugh, don’t be angry, it is very, very important to me.”

“I haven’t thought about the suit yet, Lise, but I’ll wear whatever you like.”

“I want you to have a dark blue velvet jacket, a white piqué waistcoat and a soft grey felt hat... Tell me, did you really believe earlier that I didn’t love you, when I disowned yesterday’s letter?”

“No, I didn’t believe it.”

“Oh, unbearable man, incorrigible!”

“You see, I knew that you... I think you love me, but I pretended to believe you, that you don’t love me, so that it would be... more convenient for you...”

“Worse still! Both worst and best of all. Alyosha, I love you terribly. Earlier, before you came, I made a wish: I’ll ask him for yesterday’s letter, and if he calmly takes it out and gives it back to me (as one can always expect of him), then it means he doesn’t love me at all, feels nothing, and is simply a stupid and unworthy boy, and I am lost. But you left the letter in your cell, and that encouraged me: isn’t it true that you left it in your cell because you had a presentiment that I would demand the letter back, so as not to give it? Is that it? It is, isn’t it?”

“Oh, Lise, not at all; why, the letter is with me now, and it was with me earlier too, in this pocket; here it is.”

Laughing, Alyosha took out the letter and showed it to her from a distance.

“Only I won’t give it to you; look at it from my hands.”

“What? So you lied earlier; you, a monk, and you lied?”

“Perhaps I lied,” Alyosha laughed too; “I lied so as not to give you the letter. It is very dear to me,” he added suddenly, with strong feeling, and blushing again; “it is for ever, and I will never give it to anyone!”

Lise looked at him in delight.

“Alyosha,” she began to babble again, “look at the door; isn’t Mama listening?”

“Very well, Lise, I’ll look; only wouldn’t it be better not to look, eh? Why suspect your mother of such baseness?”

“What baseness? Baseness in what? Her listening to her daughter, that is her right, not baseness,” Lise flared up. “You may be sure, Alexei Fyodorovich, that when I am a mother myself and have a daughter like me, I shall certainly listen to her.”

“Really, Lise? That isn’t good.”

“Ah, my God, what baseness is there in it? If it were some ordinary society conversation and I listened in, that would be baseness, but here one’s own daughter has locked herself in with a young man... Listen, Alyosha, know that I shall spy on you too, as soon as we are married, and know too that I shall unseal all your letters and read everything... You had better be forewarned of that...”

“Yes, of course, if so...” Alyosha muttered, “only it isn’t good...”

“Ah, what contempt! Alyosha, dear, let us not quarrel from the very first time; I had better tell you the whole truth: of course it is very bad to listen at doors, and of course I am wrong and you are right, but all the same I shall listen.”

“Do. You won’t spy out anything of that kind in me,” Alyosha laughed.

“Alyosha, and will you obey me? That must be decided beforehand too.”

“With great willingness, Lise, and without fail, only not in the most important thing. In the most important thing, if you disagree with me, I shall still do as my duty tells me.”

“That is how it should be. Then know that I, on the contrary, am ready not only to obey in the most important thing, but to give way to you in everything, and I swear it to you now: in everything and for all my life,” Lise cried ardently, “and this with happiness, with happiness! More than that, I swear to you that I shall never listen in on you, never once, and shall not read a single letter of yours, because you are right and I am not. And though I shall want terribly to listen, I know it, still I won’t, because you consider it ignoble. You are like my Providence now... Listen, Alexei Fyodorovich, why have you been so sad all these days, yesterday and today? I know that you have worries and troubles, but I see besides that you have some special sadness, a secret one perhaps, eh?”

“Yes, Lise, there is a secret one too,” Alyosha said sadly. “I see that you love me, since you have guessed it.”

“What sadness? About what? Can you tell me?” Lise said with timid entreaty.

“I’ll tell you later, Lise... afterwards...” Alyosha was embarrassed. “Now, perhaps, it would not even be understood. And I might not even be able to say it myself.”

“I know, besides, that your brothers and your father are tormenting you?”

“Yes, my brothers too,” said Alyosha, as if in thought.

“I don’t like your brother Ivan Fyodorovich, Alyosha,” Lise remarked suddenly.

Alyosha noted this remark with some surprise, but did not take it up.

“My brothers are destroying themselves,” he went on, “and my father too. And they are destroying others along with them. Here is ‘the earthy Karamazov force’, as Father Paissy put it the other day: earthy and violent and unhewn... I don’t even know whether the spirit of God moves above this force. I know only that I too am a Karamazov... I am a monk, a monk? Am I a monk, Lise? You said something just now about my being a monk?”

“Yes, I did.”

“And perhaps I don’t even believe in God.”

“You don’t believe, what is the matter with you?” Lise said quietly and carefully. But Alyosha did not answer this. There was in these too sudden words of his something too mysterious and too subjective, perhaps unclear even to himself, but already undoubtedly tormenting him.

“And now, on top of everything, my friend is going, the first man in the world, he is leaving the earth. If you knew, if you only knew, Lise, how bound I am, how welded in soul to this man! And so I shall be left alone... I shall come to you, Lise... From now on we will be together...”

“Yes, together, together! From now on always together, all our lives. Listen, kiss me, I allow it.”

Alyosha kissed her.

“Well, now go, Christ be with you!” (And she made the sign of the cross over him.) “Go quickly to him while he is alive. I see that I have kept you cruelly. I shall pray today for him and for you. Alyosha, we shall be happy! We shall be happy, shan’t we?”

“I think we shall, Lise.”

On leaving Lise, Alyosha did not see fit to go in to Madame Khokhlakova, and, without saying goodbye to her, was about to leave the house. But as soon as he opened the door and came out onto the stairs, from nowhere Madame Khokhlakova herself appeared before him. From her first word Alyosha guessed that she had been waiting for him there on purpose.

“Alexei Fyodorovich, this is terrible. This is childish nonsense and all rubbish. I hope you won’t take it into your head to dream... Foolishness, foolishness and foolishness!” she fell upon him.

“Only don’t tell her that,” said Alyosha, “or she will be upset, and that is bad for her now.”

“I hear the sensible word of a sensible young man. Am I to understand that you agreed with her only because, out of compassion for her sickly state, you did not want to anger her by contradiction?”

“Oh no, not at all; I talked with her quite seriously,” Alyosha declared firmly.

“Seriousness is impossible here, unthinkable; and in the first place I shall not receive you even once now, and in the second I shall go away and take her with me, know that.”

“But why,” said Alyosha, “it is still so far off; we shall have to wait a year and a half yet, perhaps.”

“Ah, Alexei Fyodorovich, that is true, of course, and in a year and a half you will quarrel with her and part a thousand times. But I am so unhappy, so unhappy! Let it all be nonsense, but it has struck me down. Now I am like Famusov in the last scene, you are Chatsky, she is Sofya, and imagine, I ran out here onto the stairs on purpose to meet you, and there too everything fateful happened on the stairs. I heard everything; I could hardly stand. So here is the explanation of the horrors of this whole night and of all the hysterics earlier! Love for the daughter and death for the mother. Into the grave with me. Now the second and most important thing: what is this letter she wrote you? Show it to me now, now!”

“No, there is no need. Tell me how Katerina Ivanovna is; I very much need to know.”

“She is still lying delirious; she hasn’t come to; her aunts are here and only sigh and put on airs with me; and Herzenstube came and was so frightened that I didn’t know what to do with him and how to save him; I even wanted to send for a doctor. He was taken away in my carriage. And suddenly, to crown it all, you suddenly come with this letter. True, all this is still a year and a half away. In the name of everything great and holy, in the name of your dying elder, show me that letter, Alexei Fyodorovich, me, her mother! If you like, hold it in your fingers, and I will read it from your hands.”

“No, I won’t show it, Katerina Osipovna; even if she allowed it, I would not show it. I will come tomorrow and, if you like, we will talk about many things, but now, goodbye!”

And Alyosha ran down the stairs into the street.
