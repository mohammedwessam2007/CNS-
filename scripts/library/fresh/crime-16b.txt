## CHAPTER IV

Raskolnikov had been an active and spirited advocate for Sonya against Luzhin, in spite of carrying so much horror and suffering of his own in his soul. But after suffering so much that morning, he was as if glad of the chance to change his impressions, which were becoming unbearable, not to speak of how much that was personal and heartfelt lay in his impulse to stand up for Sonya. Besides, he had in view, and it alarmed him terribly, especially at moments, the coming meeting with Sonya: he had to tell her who had killed Lizaveta, and he foresaw a terrible torment for himself, and was as if waving it away with his hands. And so, when he exclaimed as he left Katerina Ivanovna’s, “Well, what will you say now, Sofya Semyonovna?”, he was evidently still in a kind of outwardly excited state of high spirits, defiance and his recent victory over Luzhin. But a strange thing happened to him. When he reached Kapernaumov’s lodging, he felt a sudden loss of strength and fear. He stopped before the door in thought, with a strange question: “Must I tell who killed Lizaveta?” The question was strange because at the same time he suddenly felt that not only was it impossible not to tell, but it was impossible even to put off the moment, even for a while. He did not yet know why it was impossible; he only felt it, and this tormenting awareness of his helplessness before necessity almost crushed him. So as not to reason and torment himself any more, he quickly opened the door and looked at Sonya from the threshold. She was sitting with her elbows on the little table and her face in her hands, but seeing Raskolnikov she got up quickly and went to meet him, as if she had been waiting for him.

“What would have become of me without you!” she said quickly, meeting him in the middle of the room. Evidently that was all she wanted to say to him as quickly as possible. That was why she had been waiting.

Raskolnikov went to the table and sat down on the chair from which she had just risen. She stood before him two steps away, exactly as yesterday.

“Well, Sonya?” he said, and suddenly felt that his voice was trembling. “The whole thing rested on ‘social position and the habits associated with it’. Did you understand that earlier?”

Suffering showed in her face.

“Only don’t talk to me as you did yesterday!” she interrupted him. “Please, don’t begin. There is enough torment as it is...”

She smiled quickly, frightened that he might not like the reproach.

“It was silly of me to leave there. What is happening there now? I was just about to go, but I kept thinking that... you would come in.”

He told her that Amalia Ivanovna was turning them out of the flat and that Katerina Ivanovna had run off somewhere “to look for justice”.

“Oh, my God!” Sonya started up. “Let us go quickly...”

And she seized her cape.

“Always the same thing!” Raskolnikov cried irritably. “They are all you have in your thoughts! Stay with me.”

“But... Katerina Ivanovna?”

“And Katerina Ivanovna, of course, won’t pass you by; she will come to you herself, since she has run out of the house,” he added peevishly. “If she doesn’t find you in, it will be you who are to blame, after all...”

Sonya sat down on a chair in tormenting indecision. Raskolnikov was silent, looking at the ground and thinking something over.

“Suppose Luzhin didn’t want to this time,” he began, without looking at Sonya. “Well, but if he had wanted to, or if it had somehow come into his calculations, he would have locked you up in prison, if Lebezyatnikov and I hadn’t happened to be there! Eh?”

“Yes,” she said in a weak voice. “Yes!” she repeated, absently and in alarm.

“And I really might not have happened to be there! And Lebezyatnikov turned up quite by chance.”

Sonya was silent.

“Well, and if it had been prison, what then? Do you remember what I said yesterday?”

Again she did not answer. He waited.

“And I thought you would cry out again: ‘Oh, don’t talk, stop it!’” Raskolnikov laughed, but somehow with a strain. “What, silence again?” he asked a minute later. “We must talk about something, mustn’t we? Now I would be particularly interested to know how you would resolve a certain ‘question’, as Lebezyatnikov says.” (He seemed to be beginning to get muddled.) “No, really, I am serious. Imagine, Sonya, that you knew all Luzhin’s intentions beforehand, knew (that is, for certain) that through them Katerina Ivanovna would perish completely, and the children too; and you as well, into the bargain (since you count yourself for nothing, so into the bargain). Polechka too... because the same road lies before her. Well, then: if suddenly all this were now left to your decision: that one or those to live in the world, that is, should Luzhin live and do vile things, or should Katerina Ivanovna die? How would you decide: which of them is to die? I am asking you.”

Sonya looked at him uneasily: she heard something peculiar in this unsteady speech, approaching something from far off.

“I had a feeling that you would ask something like that,” she said, looking at him searchingly.

“Very well, let it be so; but still, how would you decide?”

“Why do you ask what cannot be?” said Sonya with disgust.

“So it is better for Luzhin to live and do vile things! You didn’t dare decide even that?”

“But I cannot know God’s providence... And why do you ask what cannot be asked? Why such empty questions? How could it happen that it should depend on my decision? And who has made me a judge here: who is to live, who is not to live?”

“Once God’s providence gets mixed up in it, there’s nothing to be done,” Raskolnikov grumbled sullenly.

“You had better say straight out what you want!” Sonya cried in anguish. “You are leading up to something again... Can it be that you came only to torment me!”

She could not bear it and suddenly began to cry bitterly. He looked at her in gloomy anguish. About five minutes passed.

“But you are right, Sonya,” he said quietly at last. He had suddenly changed; his affectedly insolent and helplessly defiant tone had vanished. Even his voice suddenly grew weak. “I told you myself yesterday that I would not come to ask forgiveness, and I have begun almost with just that, with asking forgiveness... What I said about Luzhin and providence, I said for myself... That was me asking forgiveness, Sonya...”

He tried to smile, but something helpless and unfinished showed in his pale smile. He bowed his head and covered his face with his hands.

And suddenly a strange, unexpected sensation of a kind of caustic hatred for Sonya passed through his heart. As if surprised and frightened himself by this sensation, he suddenly raised his head and looked at her intently; but he met her uneasy look, anxious for him to the point of torment; there was love here; his hatred vanished like a phantom. That was not it; he had mistaken one feeling for another. It only meant that the moment had come.

Again he covered his face with his hands and bowed his head. Suddenly he turned pale, got up from the chair, looked at Sonya, and, without saying a word, moved mechanically to her bed and sat down on it.

This moment was terribly like, in his sensation, the moment when he had stood behind the old woman, having already freed the axe from its loop, and felt that “there was not a moment more to lose”.

“What is the matter with you?” asked Sonya, terribly frightened.

He could not utter a word. He had meant to tell her quite, quite differently, and he did not himself understand what was happening to him now. She came up to him quietly, sat down on the bed beside him and waited, not taking her eyes off him. Her heart was pounding and stopping. It became unbearable: he turned his deathly pale face to her; his lips twisted helplessly, straining to utter something. Horror passed through Sonya’s heart.

“What is the matter with you?” she repeated, drawing back from him slightly.

“Nothing, Sonya. Don’t be frightened... Nonsense! Really, if you think about it, it’s nonsense,” he muttered, like a man in delirium who does not know what he is doing. “Why have I come to torment you, of all people?” he added suddenly, looking at her. “Really. Why? I keep asking myself this question, Sonya...”

He might have been asking himself this question a quarter of an hour ago, but now he said it in complete helplessness, barely aware of himself and feeling a continuous trembling in his whole body.

“Oh, how you are suffering!” she said in anguish, gazing at him.

“It’s all nonsense!.. Here is the thing, Sonya” (he suddenly smiled for some reason, somehow palely and helplessly, for about two seconds), “do you remember what I wanted to tell you yesterday?”

Sonya waited uneasily.

“I said, as I was leaving, that perhaps I was saying goodbye to you for ever, but that if I came today, I would tell you... who killed Lizaveta.”

She suddenly trembled all over.

“Well, so I have come to tell you.”

“So you really meant it yesterday...” she whispered with difficulty. “How do you know?” she asked quickly, as if suddenly coming to herself.

Sonya began to breathe with difficulty. Her face was growing paler and paler.

“I know.”

She was silent for a minute.

“Have they found him?” she asked timidly.

“No, they haven’t.”

“Then how do you know about it?” she asked again, barely audibly, and again almost after a minute’s silence.

He turned to her and looked at her very, very intently.

“Guess,” he said, with the same twisted and helpless smile as before.

It was as if convulsions ran through her whole body.

“But you... me... why do you... frighten me like this?” she said, smiling like a child.

“So I must be a great friend of his... if I know,” Raskolnikov went on, still looking steadily into her face, as if no longer able to take his eyes away. “He did not mean to kill that Lizaveta... He killed her... by accident... He meant to kill the old woman... when she was alone... and he came... And then Lizaveta came in... And he... killed her too.”

Another terrible minute passed. They both kept looking at each other.

“So you can’t guess?” he asked suddenly, with the sensation of throwing himself down from a bell tower.

“N-no,” Sonya whispered, barely audibly.

“Look carefully.”

And as soon as he said this, again a former, familiar sensation suddenly turned his soul to ice: he looked at her and suddenly, in her face, he seemed to see Lizaveta’s face. He vividly remembered the expression on Lizaveta’s face when he had approached her that time with the axe, and she had backed away from him to the wall, putting out her hand, with completely childlike fright in her face, exactly like small children when they suddenly begin to be frightened of something, look fixedly and uneasily at the thing that frightens them, shrink back and, holding out a little hand, get ready to cry. Almost the same thing happened now with Sonya: just as helplessly, with the same fright, she looked at him for some time and suddenly, putting out her left hand, she pressed her fingers slightly, just barely, against his chest and slowly began to rise from the bed, drawing further and further away from him, and her gaze at him grew more and more fixed. Her horror suddenly communicated itself to him too: exactly the same fright appeared in his face, he too began to look at her in exactly the same way, and almost even with the same childlike smile.

“Have you guessed?” he whispered at last.

“Lord!” a terrible wail broke from her breast. She fell helplessly on the bed, face down in the pillows. But a moment later she quickly raised herself, quickly moved close to him, seized him by both hands and, squeezing them tightly, as in a vice, with her thin fingers, again began looking into his face, motionless, as if glued to it. With this last, desperate look she wanted to search out and catch at least some last hope for herself. But there was no hope; no doubt remained at all; it was all so! Even later, afterwards, when she remembered that moment, it seemed strange and wonderful to her: why exactly had she seen at once then that there was no doubt any more? She could not say, for instance, that she had had some presentiment of the kind, could she? And yet now, as soon as he had told her this, it suddenly seemed to her that she really had had a presentiment of this very thing.

“Enough, Sonya, enough! Don’t torment me!” he begged in anguish.

He had meant to tell her quite, quite differently, but it had turned out like this.

As if not knowing what she was doing, she jumped up and, wringing her hands, went to the middle of the room; but she quickly came back and sat down beside him again, almost touching him, shoulder to shoulder. Suddenly, as if pierced, she shuddered, cried out, and threw herself on her knees before him, not knowing why herself.

“What have you done, what have you done to yourself!” she said in despair, and, jumping up from her knees, she threw her arms round his neck, embraced him and held him tight, tight in her arms.

Raskolnikov drew back and looked at her with a sad smile:

“How strange you are, Sonya: you embrace me and kiss me, when I have told you about that. You don’t know what you are doing.”

“No, there is no one more unhappy than you now in the whole world!” she exclaimed, as if in a frenzy, not hearing his remark, and suddenly burst into sobs, as if in hysterics.

A feeling long unfamiliar to him flooded into his soul like a wave and softened it all at once. He did not resist it: two tears rolled out of his eyes and hung on his lashes.

“So you won’t leave me, Sonya?” he said, looking at her almost with hope.

“No, no; never and nowhere!” cried Sonya. “I will follow you, I will go everywhere! Oh Lord!.. Oh, unhappy me!.. And why, why didn’t I know you before! Why didn’t you come before? Oh Lord!”

“Well, I have come.”

“Now! Oh, what is to be done now!.. Together, together!” she repeated as if in oblivion, and embraced him again. “I will go to penal servitude with you!” Something seemed to jerk through him suddenly; the former hateful and almost haughty smile forced itself onto his lips.

“Perhaps I don’t want to go to penal servitude yet, Sonya,” he said.

Sonya looked at him quickly.

After her first passionate and tormented sympathy for the unhappy man, the terrible idea of the murder struck her again. In the changed tone of his words she suddenly heard the murderer. She looked at him in amazement. She did not yet know anything: why, or how, or for what it had been. Now all these questions flared up at once in her mind. And again she did not believe it: “He, he a murderer! Is it possible?”

“But what is this! Where am I standing!” she said in deep bewilderment, as if not yet come to herself. “But how could you, you, such a... bring yourself to do it?.. But what is this!”

“Well, yes, to rob. Stop it, Sonya!” he answered somehow wearily and even as if with vexation.

Sonya stood as if stunned, but suddenly cried out:

“You were hungry! you... to help your mother? Yes?”

“No, Sonya, no,” he muttered, turning away and hanging his head. “I wasn’t so hungry... I really did want to help my mother, but... that is not quite true either... don’t torment me, Sonya!”

Sonya clasped her hands.

“But can it be, can it be that all this is real! Lord, what kind of truth is this! Who can believe it?.. And how, how is it that you give away your last money yourself, and yet you killed to rob! Ah!..” she cried suddenly. “That money you gave Katerina Ivanovna... that money... Lord, can it be that that money too...”

“No, Sonya,” he interrupted hastily, “that was not that money; calm yourself! That money my mother sent me, through a certain merchant, and I received it when I was ill, the same day I gave it away... Razumikhin saw... it was he who received it for me... that money is mine, my own, really mine.”

Sonya listened to him in bewilderment and tried with all her might to make sense of something.

“And that money... I don’t even know, by the way, whether there was any money there,” he added quietly and as if in thought. “I took a purse off her neck then, a chamois one... a full, tight sort of purse... but I didn’t look into it; I didn’t have time, I suppose... Well, and the things, some studs and little chains, I buried all those things and the purse under a stone in a strange courtyard on V— Prospect, the very next morning. It is all lying there now.”

Sonya listened with all her might.

“But then why... how could you say: to rob, when you didn’t take anything yourself?” she asked quickly, clutching at a straw.

“I don’t know... I haven’t decided yet whether I will take that money or not,” he said, again as if in thought, and suddenly, recollecting himself, he gave a quick, short laugh. “Eh, what a stupid thing I’ve just come out with, eh?”

The thought flashed through Sonya’s mind: “Is he mad?” But she dropped it at once: no, this was something else. She understood nothing, nothing here!

“You know, Sonya,” he said suddenly, with a kind of inspiration, “you know what I will tell you: if I had only killed because I was hungry,” he went on, stressing every word and looking at her enigmatically but sincerely, “then I would be... happy now! Know that!”

“And what is it to you, what is it to you,” he cried a moment later, even with a kind of despair, “well, what is it to you if I were to confess now that I did wrong? Well, what do you want with this stupid triumph over me? Ah, Sonya, is that what I came to you for now!”

Sonya was about to say something again, but stayed silent.

“That is why I called you to come with me yesterday, because you are all I have left.”

“Called me where?” Sonya asked timidly.

“Not to steal and not to kill, don’t worry, not for that,” he smiled caustically. “We are different people... And you know, Sonya, it is only now, only this moment, that I have understood: where was I calling you yesterday? And yesterday, when I called you, I didn’t understand myself where. I called you for one thing, I came for one thing: so that you would not leave me. You won’t leave me, Sonya?”

She pressed his hand.

“And why, why did I tell her, why did I reveal it to her!” he exclaimed in despair a minute later, looking at her with infinite torment. “Here you are waiting for explanations from me, Sonya, you sit and wait, I can see it; but what am I to tell you? You will understand nothing of it, and only suffer all over... because of me! Well, there, you are crying and embracing me again; well, why are you embracing me? Because I couldn’t bear it myself and came to unload it on someone else: ‘You suffer too, it will be easier for me!’ And can you love such a scoundrel?”

“But aren’t you suffering too?” cried Sonya.

Again the same feeling flooded into his soul like a wave, and again for an instant softened it.

“Sonya, I have a wicked heart, note that: much can be explained by that. That is why I came, because I am wicked. There are people who would not have come. But I am a coward and... a scoundrel! But... let it be! all this is not it... I must speak now, and I don’t know how to begin...”

He stopped and fell to thinking.

“E-eh, we are different people!” he cried again. “Not a pair. And why, why did I come! I will never forgive myself for it!”

“No, no, it is good that you came!” exclaimed Sonya. “It is better that I should know! Much better!” He looked at her with pain.

“And what if it really is so!” he said, as if he had thought of something. “Why, that is just how it was! Here is the thing: I wanted to become a Napoleon, that is why I killed... Well, do you understand now?”

“N-no,” Sonya whispered naively and timidly, “only... speak, speak! I will understand, I will understand everything for myself!” she begged him.

“You will understand? Well, very good, we shall see!”

He was silent and thought for a long time.

“The thing is this: I once asked myself this question: what if, for example, Napoleon had found himself in my place, and he had had, to begin his career, neither Toulon, nor Egypt, nor the crossing of Mont Blanc, but instead of all those fine and monumental things simply some ridiculous little old woman, a registrar’s widow, who had to be killed besides, in order to steal money from her trunk (for his career, you understand?), well, would he have brought himself to do it, if there had been no other way out? Would he not have been squeamish, because it is far too unmonumental and... and sinful? Well, I tell you that I tormented myself over this ‘question’ terribly long, so that I felt terribly ashamed when at last I guessed (suddenly, somehow) that not only would he not have been squeamish, it would not even have entered his head that it was unmonumental... and he would not even have understood at all: what is there to be squeamish about? And if only there had been no other road for him, he would have strangled her so that she didn’t even squeak, without any brooding!.. Well, and I... came out of my brooding... strangled her... following the example of authority... And that is exactly how it was! Do you find it funny? Yes, Sonya, what is funniest of all here is that perhaps that is exactly how it was...”

Sonya did not find it at all funny.

“You had better tell me plainly... without examples,” she begged, still more timidly and barely audibly.

He turned to her, looked at her sadly and took her hands.

“You are right again, Sonya. It is all nonsense, after all, almost mere chatter! You see: you know that my mother has almost nothing. My sister received an education, by chance, and is condemned to drag about as a governess. All their hopes were in me alone. I was studying, but I couldn’t keep myself at the university and was forced to leave for a time. Even if things had dragged on like that, in ten or twelve years (if circumstances had turned out well) I could still have hoped to become some kind of teacher or official, with a salary of a thousand roubles...” (He spoke as if reciting something learned by heart.) “And by that time my mother would have withered away from care and grief, and I would still not have managed to set her at rest, and my sister... well, something even worse might have happened to my sister!.. And what is the point of passing everything by all one’s life and turning away from everything, forgetting one’s mother, and respectfully enduring the insult to one’s sister, for instance? What for? So as to bury them and acquire new ones, a wife and children, and then leave them too without a penny and without a crust? Well... well, so I decided, having taken possession of the old woman’s money, to use it for my first years, without tormenting my mother, to keep myself at the university, for my first steps after the university, and to do all this broadly, radically, so as to set up a whole new career and set out on a new, independent road... Well... well, that’s all... Well, of course, killing the old woman, that was wrong of me... well, and enough!”

In a kind of helplessness he dragged himself to the end of his story and hung his head.

“Oh, that is not it, not it,” Sonya exclaimed in anguish. “And can one really... no, it is not like that, not like that!”

“You see for yourself that it is not like that!.. And yet I told it sincerely, the truth!”

“But what kind of truth is that! Oh Lord!”

“I only killed a louse, Sonya, a useless, nasty, harmful one.”

“A human being, a louse!”

“But I know myself that she is not a louse,” he answered, looking at her strangely. “But then, I am lying, Sonya,” he added, “I have been lying for a long time... All this is not it; you are right. There are quite, quite, quite other reasons here!.. I haven’t talked with anyone for a long time, Sonya... My head aches badly now.”

His eyes were burning with a feverish fire. He was almost beginning to rave; an uneasy smile wandered on his lips. A terrible helplessness was already showing through his excited state of mind. Sonya understood how he was suffering. Her head too was beginning to spin. And he spoke so strangely: as if something were understandable, but... “But how! How! Oh Lord!” And she wrung her hands in despair.

“No, Sonya, that is not it!” he began again, suddenly raising his head, as if a sudden turn of thought had struck and excited him again. “That is not it! Better... suppose (yes! that really is better!), suppose that I am vain, envious, wicked, vile, vindictive, well... and perhaps inclined to madness besides. (Let it all come at once! They talked about madness before, I noticed!) I told you just now that I couldn’t keep myself at the university. But do you know that perhaps I could have? My mother would have sent me money to pay what was needed, and for boots, clothes and bread I could have earned it myself; surely! There were lessons to be had; they offered half a rouble each. Razumikhin works, after all! But I turned spiteful and didn’t want to. That’s it, I turned spiteful (that is a good word!). Then, like a spider, I hid myself away in my corner. You have been in my kennel, you saw it... And do you know, Sonya, that low ceilings and cramped rooms cramp the soul and the mind! Oh, how I hated that kennel! And yet I didn’t want to leave it. I didn’t want to on purpose! I didn’t go out for days on end, and I didn’t want to work, and I didn’t even want to eat; I just lay there. If Nastasya brought something, I ate; if she didn’t, the day would pass like that; I didn’t ask on purpose, out of spite! At night there was no light; I lay in the dark, and I wouldn’t earn money for candles. I ought to have been studying; I sold my books; and on my table, on my notes and exercise books, there is a finger’s depth of dust even now. I preferred to lie and think. And I kept thinking... And I kept having such dreams, strange, various dreams; no need to say what! But only then I also began to imagine that... No, that isn’t it! I am telling it wrong again! You see, I kept asking myself then: why am I so stupid that, if others are stupid and I know for certain that they are stupid, I don’t want to be cleverer myself? Then I learned, Sonya, that if one waits until everyone becomes clever, it will take far too long... Then I learned besides that it will never happen, that people won’t change, and no one can remake them, and it is not worth wasting effort on it! Yes, that is so! That is their law... A law, Sonya! That is so!.. And I know now, Sonya, that whoever is strong and powerful in mind and spirit is master over them! Whoever dares much is right in their eyes. Whoever can spit on more is their lawgiver, and whoever can dare most of all is the most right of all! So it has been until now and so it will always be! Only a blind man would not see it!”

As he said this, Raskolnikov, though he was looking at Sonya, no longer cared whether she would understand or not. The fever had taken complete hold of him. He was in a kind of gloomy rapture. (Indeed, he had not talked with anyone for too long!) Sonya understood that this gloomy catechism had become his faith and law.

“I guessed then, Sonya,” he went on rapturously, “that power is given only to the one who dares to bend down and take it. There is only one thing here, one thing: one has only to dare! A thought came to me then, for the first time in my life, which no one had ever thought of before me! No one! It suddenly appeared to me, clear as the sun: how is it that not a single person until now has dared, or dares, walking past all this absurdity, simply to take the whole thing by the tail and shake it to the devil! I... I wanted to dare, and I killed... I only wanted to dare, Sonya; that is the whole reason!”

“Oh, be quiet, be quiet!” cried Sonya, clasping her hands. “You turned away from God, and God has struck you, given you over to the devil!..”

“By the way, Sonya, when I was lying there in the dark and kept imagining things, that was the devil tempting me, was it? eh?”

“Be quiet! Don’t laugh, you blasphemer; you don’t understand anything, anything! Oh Lord! He won’t understand anything, anything!”

“Be quiet, Sonya, I am not laughing at all; I know myself that the devil was dragging me. Be quiet, Sonya, be quiet!” he repeated gloomily and insistently. “I know everything. I thought all this over and whispered it over to myself when I was lying there in the dark... I argued it all out with myself, down to the last smallest detail, and I know everything, everything! And how sick, how sick I was then of all this chatter! I kept wanting to forget it all and begin again, Sonya, and stop chattering! And do you really think I went like a fool, headlong? I went as a clever man, and that is just what ruined me! And do you really think I didn’t know, for instance, at least this: that if I had begun to ask myself and question myself: have I the right to have power? then it follows that I have no right to have power. Or that if I ask the question: is a human being a louse? then it follows that a human being is not a louse for me, but a louse for the one to whom it never even occurs, and who goes straight ahead without questions... If I tormented myself for so many days over whether Napoleon would have gone or not, then I clearly felt already that I am not a Napoleon... All, all the torment of all this chatter I endured, Sonya, and I wanted to shake it all off my shoulders: I wanted, Sonya, to kill without casuistry, to kill for myself, for myself alone! I didn’t want to lie about it even to myself! It was not to help my mother that I killed: nonsense! I did not kill so that, having got means and power, I could become a benefactor of humanity. Nonsense! I simply killed; I killed for myself, for myself alone: and whether I would then become anyone’s benefactor, or spend my whole life like a spider catching everyone in my web and sucking the living juices out of them all, at that moment it was supposed to be all the same to me!.. And it was not money, above all, that I needed, Sonya, when I killed; it was not so much money I needed as something else... I know all this now... Understand me: perhaps, going along that same road, I would never have repeated the murder again. I needed to find out something else; something else was pushing me by the arms: I needed to find out then, and to find out quickly, whether I was a louse like everyone else, or a man? Whether I can step over or not! Whether I dare bend down and take, or not? Whether I am a trembling creature, or whether I have the right...”

“To kill? You have the right to kill?” Sonya clasped her hands.

“E-eh, Sonya!” he cried irritably; he was about to object to her, but fell silent contemptuously. “Don’t interrupt me, Sonya! I only wanted to prove one thing to you: that the devil dragged me there then, and only afterwards explained to me that I had no right to go there, because I am exactly the same louse as everyone else! He made a fool of me, and so I have come to you now! Receive your guest! If I were not a louse, would I have come to you? Listen: when I went to the old woman’s that time, I only went to try... Know that!”

“And you killed! You killed!”

“But how did I kill? Is that how people kill? Do people go to kill the way I went then? Some day I will tell you how I went... Did I kill the old woman? I killed myself, not the old woman! There, at one stroke, I did away with myself, for ever!.. And that old woman, the devil killed her, not I... Enough, enough, Sonya, enough! Leave me,” he cried suddenly in convulsive anguish, “leave me!”

He leaned his elbows on his knees and squeezed his head in his palms as in pincers.

“What suffering!” a tormented wail broke from Sonya.

“Well, what is to be done now, tell me!” he asked, suddenly raising his head and looking at her with a face hideously distorted by despair.

“What is to be done!” she exclaimed, suddenly jumping up from her place, and her eyes, until now full of tears, suddenly flashed. “Stand up!” (She seized him by the shoulder; he raised himself, looking at her almost in amazement.) “Go now, this very minute, stand at the crossroads, bow down, first kiss the earth which you have defiled, and then bow down to the whole world, on all four sides, and say to everyone, aloud: ‘I have killed!’ Then God will send you life again. Will you go? Will you go?” she asked him, trembling all over, as if in a fit, seizing him by both hands, squeezing them tightly in hers and looking at him with a fiery look.

He was amazed and even struck by her sudden rapture.

“Do you mean penal servitude, Sonya? I must inform on myself, must I?” he asked gloomily.

“Accept suffering and redeem yourself by it; that is what is needed.”

“No! I will not go to them, Sonya.”

“But how will you live, how will you live? What will you live with?” exclaimed Sonya. “Is it possible now? Well, how will you talk to your mother? (Oh, what will become of them, of them, now!) But what am I saying! You have already abandoned your mother and sister. You have already abandoned them, abandoned them. Oh Lord!” she cried. “He knows all this himself already! Well, how, how can one live without a single human being! What will become of you now!”

“Don’t be a child, Sonya,” he said quietly. “How am I guilty before them? Why should I go? What shall I tell them? All this is only a phantom... They destroy people by the million themselves, and count it a virtue besides. They are rogues and scoundrels, Sonya!.. I will not go. And what shall I say: that I killed, but didn’t dare take the money, and hid it under a stone?” he added with a caustic smile. “Why, they will laugh at me themselves; they will say: a fool, not to take it. A coward and a fool! They won’t understand anything, anything, Sonya, and they are not worthy to understand. Why should I go? I won’t go. Don’t be a child, Sonya...”

“You will be worn out with torment, worn out,” she repeated, stretching out her hands to him in desperate entreaty.

“Perhaps I have slandered myself,” he remarked gloomily, as if in thought. “Perhaps I am still a man and not a louse, and was in too much of a hurry to condemn myself... I will fight on yet.”

A haughty smile forced itself onto his lips.

“To carry such torment! And for a whole life, a whole life!..”

“I’ll get used to it...” he said sullenly and thoughtfully. “Listen,” he began a minute later, “stop crying, it’s time to talk business: I came to tell you that they are looking for me now, hunting me...”

“Ah!” cried Sonya in fright.

“Well, why did you cry out! You want me to go to penal servitude yourself, and now you are frightened? Only here is the thing: I won’t give myself up to them. I will fight them yet, and they will do nothing. They have no real evidence. Yesterday I was in great danger and thought I was already lost; but today things have improved. All their evidence cuts both ways; that is, I can turn their accusations to my own advantage, do you understand? and I will turn them; because I have learned now... But they will certainly put me in prison. If it hadn’t been for one incident, they might have put me there today, certainly; perhaps they will even put me there today yet... Only that is nothing, Sonya: I’ll sit there a while, and they will let me out... because they have not a single real proof and won’t have, I give you my word. And with what they have, you can’t lock a man away. Well, enough... I only wanted you to know... With my sister and mother I will try somehow to manage to reassure them and not frighten them... My sister, though, seems to be provided for now... and so my mother too... Well, that’s all. Be careful, though. Will you come to see me in prison, when I am inside?”

“Oh, I will! I will!”

They both sat side by side, sad and crushed, as if they had been thrown up alone on an empty shore after a storm. He looked at Sonya and felt how much of her love was upon him, and strangely, he suddenly felt it heavy and painful to be loved like that. Yes, it was a strange and terrible sensation! Coming to Sonya, he had felt that all his hope and all his way out lay in her; he had thought to lay down at least part of his torment, and suddenly, now, when her whole heart had turned to him, he suddenly felt and realised that he had become incomparably more unhappy than he had been before.

“Sonya,” he said, “better not come to see me when I am in prison.”

Sonya did not answer; she was crying. Several minutes passed.

“Are you wearing a cross?” she suddenly asked unexpectedly, as if she had just remembered.

At first he did not understand the question.

“No, you aren’t, are you? Here, take this one, of cypress wood. I have another left, a copper one, Lizaveta’s. Lizaveta and I exchanged crosses; she gave me her cross, and I gave her my little icon. I will wear Lizaveta’s now, and this one is for you. Take it... it’s mine! It’s mine!” she begged. “We shall go and suffer together, and we shall bear the cross together!..”

“Give it to me!” said Raskolnikov. He did not want to grieve her. But at once he drew back the hand he had held out for the cross.

“Not now, Sonya. Better later,” he added, to calm her.

“Yes, yes, better, better,” she took up eagerly. “When you go to your suffering, then you will put it on. You will come to me, I will put it on you, we will pray and go.”

At that moment someone knocked three times at the door.

“Sofya Semyonovna, may I come in?” came a very familiar, polite voice.

Sonya rushed to the door in fright. Mr Lebezyatnikov’s fair-haired face peeped into the room.
