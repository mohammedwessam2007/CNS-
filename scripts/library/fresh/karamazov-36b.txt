## Epilogue · Chapter II · For a Moment the Lie Became Truth

He hurried to the hospital, where Mitya was now lying. On the second day after the verdict he had fallen ill with a nervous fever and been sent to our town hospital, to the prisoners’ ward. But the doctor Varvinsky, at the request of Alyosha and many others (Khokhlakova, Lise and so on), had put Mitya not with the prisoners but separately, in that very little room where Smerdyakov had lain before. True, there was a sentry at the end of the corridor, and the window had bars, and Varvinsky could feel easy about his indulgence, which was not quite legal; but he was a kind and compassionate young man. He understood how hard it was for someone like Mitya to step suddenly and directly into the company of murderers and swindlers, and that one had first to get used to it. Visits from relatives and acquaintances were permitted by the doctor, by the governor, and even by the police captain, all unofficially. But in these days only Alyosha and Grushenka had visited Mitya. Rakitin had tried twice to see him; but Mitya had insistently asked Varvinsky not to let him in.

Alyosha found him sitting on the bed, in a hospital dressing gown, slightly feverish, with his head wrapped in a towel soaked in water and vinegar. He looked at Alyosha as he came in with a vague look, but in his look there flashed, all the same, a kind of fright.

In general, ever since the trial he had become terribly thoughtful. Sometimes he would be silent for half an hour, seemingly thinking something over with difficulty and torment, forgetting whoever was there. And if he came out of his thoughtfulness and began to speak, he always began somehow suddenly, and invariably not about what he really needed to say. Sometimes he looked at his brother with suffering. With Grushenka he seemed to be more at ease than with Alyosha. True, he hardly talked with her at all, but as soon as she came in, his whole face lit up with joy. Alyosha sat down silently beside him on the bed. This time Mitya had been waiting anxiously for Alyosha, but he did not dare ask anything. He considered Katya’s consent to come unthinkable, and at the same time he felt that if she did not come, something quite impossible would happen. Alyosha understood his feelings.

“Trifon,” Mitya began fussily, “Trifon Borisych, they say, has wrecked his whole inn: he is lifting the floorboards, tearing off the planks; he has smashed the whole ‘gallery’, they say, into splinters; he is looking for treasure, those very fifteen hundred that the prosecutor said I had hidden there. As soon as he got back, they say, he started his antics at once. Serves the rogue right! The warder here told me yesterday; he is from there.”

“Listen,” said Alyosha, “she will come, but I don’t know when; perhaps today, perhaps in a few days; I don’t know that; but she will come, she will, that is certain.”

Mitya started; he was about to say something, but kept silent. The news had a terrible effect on him. It was plain that he longed tormentingly to know the details of the conversation, but that again he was afraid to ask now: anything cruel and contemptuous from Katya would have been like a knife thrust to him at this moment.

“This is what she said, among other things: that I must be sure to set your conscience at rest about the escape. If Ivan has not recovered by then, she will take it on herself.”

“You have told me that already,” Mitya remarked thoughtfully.

“And you have already repeated it to Grusha,” Alyosha remarked.

“Yes,” Mitya admitted. “She won’t come this morning,” he looked timidly at his brother. “She will only come in the evening. As soon as I told her yesterday that Katya was managing things, she was silent; and her lips twisted. She only whispered: ‘Let her!’ She understood that it is important. I didn’t dare question her further. She seems to understand now, doesn’t she, that the other one loves not me but Ivan?”

“Is that so?” broke from Alyosha.

“Perhaps it isn’t. Only she won’t come this morning,” Mitya hastened to point out once more. “I have given her an errand… Listen, brother Ivan will surpass us all. It is for him to live, not for us. He will recover.”

“Imagine, though Katya is trembling for him, she hardly doubts that he will recover,” said Alyosha.

“That means she is convinced he will die. It is from fear that she is sure he will recover.”

“Our brother has a strong constitution. And I too have great hopes that he will recover,” Alyosha remarked anxiously.

“Yes, he will recover. But she is sure he will die. She has a great deal of grief…”

There was a silence. Something very important was tormenting Mitya.

“Alyosha, I love Grusha terribly,” he said suddenly in a trembling voice, full of tears.

“They won’t let her come to you there,” Alyosha caught up at once.

“And there is something else I wanted to tell you,” Mitya went on in a voice that suddenly rang somehow. “If they beat me on the way or there, I won’t submit; I’ll kill, and they’ll shoot me. And it is twenty years! Here they are already beginning to say ‘thou’ to me. The warders say ‘thou’ to me. I lay here all night today judging myself: I am not ready! I haven’t the strength to accept it! I wanted to sing a ‘hymn’, and I can’t master the warders saying ‘thou’ to me! For Grusha I would bear everything, everything… except beatings, though… But they won’t let her come there.”

Alyosha smiled quietly.

“Listen, brother, once for all,” he said. “Here are my thoughts about it. And you know that I won’t lie to you. Listen then: you are not ready, and such a cross is not for you. More than that: you, not being ready, don’t need such a martyr’s cross. If you had killed father, I would be sorry that you rejected your cross. But you are innocent, and such a cross is too much for you. You wanted to regenerate another man in yourself through suffering; in my opinion, only remember always, all your life and wherever you escape to, that other man, and that is enough for you. Your not having accepted the great suffering of the cross will only serve to make you feel a still greater duty in yourself, and by this constant feeling henceforth, all your life, you will help your regeneration, perhaps more than if you had gone there. For there you will not endure it and will murmur, and perhaps really in the end you will say: ‘I am quits.’ The lawyer told the truth in this case. Heavy burdens are not for everyone; for some they are impossible… Those are my thoughts, if you need them so much. If others were to be held answerable for your escape, officers or soldiers, then I would ‘not allow’ you to escape,” Alyosha smiled. “But they say, and assure us (the man in charge of that halting place told Ivan himself), that with skill there may be no great penalty, and one can get off with trifles. Of course it is dishonest to bribe, even in this case; but here I won’t undertake to judge on any account, because, properly speaking, if Ivan and Katya, for instance, entrusted me with managing this business for you, then, I know it, I would go and bribe; I must tell you the whole truth about that. And so I am not your judge in how you yourself act. But know that I shall never condemn you either. And it would be strange indeed: how could I be your judge in this matter? Well, now I think I have gone over everything.”

“But I shall condemn myself!” exclaimed Mitya. “I shall escape; that was decided without you: can Mitka Karamazov not escape? But I shall condemn myself, and there I shall pray for my sin for ever! That is what the Jesuits say, isn’t it? The way we are talking now, eh?”

“Yes,” Alyosha smiled quietly.

“I love you for always telling the whole, entire truth and hiding nothing!” Mitya exclaimed, laughing joyfully. “So I have caught my Alyoshka being a Jesuit! I must kiss you all over for that, that’s what! Well, listen now to the rest; I’ll open the other half of my soul to you. This is what I have thought up and decided: if I do escape, even with money and a passport and even to America, I am still cheered by the thought that I shall be escaping not to joy, not to happiness, but truly to another penal servitude, perhaps no better than this one! No better, Alexei, I tell you truly, no better! I hate that America already, the devil take it. Suppose Grusha is with me; but look at her: is she an American? She is Russian, Russian to the bone; she will pine for her mother earth, and I shall see every hour that it is for me she is pining, for me she has taken up such a cross; and what is she guilty of? And shall I be able to bear the louts over there, though perhaps every one of them is better than I am? I hate that America already! And even if they are all, every one of them, boundless mechanics or whatever, the devil take them; they are not my people, not of my soul! I love Russia, Alexei; I love the Russian God, though I am a scoundrel myself! Why, I shall die there like a dog!” he suddenly exclaimed, his eyes flashing. His voice trembled with tears.

“Well, this is what I have decided, Alexei; listen!” he began again, suppressing his agitation. “Grusha and I will go there, and at once we’ll plough, work, with the wild bears, in solitude, somewhere far away. There must be some faraway place even there! There are still redskins there, they say, somewhere on the edge of the horizon; well then, to that edge, to the last of the Mohicans. And at once we’ll set about grammar, Grusha and I. Work and grammar, and so for about three years. In those three years we shall learn English like the most English of Englishmen. And as soon as we have learned it, an end to America! We shall run back here, to Russia, as American citizens. Don’t worry, we won’t turn up here in this little town. We shall hide somewhere far away, in the north or the south. By that time I shall have changed, and so will she; there, in America, a doctor will make me some wart or other; they are not mechanics for nothing. And if not, I’ll put out one of my eyes, grow a beard a yard long, grey (I shall go grey pining for Russia); perhaps they won’t recognise me. And if they do recognise me, let them exile me; it doesn’t matter; it means it wasn’t to be! Here too we shall plough the land somewhere in the wilds, and all my life I shall pretend to be an American. But we shall die in our native land. That is my plan, and it is unalterable. Do you approve?”

“I approve,” said Alyosha, not wishing to contradict him.

Mitya was silent for a minute and suddenly said:

“And how they framed me at the trial! How they framed me!”

“Even if they hadn’t, they would have convicted you all the same,” said Alyosha with a sigh.

“Yes, the public here are sick of me! God be with them; but it is hard!” Mitya moaned with suffering.

Again they were silent for a minute.

“Alyosha, cut my throat this minute!” he suddenly exclaimed. “Will she come now or not? Tell me! What did she say? How did she say it?”

“She said she would come, but I don’t know whether today. It is hard for her, you know!” Alyosha looked timidly at his brother.

“Well, of course it is; of course it is hard! Alyosha, I shall go mad over this. Grusha keeps looking at me. She understands. God, Lord, humble me: what am I demanding? I am demanding Katya! Do I understand what I am demanding? The Karamazov unrestraint, the ungodly kind! No, I am not capable of suffering! A scoundrel, and that’s all there is to say!”

“Here she is!” exclaimed Alyosha.

At that moment Katya suddenly appeared in the doorway. For an instant she stopped, looking at Mitya with a sort of lost look. He leapt to his feet; his face expressed fright; he turned pale; but at once a timid, pleading smile flickered on his lips, and suddenly, irresistibly, he held out both hands to Katya. Seeing this, she rushed to him. She seized him by the hands and almost by force sat him down on the bed, sat down beside him herself, and, still not letting go of his hands, pressed them tightly, convulsively. Several times both of them tried to say something, but stopped and again looked at each other silently, intently, as if riveted, with a strange smile; so two minutes passed.

“Have you forgiven me or not?” Mitya murmured at last, and at the same instant, turning to Alyosha, his face distorted with joy, he cried to him:

“Do you hear what I am asking, do you hear!”

“That is why I loved you, because your heart is generous!” suddenly broke from Katya. “And you don’t need my forgiveness, nor I yours; it is all the same whether you forgive me or not; all my life you will remain a wound in my soul, and I in yours; so it must be…” She stopped to catch her breath.

“What did I come for?” she began again, frantically and hurriedly. “To embrace your feet, to press your hands, like this, till it hurts; do you remember how I pressed them in Moscow? To tell you again that you are my god, my joy; to tell you that I love you madly,” she moaned as if in torment, and suddenly pressed her lips greedily to his hand. Tears gushed from her eyes.

Alyosha stood silent and confused; he had not at all expected what he saw.

“Love has passed, Mitya!” Katya began again. “But what has passed is dear to me to the point of pain. Know that for ever. But now, for one little minute, let there be what might have been,” she murmured with a twisted smile, again looking joyfully into his eyes. “And you love another now, and I love another, and yet I shall love you for ever, and you me; did you know that? Do you hear, love me, love me all your life!” she exclaimed with an almost threatening tremor in her voice.

“I will love you, and… you know, Katya,” Mitya began too, catching his breath at every word, “you know, five days ago, that evening, I loved you… When you fell and they carried you out… All my life! So it will be, so it will be for ever…”

So they both murmured to each other words almost senseless and frantic, perhaps even untrue; but at that moment everything was true, and they believed themselves wholeheartedly.

“Katya,” Mitya suddenly exclaimed, “do you believe I killed him? I know you don’t believe it now, but then… when you were giving evidence… Can you, can you really have believed it!”

“I didn’t believe it then either! I never believed it! I hated you, and suddenly I convinced myself, just for that moment… When I was giving evidence… I convinced myself and believed it… and as soon as I had finished giving evidence, I stopped believing it again at once. Know all that. I had forgotten that I came here to punish myself!” she said with a suddenly quite new expression, quite unlike the loving murmur of a moment before.

“It is hard for you, woman!” somehow broke from Mitya, quite unrestrainedly.

“Let me go,” she whispered. “I will come again; it is hard now!…”

She was about to rise from her place, but suddenly cried out loudly and staggered back. Grushenka had suddenly, though quite quietly, come into the room. No one was expecting her. Katya stepped quickly towards the door, but, coming level with Grushenka, she suddenly stopped, turned as white as chalk, and moaned to her quietly, almost in a whisper:

“Forgive me!”

Grushenka looked her straight in the face and, after waiting a moment, answered in a venomous voice poisoned with spite:

“We are wicked, mother, you and I! Both of us wicked! How should we forgive, you and I? Save him, and I will pray for you all my life.”

“And you won’t forgive her!” Mitya shouted to Grushenka with frantic reproach.

“Rest assured, I will save him for you!” Katya whispered quickly, and ran out of the room.

“And you could refuse to forgive her, after she herself said to you, ‘Forgive me’?” Mitya exclaimed bitterly again.

“Mitya, don’t dare reproach her; you have no right!” Alyosha shouted hotly at his brother.

“It was her proud lips that spoke, not her heart,” Grushenka said with a kind of loathing. “If she saves you, I will forgive everything…”

She fell silent, as if crushing something in her soul. She could not yet recover herself. She had come in, as it turned out afterwards, quite by chance, suspecting nothing and not expecting to meet what she met.

“Alyosha, run after her!” Mitya turned impetuously to his brother. “Tell her… I don’t know what… don’t let her go like this!”

“I’ll come to you before evening!” Alyosha cried, and ran after Katya. He caught up with her outside the hospital grounds. She was walking fast, hurrying, but as soon as Alyosha caught up with her, she said to him quickly:

“No, before that woman I can’t punish myself! I said to her ‘forgive me’ because I wanted to punish myself to the end. She didn’t forgive… I love her for that!” Katya added in a distorted voice, and her eyes flashed with savage anger.

“My brother didn’t expect it at all,” Alyosha began to mutter. “He was sure she wouldn’t come…”

“No doubt. Let us leave that,” she cut him short. “Listen: I can’t go with you to the funeral now. I have sent them flowers for the little coffin. They still have money, I think. If it is needed, tell them that I will never abandon them in future… Well, now leave me, leave me, please. You are late already; they are ringing for the late liturgy… Leave me, please!”
