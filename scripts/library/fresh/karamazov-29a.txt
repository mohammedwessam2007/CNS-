## Book Eleven, Chapter VI · The First Meeting with Smerdyakov

This was already the third time Ivan Fyodorovich had gone to talk with Smerdyakov since his return from Moscow. The first time after the catastrophe he had seen him and talked with him on the very first day of his arrival; then he had visited him once more two weeks later. But after this second time he had stopped seeing Smerdyakov, so that now it was over a month since he had seen him and he had heard almost nothing of him. Ivan Fyodorovich had come back from Moscow only on the fifth day after his father’s death, so that he did not even find the coffin: the funeral had taken place on the very day before his arrival. The reason for Ivan Fyodorovich’s delay was that Alyosha, not knowing his Moscow address exactly, had turned to Katerina Ivanovna to send the telegram, and she, also not knowing his real address, had telegraphed to her sister and aunt, reckoning that Ivan Fyodorovich would call on them as soon as he reached Moscow. But he called on them only on the fourth day after his arrival, and, reading the telegram, of course flew back to us at once at breakneck speed. Here he met Alyosha first, but, talking with him, he was very surprised that Alyosha would not even suspect Mitya, but pointed straight at Smerdyakov as the murderer, which went against all other opinion in our town. Then, after seeing the police captain and the prosecutor and learning the details of the charge and the arrest, he was still more surprised at Alyosha, and put his opinion down only to his brotherly feeling, roused to the utmost, and to his compassion for Mitya, whom, as Ivan knew, Alyosha loved very much. By the way, let us say just two words once for all about Ivan’s feelings for his brother Dmitri Fyodorovich: he decidedly did not love him, and at the very most felt compassion for him at times, but even that mixed with a great contempt that went as far as disgust. Mitya, his whole person, even his whole figure, was extremely unsympathetic to him. Katerina Ivanovna’s love for him Ivan regarded with indignation. With the accused Mitya, however, he had also had a meeting on the first day of his arrival, and this meeting not only did not weaken his conviction of his guilt but even strengthened it. He found his brother then restless, in a morbid agitation. Mitya was talkative, but scattered and disjointed; he spoke very sharply, accused Smerdyakov and got terribly muddled. Most of all he kept talking about the same three thousand that the dead man had “stolen” from him. “The money was mine, it was mine,” Mitya kept repeating; “even if I had stolen it, I would have been in the right.” He hardly disputed any of the evidence against him, and if he interpreted the facts in his own favour, again it was very confusedly and absurdly, as if in general he did not even wish to justify himself at all before Ivan or anyone; on the contrary, he was angry, proudly disdained the accusations, swore and fumed. At Grigory’s evidence about the open door he only laughed contemptuously and declared that “the devil opened it”. But he could offer no coherent explanation of this fact. In this first meeting he even managed to insult Ivan Fyodorovich, telling him sharply that it was not for those who maintain that “everything is permitted” to suspect and question him. In general he was very unfriendly to Ivan Fyodorovich this time. Immediately after this meeting with Mitya, Ivan Fyodorovich set off to see Smerdyakov.

Already in the train, flying back from Moscow, he had been thinking all the time about Smerdyakov and about his last conversation with him on the evening before his departure. Much troubled him; much seemed suspicious. But in giving his evidence to the examining magistrate, Ivan Fyodorovich had kept silent for the time being about that conversation. He had put everything off until he saw Smerdyakov. Smerdyakov was then in the town hospital. Doctor Herzenstube, and the physician Varvinsky, whom Ivan Fyodorovich met at the hospital, answered Ivan Fyodorovich’s insistent questions firmly that Smerdyakov’s falling sickness was beyond doubt, and were even surprised at the question: “Wasn’t he shamming on the day of the catastrophe?” They gave him to understand that the fit had even been an unusual one, lasting and recurring for several days, so that the patient’s life had been in decided danger, and that only now, after the measures taken, could it be said positively that he would live, though it was very possible (Doctor Herzenstube added) that his reason would remain partly disordered, “if not for life, then for a fairly long time”. To Ivan Fyodorovich’s impatient question, “Then he is mad now?”, they answered that “it isn’t so yet in the full sense, but certain abnormalities are observed.” Ivan Fyodorovich resolved to find out for himself what these abnormalities were. At the hospital he was admitted at once. Smerdyakov was in a separate room, lying on a bed. Next to him was another bed, occupied by an enfeebled townsman, all swollen with dropsy, evidently ready to die tomorrow or the day after; he could be no hindrance to their conversation. Smerdyakov grinned distrustfully on seeing Ivan Fyodorovich, and in the first instant even seemed to quail. So at least it flashed upon Ivan Fyodorovich. But that was only for an instant; for the rest of the time, on the contrary, Smerdyakov almost astonished him with his calm. From the very first glance at him, Ivan Fyodorovich was convinced beyond doubt of his complete and extreme state of illness: he was very weak, spoke slowly, as if moving his tongue with difficulty; he had grown very thin and yellow. Throughout the twenty minutes or so of their meeting he complained of headache and of aching in all his limbs. His dry, gelded face seemed to have grown so small; the hair at his temples was dishevelled, and in place of the little tuft only one thin wisp of hair stuck up. But his left eye, screwed up and as if hinting at something, betrayed the old Smerdyakov. “It is worth talking to a clever man,” Ivan Fyodorovich remembered at once. He sat down at his feet on a stool. Smerdyakov moved his whole body on the bed with suffering, but did not speak first; he was silent, and even looked as if he were not very curious.

“Can you talk to me?” asked Ivan Fyodorovich. “I won’t tire you much.”

“I can very well, sir,” Smerdyakov mumbled in a weak voice. “Have you been back long?” he added condescendingly, as though encouraging an embarrassed visitor.

“Only today… To clear up the mess you have made here.”

Smerdyakov sighed.

“Why do you sigh? You knew, didn’t you?” Ivan Fyodorovich blurted out.

Smerdyakov was solidly silent for a while.

“How could I not know, sir? It was clear beforehand. Only how could anyone know, sir, that they would take it this way?”

“Take what this way? Don’t wriggle! You predicted, didn’t you, that you would have a fit as soon as you went down into the cellar? You pointed straight at the cellar.”

“Have you already said so in your evidence?” Smerdyakov inquired calmly.

Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly grew angry.

“No, I haven’t yet, but I certainly shall. You must explain a great deal to me now, brother, and know, my dear fellow, that I won’t allow myself to be played with!”

“And why should I play such a game, sir, when all my hope is in you, as in the Lord God alone, sir!” said Smerdyakov, still quite calmly, only closing his eyes for a moment.

“In the first place,” Ivan Fyodorovich began, “I know that a fit of the falling sickness can’t be foretold. I have made inquiries; don’t wriggle. The day and the hour can’t be foretold. Then how did you foretell the day and the hour to me, and the cellar too? How could you know beforehand that you would fall into that very cellar in a fit, unless you shammed the fit on purpose?”

“I had to go to the cellar anyway, sir, even several times a day, sir,” Smerdyakov drawled unhurriedly. “In just the same way, a year ago I fell from the attic, sir. It is quite true that a fit can’t be foretold by the day and the hour, but one can always have a presentiment.”

“But you foretold the day and the hour!”

“As regards my falling sickness, sir, you had best inquire of the doctors here whether it was real or not, and I have nothing more to say to you on that subject.”

“And the cellar? How did you know about the cellar beforehand?”

“You do keep on about that cellar! When I went down into that cellar, I was in fear and in doubt; more in fear, because I had lost you and expected protection from no one in the whole world. So I go down into that very cellar and think: ‘Now it will come, now it will strike me; shall I fall or not?’ And from that very doubt the inevitable spasm suddenly seized me in the throat, sir… and down I went. All this, and the whole of our previous conversation, sir, the evening before at the gate, sir, when I told you my fear and about the cellar, sir, I disclosed all of it in detail to Doctor Herzenstube and to the investigator, Nikolai Parfenovich, and they wrote it all down in the record, sir. And the doctor here, Mr Varvinsky, particularly insisted before them all that it came about precisely from brooding, from that very apprehension, that is, ‘shall I fall or not?’ And that was when it caught hold of me. So they wrote down, sir, that it was bound to happen just so, from my fear alone, sir.”

Having said this, Smerdyakov drew a deep breath, as if exhausted.

“So you have already stated that in your evidence?” asked Ivan Fyodorovich, somewhat taken aback. He had meant to frighten him precisely by threatening to tell about their conversation that evening, and it turned out that Smerdyakov had already told everything himself.

“What have I to be afraid of? Let them write down the whole real truth,” Smerdyakov said firmly.

“And you told them everything about our conversation at the gate, word for word?”

“No, not exactly word for word, sir.”

“And did you tell them too that you know how to sham fits, as you boasted to me then?”

“No, I didn’t tell them that either, sir.”

“Tell me now: why did you send me to Chermashnya then?”

“I was afraid you would go to Moscow; Chermashnya is nearer, after all, sir.”

“You lie; you invited me to go away yourself: go away, you said, out of harm’s way!”

“I said that then out of pure friendship for you and my heartfelt devotion, foreseeing trouble in the house, sir, out of pity for you. Only I pitied myself more than you, sir. That is why I said: go away out of harm’s way, so that you would understand that things would go badly at home and would stay to protect your father.”

“Then you should have said it more plainly, you fool!” Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly flared up.

“How could I have said it more plainly then, sir? It was only fear speaking in me, sir, and you might have been angry. Of course I might have feared that Dmitri Fyodorovich would make some scandal and carry off that very money, since he regarded it as good as his own; but who could know it would end in such a murder? I thought he would simply steal the three thousand roubles that lay under the master’s mattress, sir, in an envelope, sir; and he went and killed him, sir. How could you have guessed it either, sir?”

“But if you say yourself that it couldn’t be guessed, how could I have guessed and stayed? What are you muddling?” said Ivan Fyodorovich, thinking it over.

“You might have guessed from my directing you to Chermashnya instead of Moscow, sir.”

“How could anyone guess from that!”

Smerdyakov seemed very tired and was silent again for a minute.

“You might have guessed from that very thing, sir: that if I was turning you away from Moscow to Chermashnya, then I wanted you to be nearer, because Moscow is far, and Dmitri Fyodorovich, knowing you were not far off, would not be so emboldened. And you could have come back more quickly, in case of need, and protected me too; for I pointed out to you Grigory Vasilyevich’s illness besides, and that I was afraid of a fit. And having explained to you about the knocks by which one could get in to the deceased, and that Dmitri Fyodorovich knew them all through me, I thought you would guess for yourself then that he would certainly do something, and would not only not go to Chermashnya but would stay altogether.”

“He talks very coherently,” thought Ivan Fyodorovich, “though he mumbles; what disorder of the faculties was Herzenstube talking about?”

“You are being cunning with me, damn you!” he exclaimed angrily.

“I confess, I thought then that you had guessed everything,” Smerdyakov parried with the most simple-hearted air.

“If I had guessed, I would have stayed!” cried Ivan Fyodorovich, flaring up again.

“Well, sir, and I thought that, having guessed everything, you were going away as fast as possible only to get out of harm’s way, just to run off somewhere and save yourself from fear, sir.”

“You thought everyone was as much a coward as you?”

“Forgive me, sir, I thought you were like me.”

“Of course I ought to have guessed,” Ivan said in agitation, “and I did suspect something vile on your part… Only you are lying, lying again,” he cried, suddenly remembering. “Do you remember how you came up to the carriage then and said to me: ‘It is worth talking to a clever man’? So you were glad I was going, since you praised me?”

Smerdyakov sighed again and again. A flush seemed to appear on his face.

“If I was glad,” he said, somewhat breathlessly, “it was only that you had agreed to Chermashnya and not Moscow. Because it was nearer, all the same; only I said those very words to you then not in praise, sir, but in reproach. You didn’t make that out, sir.”

“What reproach?”

“Why, that, foreseeing such a misfortune, you were leaving your own father, sir, and didn’t want to protect us, because I could always have been dragged in over those three thousand, as if I had stolen them, sir.”

“Damn you!” Ivan swore again. “Wait: did you tell the investigator and the prosecutor about the signals, about those knocks?”

“I told them everything just as it was, sir.”

Ivan Fyodorovich was surprised to himself again.

“If I thought of anything then,” he began again, “it was only of some vileness on your part. Dmitri might kill, but that he would steal, I didn’t believe then… But from you I expected every vileness. You told me yourself that you knew how to sham fits; why did you tell me that?”

“Out of pure simple-heartedness. And I never in my life shammed a fit on purpose; I only said it to boast before you. Just foolishness, sir. I had grown very fond of you then, and I was quite simple with you.”

“My brother accuses you outright of having killed and stolen.”

“Why, what else is left for him?” Smerdyakov grinned bitterly. “And who will believe him after all that evidence? Grigory Vasilyevich saw the door open, sir; after that, how can he? Well, never mind, God be with him! He is trembling to save himself…”

He was quietly silent for a while, and suddenly, as if reflecting, added:

“You see, sir, there it is again: he wants to put it on me, that it was my doing, sir; I have heard that already, sir. But take just this very thing, that I am a master at shamming fits: would I have told you beforehand that I knew how to sham, if I had really had some design on your father then? If I had planned such a murder, could I have been such a fool as to tell such evidence against myself beforehand, and to his own son, if you please, sir?! Does that sound likely? For that to be possible, sir, why, on the contrary, never, sir. Now, this conversation of ours no one hears but providence itself, sir; and if you were to tell the prosecutor and Nikolai Parfenovich, you could thereby defend me completely, sir: for what sort of villain is that, who is so simple-hearted beforehand? They can very well reason all this out.”

“Listen,” Ivan Fyodorovich got up, struck by Smerdyakov’s last argument, and broke off the conversation. “I don’t suspect you at all, and I even think it absurd to accuse you… On the contrary, I am grateful to you for having set my mind at rest. I am going now, but I shall come again. Goodbye for now; get well. Do you need anything?”

“I am grateful for everything, sir. Marfa Ignatyevna doesn’t forget me, sir, and helps me in everything, if I need anything, out of her old kindness. Good people visit me every day.”

“Goodbye. I won’t say anything, though, about your knowing how to sham… and I advise you not to mention it either,” Ivan said suddenly, for some reason.

“I understand very well, sir. And if you don’t mention that, then I won’t tell, sir, the whole of our conversation that time at the gate either…”

Here it happened that Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly went out, and only when he had gone some ten paces along the corridor did he suddenly feel that there was some insulting meaning in Smerdyakov’s last phrase. He was about to go back, but it only flashed through his mind; and, saying “Nonsense!”, he hurried out of the hospital. The main thing was that he felt he really had been set at rest, and precisely by the circumstance that it was not Smerdyakov who was guilty but his brother Mitya, though it would seem that it should have been the other way round. Why this was so he did not want to examine then; he even felt disgust at digging into his own sensations. He wanted as quickly as possible to forget something, as it were. Then, in the following few days, he became completely convinced of Mitya’s guilt, when he became more closely and thoroughly acquainted with all the overwhelming evidence against him. There was evidence from the most insignificant people, but almost staggering, for instance from Fenya and her mother. About Perkhotin, the tavern, Plotnikov’s shop, the witnesses at Mokroye, there was nothing to be said. Above all, the details were overwhelming. The news of the secret “knocks” struck the investigator and the prosecutor almost as much as Grigory’s evidence about the open door. Grigory’s wife, Marfa Ignatyevna, in answer to Ivan Fyodorovich’s question, told him straight out that Smerdyakov had lain all night behind the partition in their room, “not three steps from our bed”, and that though she herself slept soundly, she woke many times, hearing him moaning there: “He moaned all the time, moaned without stopping.” Talking with Herzenstube and telling him his opinion that Smerdyakov did not seem to him mad at all, only weak, he only drew a thin little smile from the old man. “And do you know what he is particularly busy with now?” he asked Ivan Fyodorovich. “He is learning French words by heart; he has a notebook under his pillow, and somebody has written French words in it in Russian letters, he-he-he!” At last Ivan Fyodorovich gave up all his doubts. Of his brother Dmitri he could no longer even think without loathing. One thing, however, was strange: that Alyosha stubbornly went on insisting that it was not Dmitri who had killed him, but “in all probability” Smerdyakov. Ivan had always felt that Alyosha’s opinion counted for much with him, and so he was very puzzled by him now. It was strange too that Alyosha did not seek conversations with him about Mitya and never began them himself, but only answered Ivan’s questions. Ivan Fyodorovich noticed this very much too. At that time, however, he was very much distracted by a quite unrelated circumstance: on coming back from Moscow, he had in the very first days given himself up wholly and irrevocably to his ardent and insane passion for Katerina Ivanovna. This is not the place to begin on this new passion of Ivan Fyodorovich’s, which was afterwards reflected in his whole life: all this could serve as the canvas for another story, another novel, which I do not know whether I shall ever undertake. But still I cannot keep silent even now about this: when Ivan Fyodorovich, walking at night with Alyosha from Katerina Ivanovna’s, as I have described, said to him, “I don’t care for her,” he was lying terribly at that moment: he loved her madly, though it is true too that at times he hated her so much that he could even have killed her. Many causes came together here. Shattered by the event with Mitya, she had thrown herself at Ivan Fyodorovich, who had come back to her again, as at a kind of saviour. She was hurt, insulted, humiliated in her feelings. And now there appeared again the man who had loved her so before (oh, she knew it only too well), and whose mind and heart she had always set so high above herself. But the severe young woman did not give herself up entirely as a sacrifice, in spite of all the Karamazov unrestraint of her lover’s desires and all his fascination for her. At the same time she tormented herself unceasingly with remorse for having betrayed Mitya, and in the stormy moments of quarrel with Ivan (and there were many of them) she told him so outright. That was what he called, speaking to Alyosha, “lie upon lie”. There was indeed a great deal of lying in it, and that irritated Ivan Fyodorovich most of all… but all this later. In a word, for a time he almost forgot about Smerdyakov. And yet, two weeks after his first visit to him, the same strange thoughts as before began to torment him again. It is enough to say that he began continually to ask himself why, on his last night in Fyodor Pavlovich’s house before his departure, he had gone out quietly onto the stairs, like a thief, and listened to what his father was doing below. Why had he remembered it afterwards with disgust? Why, on the next morning on the road, had he suddenly been seized with such anguish, and, coming into Moscow, said to himself: “I am a scoundrel”? And now it once occurred to him that because of all these tormenting thoughts he was ready, perhaps, to forget even Katerina Ivanovna, so strongly had they suddenly taken hold of him again! Just as he thought this, he met Alyosha in the street. He stopped him at once and suddenly asked him a question:

“Do you remember when Dmitri burst into the house after dinner and beat father, and afterwards I told you in the yard that I reserved ‘the right to wish’? Tell me, did you think then that I wished for father’s death, or not?”

“I did,” Alyosha answered quietly.

“And so it was; there was nothing to guess there. But didn’t it occur to you then too that I was wishing precisely that ‘one reptile should devour the other’, that is, that Dmitri should kill father, and soon too… and that I myself was not averse even to helping it along?”

Alyosha turned slightly pale and looked silently into his brother’s eyes.

“Speak!” exclaimed Ivan. “I want with all my strength to know what you thought then. I need it; the truth, the truth!” He drew a heavy breath, already looking at Alyosha with a kind of spite in advance.

“Forgive me, I thought that too then,” Alyosha whispered, and fell silent, without adding a single “extenuating circumstance”.

“Thank you!” Ivan snapped, and, leaving Alyosha, walked quickly on his way. From then on Alyosha noticed that his brother Ivan began somehow sharply to draw away from him and even seemed to take a dislike to him, so that afterwards he himself stopped going to see him. But at that moment, immediately after that meeting with him, Ivan Fyodorovich, without going home, suddenly set off again to see Smerdyakov.
