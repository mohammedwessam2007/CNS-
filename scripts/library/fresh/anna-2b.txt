## Chapter IX

At four o’clock, feeling his heart beating, Levin got out of the cab at the Zoological Gardens and walked along the path to the ice-hills and the skating-rink, knowing for certain that he would find her there, because he had seen the Shcherbatskys’ carriage at the entrance.

It was a clear, frosty day. At the entrance stood rows of carriages, sledges, cabmen, gendarmes. Well-dressed people, their hats shining in the bright sun, swarmed at the gate and along the swept paths between the little Russian-style houses with their carved ridges; the old curly birches of the garden, all their branches weighed down with snow, seemed to have been decked out in new and solemn vestments.

He walked along the path to the rink and said to himself: “I must not get excited, I must calm down. What’s the matter with you? What do you want? Be quiet, you fool,” he said to his heart. And the more he tried to calm himself, the worse his breath caught. An acquaintance met him and called out to him, but Levin did not even recognize who it was. He came to the ice-hills, where the chains of the sledges being let down and pulled up clanked, where the sledges rolling down rumbled and merry voices rang. He went on a few steps more, and the rink opened before him, and at once, among all the skaters, he knew her.

He knew she was there by the joy and the fear that seized his heart. She was standing at the far end of the rink, talking to a lady. There seemed to be nothing special either in her dress or in her pose; but for Levin it was as easy to know her in that crowd as a rose among nettles. Everything was lit up by her. She was the smile that brightened everything round her. “Can I really go down there, onto the ice, and go up to her?” he thought. The place where she stood seemed to him an unapproachable sanctuary, and there was a moment when he almost went away, so frightened did he become. He had to make an effort over himself and reason that all sorts of people were moving around her, and that he himself might come there to skate. He went down, avoiding looking at her for long, as one avoids the sun; but, like the sun, he saw her without looking.

On that day of the week and at that time of day, people of one circle gathered on the ice, all of them acquainted with one another. There were masters of skating there, showing off their art, and learners pushing chairs in front of them, with timid, awkward movements, and boys, and old people skating for their health; all of them seemed to Levin chosen, lucky people, because they were here, near her. All the skaters, it seemed, with perfect indifference overtook her, caught up with her, even talked to her, and enjoyed themselves quite independently of her, making use of the excellent ice and the fine weather.

Nikolai Shcherbatsky, Kitty’s cousin, in a short jacket and tight trousers, was sitting on a bench with his skates on, and, seeing Levin, shouted to him:

“Ah, the first skater in Russia! Have you been here long? The ice is excellent; come on, put your skates on.”

“I haven’t even got any skates,” answered Levin, marvelling at this boldness and ease in her presence, and not for a second losing sight of her, though he did not look at her. He felt that the sun was coming closer to him. She was at a corner and, setting her narrow feet in their high boots at a blunt angle, evidently timid, was skating towards him. A boy in Russian dress, waving his arms desperately and bending low to the ice, was overtaking her. She did not skate quite steadily; taking her hands out of the little muff that hung on a cord, she held them ready, and, looking at Levin, whom she had recognized, she smiled at him and at her own fear. When the turn was over, she gave herself a push with her springy little foot and glided straight up to Shcherbatsky; and, catching hold of him with one hand, she nodded to Levin, smiling. She was more beautiful than he had imagined her.

When he thought of her, he could picture all of her vividly to himself, especially the charm of that small fair head, with its expression of childlike clearness and kindness, set so freely on her shapely girlish shoulders. The childlike look of her face, together with the delicate beauty of her figure, made up her special charm, which he remembered well; but what always struck him in her, like something unexpected, was the expression of her eyes, gentle, calm and truthful, and above all her smile, which always carried Levin into an enchanted world, where he felt himself touched and softened, as he could remember himself on rare days of his early childhood.

“Have you been here long?” she said, giving him her hand. “Thank you,” she added, when he picked up the handkerchief that had fallen from her muff.

“I? I’ve only just come, I came yesterday… today, I mean,” answered Levin, who in his agitation had not at once understood her question. “I meant to call on you,” he said; and at once, remembering with what intention he had been looking for her, he was confused and blushed. “I didn’t know you skated, and skated so well.”

She looked at him attentively, as though wanting to understand the cause of his confusion.

“Your praise is worth having. There is a tradition here that you are the best skater,” she said, brushing off with her little black-gloved hand the needles of hoar-frost that had fallen on her muff.

“Yes, at one time I skated with passion; I wanted to reach perfection.”

“You do everything with passion, it seems,” she said, smiling. “I so want to see how you skate. Do put on some skates, and let us skate together.”

“Skate together! Can it be possible?” thought Levin, looking at her.

“I’ll put them on at once,” he said.

And he went off to put on skates.

“You haven’t been to see us for a long time, sir,” said the attendant, holding up his foot and screwing on the heel. “Since you, there hasn’t been a single master among the gentlemen. Will that be all right?” he said, tightening the strap.

“All right, all right, quickly, please,” answered Levin, with difficulty holding back the smile of happiness that came out on his face in spite of him. “Yes,” he thought, “this is life, this is happiness! Together, she said, let us skate together. Shall I tell her now? But it’s precisely because I’m happy now that I’m afraid to tell her, happy if only with hope… And then?… But I must! I must, I must! Away with weakness!”

Levin stood up, took off his overcoat and, taking a run over the rough ice by the hut, came out onto the smooth ice and glided along without effort, as though quickening, shortening and steering his course by will alone. He came up to her timidly, but again her smile calmed him.

She gave him her hand, and they went side by side, gathering speed; and the faster they went, the more tightly she pressed his hand.

“With you I should learn sooner; for some reason I feel sure of you,” she said to him.

“And I feel sure of myself when you lean on me,” he said, but at once took fright at what he had said and blushed. And indeed, as soon as he had spoken these words, suddenly, as when the sun goes behind a cloud, her face lost all its warmth, and Levin recognized the familiar play of her face that meant an effort of thought: a little wrinkle swelled on her smooth forehead.

“Is anything troubling you? But I have no right to ask,” he said quickly.

“Why not?… No, nothing is troubling me,” she answered coldly, and at once added: “Have you seen Mademoiselle Linon?”

“Not yet.”

“Go to her; she is so fond of you.”

“What is this? I have upset her. Lord, help me!” thought Levin, and he skated off to the old Frenchwoman with grey ringlets who was sitting on a bench. Smiling and showing her false teeth, she met him like an old friend.

“Yes, here we are growing up,” she said to him, indicating Kitty with her eyes, “and growing old. The tiny bear has grown big already!” the Frenchwoman went on, laughing, and she reminded him of his joke about the three young ladies, whom he used to call the three bears from the English fairy tale. “Do you remember, you used to say that?”

He decidedly did not remember it, but she had been laughing at that joke for ten years or so and loved it.

“Well, go along, go and skate. Our Kitty has learned to skate well, hasn’t she?”

When Levin skated up to Kitty again, her face was no longer stern, her eyes looked at him just as truthfully and kindly; but it seemed to Levin that there was in her kindness a special, deliberately calm tone. And he felt sad. After talking about her old governess and her oddities, she asked him about his life.

“Don’t you really find it dull in the country in winter?” she said.

“No, not dull; I am very busy,” he said, feeling that she was bringing him under her calm tone, out of which he would not have the strength to break, just as it had been at the beginning of the winter.

“Have you come for long?” Kitty asked him.

“I don’t know,” he answered, not thinking of what he was saying. The thought came to him that if he gave in to this tone of calm friendship of hers he would go away again having settled nothing, and he decided to rebel.

“How can you not know?”

“I don’t know. It depends on you,” he said, and was at once horrified at his own words.

Whether she did not hear his words or did not want to hear them, she seemed to stumble, tapped her foot twice, and hurriedly skated away from him. She skated up to Mlle Linon, said something to her, and made for the hut where the ladies took off their skates.

“My God, what have I done! Lord my God! Help me, teach me,” said Levin, praying, and at the same time feeling a need for violent movement, he took a run and cut outer and inner circles.

At that moment one of the young men, the best of the new skaters, with a cigarette in his mouth and his skates on, came out of the coffee-house and, taking a run, went down the steps on his skates, clattering and bouncing. He flew down and, without even changing the easy position of his arms, glided away over the ice.

“Ah, that’s a new trick!” said Levin, and at once ran up to the top to do this new trick.

“Don’t kill yourself, it takes practice!” Nikolai Shcherbatsky shouted to him.

Levin went up onto the landing, took as long a run as he could from the top, and went off down, keeping his balance with his arms in the unfamiliar movement. On the last step he caught his foot, but, barely touching the ice with his hand, he made a strong movement, recovered himself and, laughing, glided on.

“Splendid, dear man,” thought Kitty at that moment, coming out of the hut with Mlle Linon and looking at him with a smile of quiet tenderness, as at a beloved brother. “And am I really to blame? Have I really done anything wrong? They say: flirting. I know it is not he whom I love; but still I am happy with him, and he is so splendid. Only why did he say that?…” she thought.

Seeing Kitty leaving, and her mother meeting her on the steps, Levin, flushed after the quick movement, stopped and grew thoughtful. He took off his skates and caught up with mother and daughter at the gate of the gardens.

“Very glad to see you,” said the princess. “We are at home on Thursdays, as always.”

“Today, then?”

“We shall be very glad to see you,” said the princess drily.

This dryness upset Kitty, and she could not resist the wish to smooth over her mother’s coldness. She turned her head and said with a smile:

“Goodbye till then.”

At that moment Stepan Arkadyich, his hat on one side, his face and eyes shining, was coming into the gardens like a cheerful conqueror. But, on reaching his mother-in-law, he answered her questions about Dolly’s health with a sad and guilty face. After talking quietly and dejectedly with his mother-in-law, he straightened his chest and took Levin’s arm.

“Well, shall we go?” he asked. “I kept thinking about you, and I am very, very glad you have come,” he said, looking him in the eyes with a significant air.

“Let’s go, let’s go,” answered the happy Levin, who still could not stop hearing the sound of the voice that had said “Goodbye till then” and seeing the smile with which it had been said.

“To the Angleterre or the Hermitage?”

“I don’t mind.”

“Well, the Angleterre, then,” said Stepan Arkadyich, choosing the Angleterre because he owed more there than at the Hermitage. For that reason he thought it wrong to avoid that hotel. “Have you got a cab? Good, because I sent my carriage away.”

The friends were silent all the way. Levin was thinking about what that change of expression on Kitty’s face had meant, and now he assured himself that there was hope, now he fell into despair and saw clearly that his hope was mad; and all the while he felt himself quite another man, not like the one he had been before her smile and her “goodbye till then”.

On the way Stepan Arkadyich was composing the menu.

“You like turbot, don’t you?” he said to Levin as they drove up.

“What?” Levin asked again. “Turbot? Yes, I love turbot terribly.”
