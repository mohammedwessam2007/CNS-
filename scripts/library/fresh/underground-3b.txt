## IV

I had known the evening before that I would arrive first. But it was no longer a question of being first.

Not only was none of them there, but I could hardly even find our room. The table was not yet fully laid. What did it mean? After many questions I finally got the waiters to tell me that the dinner had been ordered for six o’clock, not five. The bar confirmed it. I was even ashamed to keep asking. It was only twenty-five past five. If they had changed the time, they ought at least to have let me know; that is what the city post is for; instead of exposing me to “disgrace” in my own eyes and... and even in front of the waiters. I sat down; a waiter began laying the table; with him there it felt somehow even more humiliating. Towards six o’clock candles were brought into the room, in addition to the lamps that were burning. The waiter had not thought, however, of bringing them as soon as I arrived. In the next room two gloomy diners, angry-looking and silent, were dining at separate tables. In one of the far rooms there was a great deal of noise, even shouting; one could hear the laughter of a whole gang of people, and some nasty French shrieks: it was a dinner with ladies. In a word, it was thoroughly sickening. I have rarely spent a nastier time, so that when they all appeared together at exactly six o’clock, I was glad to see them at first, as if they were some kind of liberators, and almost forgot that I was obliged to look offended.

Zverkov came in ahead of the others, evidently leading them. He and all of them were laughing; but on seeing me Zverkov drew himself up, came over unhurriedly, bending a little at the waist as if flirting, and gave me his hand, kindly but not too kindly, with a kind of cautious, almost general-like courtesy, as if, in giving me his hand, he were protecting himself from something. I had imagined, on the contrary, that as soon as he came in he would laugh his old thin, squealing laugh, and that his flat jokes and witticisms would start with the very first words. I had been preparing for them since the evening before, but I had never expected such condescension, such high-ranking affability. So he now considered himself immeasurably superior to me in every respect? If he only meant to insult me with this general’s manner, that would be nothing, I thought; I would spit it back somehow. But what if, without any wish to insult me, the little idea had seriously crept into his sheep’s head that he was immeasurably superior to me and could only look at me with patronage? The mere supposition made me choke.

“I was surprised to hear of your wish to join us,” he began, lisping and drawling his words, which he had never done before. “We never seem to meet, you and I. You avoid us. You shouldn’t. We are not as terrible as you think. Well, at any rate, I am glad to re-new our ac-quain-tance...”

And he turned carelessly to put his hat on the window sill.

“Have you been waiting long?” asked Trudolyubov.

“I arrived at exactly five o’clock, as I was told yesterday,” I answered loudly, with an irritation that promised an explosion soon.

“Didn’t you let him know we changed the time?” Trudolyubov turned to Simonov.

“No, I didn’t. I forgot,” he answered, without the least remorse, and without even apologising to me he went off to see about the hors d’oeuvres.

“So you’ve been here an hour already, oh, poor fellow!” cried Zverkov mockingly, because by his notions this really must have been terribly funny. That scoundrel Ferfichkin burst out laughing after him in his nasty little voice, ringing like a puppy’s. He too thought my position very funny and embarrassing.

“It isn’t funny at all!” I shouted at Ferfichkin, more and more irritated. “It was other people’s fault, not mine. They neglected to tell me. It’s, it’s, it’s simply absurd.”

“Not only absurd, but something else besides,” grumbled Trudolyubov, naively taking my side. “You are too mild. It was simply rude. Not on purpose, of course. And how could Simonov... hm!”

“If anyone had played a trick like that on me,” remarked Ferfichkin, “I would...”

“But you should have ordered something for yourself,” Zverkov interrupted, “or simply asked for dinner without waiting.”

“You must agree that I could have done that without anyone’s permission,” I snapped. “If I waited, then...”

“Let’s sit down, gentlemen,” cried Simonov, coming in. “Everything’s ready; I answer for the champagne, it’s perfectly chilled... I didn’t know your address; where was I to look for you?” he suddenly turned to me, but again somehow without looking at me. He evidently had something against me. He must have thought it over after yesterday.

Everyone sat down; I sat down too. The table was round. Trudolyubov was on my left, Simonov on my right. Zverkov sat opposite; Ferfichkin next to him, between him and Trudolyubov.

“Te-e-ll me, are you... in a government department?” Zverkov went on attending to me. Seeing that I was embarrassed, he seriously imagined that I needed to be treated kindly and, so to speak, encouraged. “What does he want, for me to throw a bottle at him?” I thought in a fury. Not being used to it, I was growing irritated unnaturally fast.

“In the ... office,” I answered abruptly, looking at my plate.

“And... is it pro-profitable for you? Te-ell me, what in-du-uced you to leave your former service?”

“What in-du-uced me was that I wanted to leave my former service,” I drawled three times as long, almost losing control of myself. Ferfichkin snorted. Simonov looked at me ironically; Trudolyubov stopped eating and began to examine me with curiosity.

Zverkov winced, but did not want to notice.

“We-e-ll, and what about your remuneration?”

“What remuneration?”

“I mean, your s-salary?”

“Why are you cross-examining me?”

However, I immediately told him what salary I received. I blushed terribly.

“Not much,” Zverkov remarked gravely.

“Yes, sir, you can’t dine in cafés and restaurants on that!” Ferfichkin added insolently.

“In my opinion it’s simply poor,” Trudolyubov remarked seriously.

“And how thin you’ve grown, how you’ve changed... since those days...” Zverkov added, now not without venom, with a kind of insolent pity, examining me and my clothes.

“Oh, stop embarrassing him,” cried Ferfichkin, giggling.

“My dear sir, let me tell you that I am not embarrassed,” I burst out at last. “Do you hear, sir! I am dining here, ‘in a café-restaurant’, on my own money, my own, and not other people’s, note that, Monsieur Ferfichkin.”

“Wha-at! Who here isn’t dining on his own money? You seem to be...” Ferfichkin fastened on to it, turning red as a lobster and looking me furiously in the eye.

“Just so,” I answered, feeling that I had gone too far, “and I suggest that we would do better to talk about something more intelligent.”

“You intend to show off your intelligence, I suppose?”

“Don’t worry, that would be quite superfluous here.”

“What are you clucking about, my dear sir, eh? Have you gone off your head in your de-part-ment?”

“Enough, gentlemen, enough!” Zverkov cried commandingly.

“How stupid this is!” grumbled Simonov.

“It really is stupid. We have gathered as friends to see off a good comrade on his journey, and you are settling scores,” said Trudolyubov, addressing me alone, rudely. “You invited yourself yesterday; don’t spoil the general harmony...”

“Enough, enough,” Zverkov shouted. “Stop it, gentlemen; this won’t do. Let me tell you instead how I nearly got married the day before yesterday...”

And there began some lampoon about how this gentleman had nearly got married the day before yesterday. There was not a word about marriage in it, though; but generals, colonels and even gentlemen of the bedchamber kept flashing through the story, with Zverkov almost at their head. Approving laughter began; Ferfichkin even squealed.

They all abandoned me, and I sat crushed and annihilated.

“Lord, is this the company for me?” I thought. “And what a fool I have made of myself in front of them! I let Ferfichkin go too far, though. The blockheads think they have done me an honour by giving me a place at their table, when they don’t understand that it is I, I who am doing them an honour, and not they me! ‘Thin! Clothes!’ Oh, those damned trousers! Zverkov noticed the yellow stain on the knee just now... But what does it matter! I must get up from the table at once, this very minute, take my hat and simply leave without a word... Out of contempt! And tomorrow, a duel if necessary. Scoundrels. It isn’t as if I grudged the seven roubles. They may think... Damn it! I don’t grudge the seven roubles! I’m leaving this minute!..”

Of course, I stayed.

From misery I drank Lafite and sherry by the glassful. Not being used to it, I quickly got drunk, and as I got drunk my vexation grew. I suddenly wanted to insult them all in the most insolent way and then leave. To seize the moment and show what I was: let them say, “He may be ridiculous, but he’s clever...” and... and... in a word, to hell with them!

I looked round at them all insolently with bleary eyes. But they seemed to have forgotten me entirely. They were noisy, loud and cheerful. Zverkov was doing all the talking. I began to listen. Zverkov was telling about some magnificent lady whom he had at last brought to the point of a declaration (he was lying like a horse, of course), and how in this affair he had been particularly helped by an intimate friend of his, some little prince, Kolya, a hussar, who owned three thousand serfs.

“And yet this Kolya with his three thousand serfs isn’t here to see you off,” I suddenly broke into the conversation. For a moment everyone fell silent.

“You’re drunk already,” Trudolyubov finally condescended to notice me, glancing contemptuously in my direction. Zverkov examined me in silence, like an insect. I lowered my eyes. Simonov hurriedly began pouring the champagne.

Trudolyubov raised his glass, and everyone after him, except me.

“Your health, and a good journey!” he cried to Zverkov. “To old times, gentlemen, to our future, hurrah!”

They all drank, and climbed over to kiss Zverkov. I did not move; my full glass stood untouched in front of me.

“Aren’t you going to drink?” roared Trudolyubov, losing patience and turning to me menacingly.

“I want to make a speech of my own, separately... and then I’ll drink, Mr Trudolyubov.”

“Nasty little spitfire!” grumbled Simonov.

I sat up straight in my chair and took my glass feverishly, preparing for something extraordinary, and not yet knowing myself exactly what I would say.

“*Silence!*” cried Ferfichkin. “Now we’ll hear some wit!” Zverkov waited very seriously, understanding what was coming.

“Lieutenant Zverkov,” I began, “let me tell you that I hate phrases, phrase-mongers and tight-laced waists... That is the first point, and the second will follow.”

Everyone stirred violently.

“The second point: I hate womanising and womanisers. Especially womanisers!

“The third point: I love truth, sincerity and honesty,” I went on almost mechanically, because I was already beginning to freeze with horror, not understanding how I could be talking like this... “I love thought, Monsieur Zverkov; I love true comradeship, on an equal footing, and not... hm... I love... But then, why not? I too will drink your health, Monsieur Zverkov. Seduce the Circassian girls, shoot the enemies of the fatherland, and... and... Your health, Monsieur Zverkov!”

Zverkov rose from his chair, bowed to me, and said:

“I am very much obliged to you.”

He was terribly offended, and had even turned pale.

“Damn it,” roared Trudolyubov, banging the table with his fist.

“No, sir, for that you get punched in the face!” squealed Ferfichkin.

“He should be thrown out!” grumbled Simonov.

“Not a word, gentlemen, not a gesture!” Zverkov cried solemnly, checking the general indignation. “I thank you all, but I can show him myself how much I value his words.”

“Mr Ferfichkin, tomorrow you will give me satisfaction for what you have just said!” I said loudly, turning to Ferfichkin with dignity.

“A duel, you mean? By all means,” he answered; but I must have been so ridiculous in issuing the challenge, and it suited my figure so badly, that they all, and Ferfichkin after them, simply collapsed with laughter.

“Yes, of course, leave him alone! He’s completely drunk!” said Trudolyubov with disgust.

“I’ll never forgive myself for putting him down!” grumbled Simonov again.

“Now is the moment to throw a bottle at them all,” I thought; I took a bottle and... poured myself a full glass.

“No, better to sit it out to the end!” I went on thinking. “You would be glad, gentlemen, if I left. Not for anything. I shall sit here and drink to the end on purpose, as a sign that I don’t attach the slightest importance to you. I shall sit and drink, because this is a tavern and I have paid my entrance money. I shall sit and drink, because I regard you as pawns, pawns that don’t exist. I shall sit and drink... and sing if I like, yes, sir, and sing, because I have the right... to sing... hm.”

But I did not sing. I only tried not to look at any of them; I assumed the most independent poses and waited impatiently for them to speak to me first. But, alas, they did not speak. And how, how I wished at that moment to make it up with them! It struck eight, at last nine. They moved from the table to the sofa. Zverkov stretched out on a couch, with one foot on a little round table. The wine was moved there too. He really did stand them three bottles of his own. He did not invite me, of course. They all sat round him on the sofa. They listened to him almost with reverence. It was clear that they loved him. “What for? What for?” I thought to myself. Now and then they fell into drunken raptures and kissed each other. They talked about the Caucasus, about what true passion is, about card games, about profitable posts in the service; about how much income the hussar Podkharzhevsky had, whom none of them knew personally, and they were glad he had a big income; about the extraordinary beauty and grace of Princess D., whom none of them had ever seen either; until at last they got to Shakespeare being immortal.

I smiled contemptuously and walked up and down on the other side of the room, right opposite the sofa, along the wall, from the table to the stove and back. I wanted with all my strength to show that I could do without them; and yet I deliberately stamped my boots, coming down on my heels. But it was all in vain. They paid no attention. I had the patience to walk like this, right in front of them, from eight o’clock until eleven, always over the same stretch, from the table to the stove and from the stove back to the table. “I’m walking, and no one can stop me.” The waiter coming into the room stopped several times to look at me; my head spun from turning so often; at moments it seemed to me I was delirious. In those three hours I broke into a sweat three times and dried off again. At times, with the deepest and most venomous pain, the thought pierced my heart: that ten years would pass, twenty years, forty years, and still, even after forty years, I would remember with disgust and humiliation these filthiest, most ridiculous and most terrible minutes of my whole life. It was impossible to humiliate oneself more shamelessly and more voluntarily, and I understood that fully, fully, and still I went on walking from the table to the stove and back. “Oh, if you only knew what feelings and thoughts I am capable of, and how cultured I am!” I thought at moments, mentally addressing the sofa where my enemies sat. But my enemies behaved as if I were not in the room at all. Once, only once, they turned to me, precisely when Zverkov began talking about Shakespeare and I suddenly laughed contemptuously. I snorted so affectedly and nastily that they all broke off their conversation at once and silently watched for about two minutes, seriously, without laughing, as I walked along the wall from the table to the stove, paying no attention to them whatever. But nothing came of it: they did not speak to me, and after two minutes they abandoned me again. It struck eleven.

“Gentlemen,” cried Zverkov, rising from the sofa, “now let’s all go *there*.”

“Of course, of course!” said the others.

I turned sharply to Zverkov. I was so worn out, so broken, that I would have cut my throat just to put an end to it! I was feverish; my hair, soaked with sweat, had dried and stuck to my forehead and temples.

“Zverkov! I beg your pardon,” I said sharply and decisively. “Ferfichkin, yours too, everyone’s, everyone’s; I have insulted everyone!”

“Aha! So a duel isn’t to his taste!” Ferfichkin hissed venomously.

That cut me painfully to the heart.

“No, I am not afraid of a duel, Ferfichkin! I am ready to fight you tomorrow, even after we have made peace. I even insist on it, and you cannot refuse me. I want to prove to you that I am not afraid of a duel. You will fire first, and I will fire into the air.”

“He’s amusing himself,” remarked Simonov.

“He’s simply gone soft in the head!” responded Trudolyubov.

“Let me pass; why are you standing in the way!.. Well, what do you want?” Zverkov answered contemptuously. They were all flushed; all their eyes were shining: they had drunk a lot.

“I ask for your friendship, Zverkov; I insulted you, but...”

“Insulted me? Y-you! M-me! Let me tell you, my dear sir, that you could never, under any circumstances, insult me!”

“And that’s enough of you; out of the way!” Trudolyubov added. “Let’s go.”

“Olympia is mine, gentlemen; that’s agreed!” cried Zverkov.

“We won’t dispute it! We won’t dispute it!” they answered, laughing.

I stood there, spat upon. The gang left the room noisily; Trudolyubov struck up some stupid song. Simonov stayed behind for a tiny moment to tip the waiters. I suddenly went up to him.

“Simonov! Give me six roubles!” I said decisively and desperately.

He looked at me with extreme astonishment, with somehow dull eyes. He was drunk too.

“Are you really coming with us *there* too?”

“Yes!”

“I have no money!” he snapped, grinned contemptuously, and went out of the room.

I seized him by his overcoat. It was a nightmare.

“Simonov! I saw you had money; why do you refuse me? Am I a scoundrel? Be careful how you refuse me: if you knew, if you knew why I am asking! Everything depends on it, my whole future, all my plans...”

Simonov took out the money and almost threw it at me.

“Take it, if you are so shameless!” he said mercilessly, and ran off to catch up with them.

I was left alone for a minute. Disorder, scraps of food, a broken wineglass on the floor, spilt wine, cigarette ends, drink and delirium in my head, an agonising anguish in my heart, and, finally, the waiter, who had seen everything and heard everything and was looking curiously into my eyes.

“*There!*” I cried. “Either they will all be on their knees, embracing my feet and begging for my friendship, or... or I will slap Zverkov’s face!”
