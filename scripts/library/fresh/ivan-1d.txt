## IV

They were all in good health. It could not be called ill health that Ivan Ilyich sometimes said he had a strange taste in his mouth and some discomfort in the left side of his abdomen.

But it happened that this discomfort began to increase and to turn, not yet into pain, but into a constant feeling of heaviness in his side and into a bad temper. This bad temper, growing stronger and stronger, began to spoil the pleasantness of easy and decent life that had been established in the Golovin family. Husband and wife began to quarrel more and more often, and soon the ease and pleasantness fell away, and decency alone was kept up with difficulty. Scenes became more frequent again. Again there remained only little islands, and few of them, on which husband and wife could meet without an explosion.

And Praskovya Fyodorovna now said, not without reason, that her husband had a difficult character. With her usual habit of exaggeration, she said he had always had this terrible character, and that it had taken her goodness to bear it for twenty years. It was true that the quarrels now started with him. His fault-finding always began just before dinner, and often just as he began to eat, over the soup. Now he would notice that some piece of crockery was damaged, now that the food was not right, now that his son had put his elbow on the table, now his daughter’s hair. And for everything he blamed Praskovya Fyodorovna. At first Praskovya Fyodorovna answered back and said unpleasant things to him, but twice at the start of dinner he flew into such a rage that she understood it was a morbid state brought on in him by taking food, and she controlled herself; she no longer answered back, but only hurried dinner along. Praskovya Fyodorovna counted her self-restraint a great merit. Having decided that her husband had a terrible character and had made her life a misery, she began to pity herself. And the more she pitied herself, the more she hated her husband. She began to wish he would die, but she could not wish it, because then there would be no salary. And this irritated her against him still more. She considered herself terribly unhappy precisely because even his death could not save her, and she was irritated, and hid it, and this hidden irritation of hers increased his irritation.

After one scene, in which Ivan Ilyich had been particularly unjust, and after which, when they talked it over, he said that he was indeed irritable but that it was because of his illness, she told him that if he was ill he must get treatment, and she demanded that he go to see a famous doctor.

He went. Everything was as he had expected; everything was done as it always is. The waiting, and the put-on, doctorly importance he knew so well, the very same importance he knew in himself in court; and the tapping, and the listening, and the questions requiring answers that were settled in advance and evidently unnecessary; and the significant air that suggested: just put yourself in our hands, and we shall arrange everything; we know, beyond doubt, how to arrange everything, in one and the same way for any person you like. It was all exactly the same as in court. Just as he in court put on an air before the accused, so the famous doctor now put on the same air before him.

The doctor said: this and that indicate that inside you there is this and that; but if this is not confirmed by examining this and that, then we must suppose that you have this and that. And if we suppose this, then... and so on. Only one question mattered to Ivan Ilyich: was his condition dangerous or not? But the doctor ignored this inappropriate question. From the doctor’s point of view, the question was idle and not open to discussion; all that existed was the weighing of probabilities: a floating kidney, chronic catarrh, or a disease of the appendix. There was no question of Ivan Ilyich’s life; there was a dispute between the floating kidney and the appendix. And before Ivan Ilyich’s eyes the doctor brilliantly settled this dispute in favour of the appendix, with the reservation that an analysis of the urine might provide new evidence, and that then the case would be reconsidered. All this was exactly what Ivan Ilyich himself had done a thousand times, in just as brilliant a manner, with the accused. The doctor summed up just as brilliantly, and looked triumphantly, even cheerfully, over his spectacles at the accused. From the doctor’s summing-up Ivan Ilyich drew the conclusion that things were bad, and that to the doctor, and perhaps to everyone, it did not matter, but that for him it was bad. And this conclusion struck Ivan Ilyich painfully, arousing in him a great pity for himself and great anger at this doctor, so indifferent to so important a question.

But he said nothing; he got up, put the money on the table and, with a sigh, said:

“We sick people probably often ask you inappropriate questions,” he said. “But in general, is this a dangerous illness or not?”

The doctor looked at him sternly with one eye through his spectacles, as if to say: “Prisoner, if you do not keep within the limits of the questions put to you, I shall be obliged to order your removal from the courtroom.”

“I have already told you what I considered necessary and appropriate,” said the doctor. “The tests will show the rest.” And the doctor bowed.

Ivan Ilyich went out slowly, got gloomily into his sledge and drove home. All the way he kept going over everything the doctor had said, trying to translate all those muddled, obscure scientific words into simple language and to read in them the answer to the question: is it bad, very bad for me, or is it still nothing much? And it seemed to him that the meaning of everything the doctor had said was that it was very bad. Everything in the streets seemed sad to Ivan Ilyich. The cabmen were sad, the houses were sad, the passers-by, the shops were sad. And this pain, this dull, gnawing pain that did not stop for a second, seemed, in connection with the doctor’s obscure words, to take on another, more serious meaning. Ivan Ilyich listened to it now with a new, heavy feeling.

He got home and began to tell his wife. His wife listened, but in the middle of his account their daughter came in with her hat on: she was about to go out with her mother. She made an effort and sat down to listen to this tedious talk, but she could not stand it for long, and her mother did not hear him out either.

“Well, I’m very glad,” said his wife. “So now, mind you take your medicine properly. Give me the prescription; I’ll send Gerasim to the chemist’s.” And she went to get dressed.

He held his breath while she was in the room, and sighed heavily when she went out.

“Well,” he said. “Perhaps it really is nothing yet.”

He began to take the medicines and follow the doctor’s instructions, which changed after the analysis of the urine. But just then it so happened that some confusion arose in this analysis and in what was to follow it. It was impossible to get through to the doctor himself, and it turned out that what was being done was not what the doctor had told him. Either the doctor had forgotten, or lied, or was hiding something from him.

But Ivan Ilyich all the same began to follow the instructions exactly, and in following them he found consolation for a while.

From the time of his visit to the doctor, Ivan Ilyich’s chief occupation became the exact carrying out of the doctor’s instructions about hygiene and taking medicine, and listening to his pain and to all the functions of his body. Ivan Ilyich’s chief interests became human illnesses and human health. When people spoke in his presence about the sick, about those who had died or recovered, especially from an illness that resembled his, he listened, trying to hide his agitation, asked questions, and applied what he heard to his own illness.

The pain did not lessen; but Ivan Ilyich made efforts to force himself to think he was better. And he could deceive himself as long as nothing upset him. But as soon as there was some unpleasantness with his wife, a setback at work, or bad cards at vint, he at once felt the full force of his illness. Before, he would have borne these setbacks, expecting that at any moment he would put things right, overcome them, wait for success, a grand slam. But now every setback cut him down and threw him into despair. He would say to himself: “Here I was just beginning to recover, and the medicine was already beginning to work, and now this damned misfortune or unpleasantness...” And he was angry at the misfortune, or at the people who were causing him unpleasantness and killing him, and he felt this anger killing him; but he could not restrain it. One would think it should have been clear to him that this anger at circumstances and people was making his illness worse, and that therefore he ought to pay no attention to unpleasant incidents; but he reasoned in exactly the opposite way: he said he needed peace, watched for everything that disturbed this peace, and at the slightest disturbance became irritated. His condition was made worse by his reading medical books and consulting doctors. The deterioration was so even that he could deceive himself by comparing one day with another: there was little difference. But when he consulted doctors, it seemed to him that he was getting worse, and even very quickly. And in spite of this he was constantly consulting doctors.

That month he went to another celebrity: the other celebrity said almost the same as the first, but put the questions differently. And the consultation with this celebrity only deepened Ivan Ilyich’s doubt and fear. A friend of a friend of his, a very good doctor, diagnosed the illness quite differently again, and although he promised recovery, with his questions and suppositions he confused Ivan Ilyich still more and increased his doubt. A homeopath diagnosed the illness differently again and gave him medicine, and Ivan Ilyich took it for about a week, secretly from everyone. But after a week, feeling no relief and losing confidence both in the earlier treatments and in this one, he sank into still greater dejection. Once a lady he knew told him about cures by icons. Ivan Ilyich caught himself listening attentively and checking whether the fact was true. This incident frightened him. “Have I really grown so weak in mind?” he said to himself. “Nonsense! It’s all rubbish; I mustn’t give in to nervousness, but choose one doctor and keep strictly to his treatment. That’s what I’ll do. Now it’s settled. I won’t think about it, and I’ll follow the treatment strictly until the summer. Then we shall see. Now an end to this wavering!” It was easy to say this, but impossible to carry out. The pain in his side kept wearing him down, seemed to keep growing stronger, became constant; the taste in his mouth became stranger and stranger; it seemed to him that his breath smelled of something disgusting; and his appetite and strength kept failing. He could not deceive himself: something terrible, new and so significant that nothing more significant had ever happened to Ivan Ilyich in his life was taking place inside him. And he alone knew about it; everyone around him did not understand, or did not want to understand, and thought that everything in the world was going on as before. It was this that tormented Ivan Ilyich more than anything. His household, above all his wife and daughter, who were in the full swing of going out into society, understood nothing, he saw; they were annoyed that he was so gloomy and demanding, as if he were to blame for it. Although they tried to hide it, he saw that he was a hindrance to them, and that his wife had worked out a certain attitude to his illness and kept to it regardless of what he said or did. The attitude was this:

“You know,” she would say to acquaintances, “Ivan Ilyich can’t follow his prescribed treatment strictly, the way sensible people do. One day he’ll take his drops and eat what he’s told and go to bed on time; the next day, if I don’t watch him, he’ll suddenly forget to take them, eat sturgeon (which he’s been forbidden) and sit up over cards until one in the morning.”

“Well, when was that?” Ivan Ilyich would say with annoyance. “Once, at Pyotr Ivanovich’s.”

“And yesterday with Shebek.”

“I couldn’t have slept anyway, with the pain...”

“Whatever the reason, you’ll never get well like this, and you torment us.”

Praskovya Fyodorovna’s outward attitude to her husband’s illness, which she expressed to others and to him, was that Ivan Ilyich was to blame for this illness and that the whole illness was a new unpleasantness he was causing his wife. Ivan Ilyich felt that this came out of her involuntarily, but that did not make it any easier for him.

At the court Ivan Ilyich noticed, or thought he noticed, the same strange attitude towards himself: now it seemed to him that people were watching him as a man who would soon be vacating his post; now his friends would suddenly begin to tease him in a friendly way about his nervousness, as if this awful, terrible, unheard-of thing that had taken root in him, that was ceaselessly sucking at him and irresistibly drawing him somewhere, were the most pleasant subject for a joke. Schwartz in particular irritated him, with his playfulness, his liveliness and his *comme il faut* manner, which reminded Ivan Ilyich of himself ten years before.

Friends would come to make up a game, and sit down. They dealt; the new cards were softened up; he sorted the diamonds together: seven of them. His partner said, “No trumps,” and supported him with two diamonds. What more could one want? It ought to be cheerful and lively: a grand slam. And suddenly Ivan Ilyich feels that sucking pain, that taste in his mouth, and it seems to him something wild that he can rejoice at a grand slam at the same time.

He looks at Mikhail Mikhailovich, his partner, at how he raps the table with his sanguine hand and politely and indulgently refrains from gathering up the tricks, but pushes them towards Ivan Ilyich so as to give him the pleasure of collecting them without having to trouble himself or stretch his arm far. “Does he think I’m so weak I can’t stretch my arm out?” thinks Ivan Ilyich; he forgets what are trumps, trumps his own partner’s card once too often, and loses the grand slam by three tricks; and what is most terrible of all is that he sees how Mikhail Mikhailovich suffers, and he does not care. And it is terrible to think why he does not care.

Everyone sees that he is having a hard time, and they say to him: “We can stop if you’re tired. Have a rest.” A rest? No, he isn’t tired at all; they finish the rubber. Everyone is gloomy and silent. Ivan Ilyich feels that he has brought this gloom upon them and cannot dispel it. They have supper and go home, and Ivan Ilyich is left alone with the awareness that his life is poisoned for him and poisons the lives of others, and that this poison is not weakening but penetrating his whole being more and more.

And with this awareness, and with physical pain as well, and with horror as well, he had to go to bed, and often lie awake from the pain for most of the night. And in the morning he had to get up again, dress, drive to the court, talk, write; or, if he did not go, be at home with the same twenty-four hours in the day, every one of which was torment. And he had to live like this on the edge of the abyss alone, without a single person who would understand him and pity him.
