## Book Five

1. At dawn, when you are reluctant to get up, have this thought ready: “I am getting up to do the work of a human being. Why then am I still grumbling, if I am going out to do what I was born for, what I was brought into the world to do? Or was I made for this, to lie under the blankets and keep warm?” “But this is more pleasant.” Were you born, then, for pleasure? In a word, for being acted upon, or for acting? Do you not see the little plants, the sparrows, the ants, the spiders, the bees, all doing their own work, each helping to put the world in order in its own way? And you are unwilling to do the work of a human being? You do not run to do what is in keeping with your own nature?

“But one must rest too.” I agree. But nature has set limits to this too, as it has to eating and drinking; and yet you go beyond the limits, beyond what is enough; but in your actions, not so: there you stay well within what you could do. The truth is, you do not love yourself; for if you did, you would love your nature and what it wants.

People who love their crafts wear themselves out over their work, unwashed and unfed. Do you value your own nature less than the metal-engraver values engraving, or the dancer dancing, or the miser money, or the vain man his bit of fame? These people, when they are keen on something, would rather not eat or sleep than fall behind in what they care about. And do actions for the common good seem to you cheaper, and less worth your effort?

2. How easy it is to push away and wipe out every troublesome or unsuitable impression, and at once to be in perfect calm.

3. Judge yourself worthy of every word and act that is in keeping with nature, and do not let the criticism or talk of some people that follows distract you. If it was right to do or say it, do not think yourself unworthy of it. Those people have their own ruling mind and follow their own impulse. Do not look round at them; go straight ahead, following your own nature and the common nature; and the road of both is one.

4. I go on through the things that are according to nature, until I fall and rest, breathing my last into the air from which I draw breath every day, and falling onto the earth from which my father gathered his seed, my mother her blood, and my nurse her milk; from which for so many years I have been fed and watered every day; which carries me as I walk on it, and lets me use it for so many things.

5. They cannot admire you for quickness of wit. Very well. But there are many other things of which you cannot say, “I have no gift for it.” Show those, then, which are wholly up to you: sincerity, dignity, hard work, indifference to pleasure, not complaining about your lot, needing little, kindness, freedom, simplicity, not chattering, greatness of mind. Do you not see how many things you could show already, where there is no excuse of lack of talent or unfitness, and yet you still choose to stay down below? Or are you forced by lack of talent to grumble, to be stingy, to flatter, to blame your poor body, to try to please, to show off, to toss about so much in your soul? No, by the gods; you could have been rid of all this long ago, and been accused, if at all, only of being rather slow and dull of understanding. And even this must be trained, not ignored, and not enjoyed as an excuse.

6. One kind of person, when he has done someone a good turn, is quick to put it down to his account as a favour owed. Another is not so quick to do this, but still thinks of the other as in his debt, and knows what he has done. A third kind, in a way, does not even know what he has done, but is like a vine that has borne grapes and asks for nothing more once it has produced its own fruit.

A horse that has run, a dog that has tracked, a bee that has made honey, and a man who has done good: he does not shout about it, but moves on to the next, as a vine moves on to bear its grapes again in season. You should be one of those who do this, in a way, without noticing. “Yes, but one should notice this very thing; for it is the mark of a social being,” he says, “to be aware that he is acting socially, and, by Zeus, to want his fellow to be aware of it too.” What you say is true; but you are misunderstanding what I am saying now, and so you will become one of those I mentioned first; for they too are led astray by a kind of plausible reasoning. But if you are willing to understand what I am saying, do not be afraid that it will make you neglect any act for the common good.

7. A prayer of the Athenians: “Rain, rain, dear Zeus, on the ploughed fields of the Athenians and on their plains.” Either we should not pray at all, or we should pray like this, simply and freely.

8. Just as we say, “Asclepius has prescribed this man riding, or cold baths, or walking barefoot,” so we can say, “The nature of the whole has prescribed this man illness, or disability, or loss, or something else of the kind.” In the first case, “prescribed” means something like this: he ordered this for this man as suited to his health. In the second, what happens to each person has been ordered for him, in a way, as suited to his fate.

For we say that things “fit” us, as builders say that the squared stones in walls or pyramids fit, when they fit together into a certain structure. For in all there is one harmony; and just as the world, this great body, is made up of all bodies, so fate, this great cause, is made up of all causes.

Even quite simple people understand what I mean; for they say, “It brought him this.” So this was brought to this man, and this was ordered for this man. Let us accept these things, then, as we accept what Asclepius prescribes. Many of those prescriptions are harsh too; but we welcome them in the hope of health.

Think of the carrying out and completion of what the common nature has decided as being like your own health. And so welcome everything that happens, even if it seems rather hard, because it leads there: to the health of the world, and the well-being and success of Zeus. For he would not have brought this to anyone, if it were not for the good of the whole; for not even an ordinary nature brings anything that does not suit what it governs.

So there are two reasons why you should be content with what happens to you. One, that it happened to you, was ordered for you, and was related to you in some way, spun for you from the beginning out of the most ancient causes. The other, that even what comes to each person individually is a cause of the well-being, the completion, and, by Zeus, the very survival of what governs the whole. For the whole is maimed if you cut off anything at all from its connection and continuity, of its causes as of its parts. And you do cut it off, as far as you can, when you are discontented; and in a way you destroy it.

9. Do not be disgusted, or give up, or lose heart, if you do not always manage to act on right principles; but when you are knocked off course, come back again, and be glad if most of what you do is worthy of a human being; and love what you are returning to. Do not go back to philosophy as to a schoolmaster, but as people with sore eyes go back to the sponge and the egg, or another to a poultice or a hot compress. In this way you will not be making a show of obeying reason, but will rest in it. Remember that philosophy wants only what your nature wants; but you wanted something else, not in keeping with nature. What could be more pleasant than this? Is that not how pleasure trips us up? But look and see whether greatness of mind, freedom, simplicity, good feeling and holiness are not more pleasant. And what is more pleasant than wisdom itself, when you think how sure-footed and smooth-flowing in everything is the power of understanding and knowledge?

10. Things are wrapped in such a veil that not a few philosophers, and not ordinary ones either, have thought them altogether beyond our grasp; and even the Stoics think them hard to grasp. And every judgement we make can change; for where is the man who never changes his mind? Pass on, then, to the objects themselves: how short-lived they are, how cheap, and how they can belong to a pervert or a prostitute or a robber. Then pass on to the characters of the people you live with, of whom it is hard to put up with even the most agreeable, not to say that a man can hardly put up with himself.

In such darkness and dirt, in such a flood of substance and time, of movement and things moved, what there is that deserves to be valued, or to be taken seriously at all, I cannot imagine. On the contrary, one must comfort oneself and wait for one’s natural release, and not be impatient at the delay, but rest in these thoughts alone: first, that nothing will happen to me that is not in keeping with the nature of the whole; and second, that it is in my power never to act against my god and the spirit within me. For no one can force me to go against it.

11. What am I using my soul for now? Ask yourself this at every turn, and examine yourself: what is there now in this part of me that they call the ruling mind? And whose soul do I have now? A child’s? A youth’s? A silly woman’s? A tyrant’s? A beast of burden’s? A wild animal’s?

12. You can learn what kind of things the many think are good from this. If someone has in mind things that are truly good, such as wisdom, self-control, justice, courage, he could not, with these in mind, bear to hear the comic poet’s line, “He has so many good things...”; for it would not fit. But if someone has in mind what the many think are good, he will listen, and readily accept the comic poet’s line as fitting. So even the many have a sense of the difference; otherwise the line would not offend and be rejected in the one case, while in the case of wealth and the lucky breaks that bring luxury or fame we accept it as fitting and witty. Go on, then, and ask whether we should value and think good those things which, when we have them in mind, the line fits, that the man who owns them is so well supplied “he has nowhere left to relieve himself”.

13. I am made of a cause and of matter. Neither of these will perish into nothing, just as neither came into being out of nothing. So every part of me will be assigned, by change, to some part of the world; and that again will change into another part of the world, and so on to infinity. It was by such a change that I came into being, and my parents, and so on back to another infinity. For nothing prevents us from saying this, even if the world is governed in limited cycles.

14. Reason and the art of reasoning are powers that are enough for themselves and for their own work. They start from their own first principle, and travel to the end set before them; and that is why such actions are called “right actions”, meaning the rightness of the road.

15. None of these things should be called a man’s which do not belong to a man as a man. They are not demanded of a man; man’s nature does not promise them; they are not what makes man’s nature complete. So neither is man’s end to be found in them, nor what completes the end, the good. Besides, if any of these things did belong to a man, it would not belong to him to despise them and stand up against them; nor would the man who makes himself independent of them be praised; nor would the man who goes without some of them be good, if they really were goods. But as it is, the more a man strips himself of these things, or of others like them, or puts up with being stripped of them, the better he is.

16. Your mind will be like the things you often think about; for the soul is dyed by its thoughts. Dye it, then, with a constant stream of thoughts like these: where a man can live, there he can also live well; but he can live in a palace; so he can also live well in a palace. And again: each thing is drawn towards what it was made for; and where it is drawn, there is its end; and where its end is, there is its good and its advantage; so the good of a rational being is fellowship. For it was shown long ago that we were born for fellowship. Or was it not clear that the lower things exist for the sake of the higher, and the higher for the sake of one another? And living things are higher than lifeless ones, and rational beings higher than those that merely live.

17. To chase after the impossible is madness; and it is impossible for bad people not to do things of this kind.

18. Nothing happens to anyone that he is not made by nature to bear. The same things happen to another, and either because he does not know they have happened, or because he wants to show his greatness of mind, he stays firm and unharmed. It is a strange thing, then, that ignorance and the wish to please should be stronger than wisdom.

19. Things themselves do not touch the soul at all. They have no way into the soul, and cannot turn it or move it. The soul alone turns and moves itself; and whatever judgements it thinks fit to make, it makes the things that come before it to match them.

20. In one way a human being is the thing nearest to us, in so far as we must do good to people and put up with them. But in so far as some of them stand in the way of my proper work, a human being becomes to me one of the indifferent things, no less than the sun, or the wind, or a wild beast. These may hinder some action of mine; but they are no hindrance to my intention or my disposition, because of the reservation and the power of turning things round. For the mind turns round and converts every obstacle to its action into a means to its aim; and what holds up the work becomes the work, and what stands in the way on the road becomes the road.

21. Honour the most powerful thing in the world: that is, what uses all things and directs all things. And in the same way honour the most powerful thing in yourself: that is, what is akin to it. For in you too it is this that uses everything else, and your life is directed by it.

22. What does not harm the city does not harm the citizen either. Whenever you think you have been harmed, apply this rule: if the city is not harmed by this, neither am I. But if the city is harmed, you must not be angry with the one who harms it, but show him what he has overlooked.

23. Think often of how fast the things that are, and the things that come to be, are carried past and away. For substance is like a river in constant flow; its activities are in continual change, its causes in countless variations, and hardly anything stands still, not even what is close at hand. And the boundless gulf of the past and of the future, in which all things vanish. How, then, is he not a fool who is puffed up by these things, or torn by them, or complains as if they had troubled him for some time, and for long?

24. Remember the whole of substance, of which you have a tiny share; and the whole of time, of which a short and momentary span has been marked out for you; and fate: how small a part of it are you?

25. Someone does me wrong? That is his business. He has his own disposition, his own activity. I have now what the common nature wants me to have now, and I am doing what my own nature wants me to do now.

26. Let the ruling and governing part of your soul stay unmoved by the smooth or rough movements of the flesh; let it not mix with them, but mark itself off, and keep those feelings within their own parts. And when they rise up into the mind through that other sympathy, as they must in a body that is all one, then you must not try to resist the sensation, which is natural; but let the ruling mind not add, of itself, the judgement that it is good or bad.

27. “Live with the gods.” And he lives with the gods who constantly shows them that his soul is content with what is given it, and does what is wanted by the spirit that Zeus has given to each of us as guardian and guide, a fragment of himself. And this is each person’s mind and reason.

28. Are you angry with the man whose armpits smell? Are you angry with the man whose breath smells? What good will it do you? He has such a mouth, he has such armpits; such a smell is bound to come from such things. “But the man has reason,” he says, “and could, if he paid attention, understand what is offensive.” Good for you! So you have reason too. Stir up his reasonable disposition with your reasonable disposition; show him, remind him. If he listens, you will cure him, and there is no need for anger.

Neither an actor nor a whore.

29. As you mean to live when you have left this life, so you can live here. But if they do not let you, then leave life, but as one who suffers no harm. “The house is smoky, and I leave it.” Why do you think it a great matter? But as long as nothing of the kind drives me out, I stay, free, and no one will prevent me from doing what I want; and I want what is in keeping with the nature of a rational and social being.

30. The mind of the whole is social. At any rate, it has made the lower things for the sake of the higher, and fitted the higher ones to one another. You see how it has subordinated, co-ordinated, and given to each its due share, and brought the best together into agreement with one another.

31. How have you behaved up to now towards the gods, your parents, your brother, your wife, your children, your teachers, your tutors, your friends, your relations, your servants? Has your rule towards all of them up to now been, “never to do or say anything wrong to anyone”? And remember what you have been through, and what you have had the strength to bear; and that the story of your life is now complete, and your service finished; and how many beautiful things you have seen, how many pleasures and pains you have looked down on, how many honours you have let pass, how many unkind people you have been kind to.

32. Why do unskilled and ignorant souls confound the one who is skilled and knowing? What, then, is the soul that is skilled and knowing? The one that knows the beginning and the end, and the reason that runs through all substance and governs the whole through all time in set cycles.

33. Very soon, ashes or a skeleton, and either a name or not even a name; and a name is a noise and an echo. And the things so highly valued in life are empty, rotten, small: puppies snapping at each other, quarrelsome children laughing and then at once crying. But good faith and modesty and justice and truth have fled “to Olympus from the wide-wayed earth”. What, then, still keeps you here, if the things of the senses are changeable and never stand still, and the senses are dim and easily deceived, and the poor soul itself is a vapour rising from blood, and to be well thought of by such people is empty? What then? You wait calmly for your end, whether it is being put out or moved elsewhere. And until that time comes, what is enough? What else but to honour and praise the gods, and to do good to people, and to put up with them and keep away from them; and to remember that everything outside the limits of your bit of flesh and breath is neither yours nor up to you.

34. You can always have a life that flows smoothly, if you can go by the right road, and think and act by the right road. These two things are common to the soul of God, and of man, and of every rational being: not to be hindered by anything else; and to find the good in a just disposition and just action, and there to let desire come to an end.

35. If this is neither a vice of mine, nor an act that comes from a vice of mine, and the common good is not harmed, why am I troubled about it? And what is the harm to the common good?

36. Do not be swept away entirely by the impression, but help people as far as you can and as they deserve, even if their loss is in indifferent things. But do not imagine it to be a harm; that is a bad habit. Be like the old man who, as he went away, asked back his foster-child’s spinning top, remembering that it was only a top. So too here. Since you are standing on the platform, making speeches: “Man, have you forgotten what these things were?” “Yes; but they matter very much to these people.” “Is that a reason for you to be a fool too?”

“Once, wherever I was caught, I was a fortunate man.” But to be fortunate is to have given yourself a good fortune; and good fortunes are good turns of the soul, good impulses, good actions.
