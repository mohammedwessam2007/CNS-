## 23

If it ever happens that you turn outward, wanting to please someone, know that you have lost your footing. So be content in everything simply to be a philosopher; and if you also want to seem one, seem one to yourself, and that will be enough.

## 24

Do not let thoughts like these weigh on you: “I shall live without honour, and be nobody anywhere.” For if lack of honour is a bad thing, you cannot be in a bad state because of someone else, any more than you can be in a shameful one. Is it your business, then, to get a post, or to be invited to a dinner? Not at all. How then is this any longer a lack of honour? And how will you be nobody anywhere, when you need to be somebody only in the things that are up to you, where you can be worth the very most?

“But my friends will go without help.” What do you mean, without help? They will not get pocket money from you, and you will not make them Roman citizens. Who told you that these things are among the things up to us, and not other people’s business? And who can give another what he does not have himself? “Then get it,” someone says, “so that we may have it.”

If I can get it while keeping myself decent, trustworthy and high-minded, show me the way and I will get it. But if you ask me to lose the good things that are mine, so that you can gain things that are not good, see for yourselves how unfair and thoughtless you are. And which do you want more: money, or a trustworthy and decent friend? Help me, then, rather towards that, and do not ask me to do the things by which I shall throw those very qualities away.

“But my country,” he says, “as far as I am concerned, will go without help.” Again, what kind of help is this? It will not have colonnades or baths because of you. And what of it? It does not get shoes from the blacksmith, or weapons from the shoemaker; it is enough if each one fulfils his own work. If you provided it with another citizen, trustworthy and decent, would you be of no use to it? “Yes, I would be of use.” Then you yourself would not be useless to it either. “What place, then,” he says, “shall I have in the city?” Whatever place you can hold while keeping yourself trustworthy and decent.

But if, in wanting to help it, you throw these away, what use would you be to it, once you have become shameless and untrustworthy?

## 25

Has someone been preferred to you at a dinner, or in being greeted, or in being called in to give advice? If these things are good, you ought to be glad that he got them; and if they are bad, do not be upset that you did not get them. And remember that, if you do not do the same things as others to get the things that are not up to us, you cannot expect to be given an equal share.

For how can the man who does not hang about someone’s door have as much as the man who does? Or the man who does not escort him as much as the man who does? Or the man who does not praise him as much as the man who does? You will be unfair and greedy, then, if, without paying the price for which these things are sold, you want to get them for nothing.

How much are lettuces sold for? An obol, say. So if someone pays the obol and gets the lettuces, and you do not pay and do not get them, do not think you have less than the man who got them. For as he has the lettuces, so you have the obol you did not give.

It is the same here. You were not invited to someone’s dinner? That is because you did not pay the host the price of the dinner. He sells it for praise; he sells it for attention. So pay the price it is sold for, if it is worth it to you. But if you want not to pay the one and still to get the other, you are greedy and a fool.

Have you nothing, then, in place of the dinner? Yes, you have this: you did not have to praise a man you did not want to praise, and you did not have to put up with the men at his door.

## 26

What nature wants can be learned from the things on which we do not differ from one another. For example, when someone else’s slave boy breaks a cup, we are ready to say at once, “These things happen.” Know, then, that when your own cup is broken, you ought to be just as you were when the other man’s was broken. Carry this over to greater things too. Someone else’s child or wife has died? There is no one who would not say, “It is the lot of man.” But when one’s own dies, at once it is “Alas, how wretched I am!” We ought to remember how we feel when we hear the same thing about others.

## 27

Just as a target is not set up in order to be missed, so nothing evil by nature exists in the world.

## 28

If someone handed your body over to whoever happened to meet you, you would be angry. But you hand over your own mind to anyone who happens along, so that if he abuses you, it is troubled and confused; are you not ashamed of that?

## 29

In each undertaking, look at what comes before it and what follows from it, and only then set about it. Otherwise you will start eagerly at first, because you have not thought about what comes next, and later, when some difficulties show themselves, you will give up in disgrace. You want to win at the Olympic games?

So do I, by the gods; it is a fine thing. But look at what comes before it and what follows from it, and only then take the task in hand. You must keep to a discipline, eat by rule, keep away from cakes, train under compulsion, at a fixed hour, in heat, in cold; not drink cold water, nor wine whenever you feel like it; in short, hand yourself over to your trainer as you would to a doctor. Then, in the contest, you must dig in beside your opponent; sometimes put a hand out of joint, twist an ankle, swallow a great deal of dust, sometimes be whipped; and after all that, be beaten.

Consider all this, and if you still want to, go in for the contest. Otherwise you will behave like children, who now play wrestlers, now gladiators, now blow trumpets, then act in a tragedy. So you too will be now an athlete, now a gladiator, then an orator, then a philosopher, and with your whole soul nothing; but like an ape you imitate every show you see, and one thing after another takes your fancy. For you did not come to anything after thinking it over, or after looking at it from every side, but at random, and with a lukewarm desire.

So some people, having seen a philosopher and heard someone speak as Euphrates speaks (and yet who can speak as he does?), want to be philosophers themselves. Man, first consider what kind of thing it is; then study your own nature too, and see whether you can bear it. Do you want to be a pentathlete, or a wrestler? Look at your arms, your thighs; study your loins.

For different people are made for different things. Do you think that, if you do this, you can eat as you do now, drink as you do now, want things and be irritated in the same way? You must stay awake, work hard, leave your own people, be despised by a slave boy, be laughed at by those who meet you, come off worse in everything: in honour, in office, in court, in every little affair.

Consider all this: whether you want, in exchange for these things, to have freedom from passion, freedom, and calm. If not, do not come near it. Do not be like children, now a philosopher, later a tax-collector, then an orator, then a procurator of Caesar. These things do not go together. You must be one person, either good or bad. You must work either on your own ruling mind or on externals; give your skill either to what is within or to what is without; that is, take either the place of a philosopher or the place of an ordinary man.

## 30

Duties are, in general, measured by our relationships. He is your father: you are called on to take care of him, to give way to him in everything, to put up with him when he abuses you or strikes you. “But he is a bad father.” Were you, then, bound by nature to a good father? No, to a father. “My brother wrongs me.” Then keep your own place towards him, and do not look at what he is doing, but at what you must do for your own will to be in harmony with nature. For no one else will harm you unless you wish it; you will be harmed only when you suppose you are harmed. In this way, then, you will discover what is due from a neighbour, from a citizen, from a general, if you get into the habit of looking at relationships.

## 31

As for piety towards the gods, know that the most important thing is this: to have right beliefs about them, that they exist and govern the whole world well and justly; and to have set yourself to obey them, and to give way to everything that happens, and to follow it willingly, as being brought about by the best of minds. For in this way you will never blame the gods, nor accuse them of neglecting you.

And this cannot come about in any other way than by taking good and evil away from the things that are not up to us, and placing them only in the things that are up to us. For if you suppose any of those other things to be good or bad, then, when you fail to get what you want and fall into what you do not want, you are bound to blame and hate those who caused it.

For every living creature is made by nature to flee from and turn away from things that seem harmful, and from their causes, and to pursue and admire things that seem helpful, and their causes. It is impossible, then, for anyone who thinks he is being harmed to take pleasure in what he thinks is harming him, just as it is impossible to take pleasure in the harm itself.

That is why a father is abused by his son, when he does not give the boy a share of what seem to be good things; and it was this that made Polynices and Eteocles enemies of each other: thinking that the throne was a good thing. That is why the farmer abuses the gods, and the sailor, and the merchant, and those who lose their wives and children. For where a man’s interest lies, there lies his piety too. So whoever takes care to desire and to avoid as he ought, in that very act takes care of his piety as well.

But to pour libations, to sacrifice, and to offer first-fruits according to the custom of our fathers is always fitting, so long as it is done cleanly, not sloppily or carelessly, nor yet meanly, nor beyond one’s means.

## 32

When you go to a diviner, remember that you do not know what will happen (you have come to find that out from him); but if you are a philosopher, you have come already knowing what sort of thing it is. For if it is one of the things that are not up to us, it must of necessity be neither good nor bad.

So do not bring desire or aversion to the diviner, and do not approach him trembling; but have it settled in your mind that everything that is going to happen is indifferent and nothing to you, and that, whatever it may be, it will be possible to use it well, and no one will prevent this. Go to the gods, then, with confidence, as to advisers. And after that, when some advice has been given you, remember whom you took as advisers, and whom you will be ignoring if you disobey.

Go to divination, as Socrates thought one should, only in matters where the whole question turns on the outcome, and where neither reason nor any other skill offers a way to see what lies ahead. So when you must share a danger with a friend or with your country, do not ask the diviner whether you should share it. For even if the diviner tells you that the omens are bad, it is clear that what is signified is death, or the maiming of some part of the body, or exile; but reason requires, even with these things in view, that you stand by your friend and share your country’s danger. So pay attention to the greater diviner, the Pythian god, who threw out of his temple the man who had not helped his friend while he was being murdered.

## 33

Lay down for yourself, from now on, a certain character and pattern, which you will keep both when you are alone and when you are with people.

Be silent for the most part, or say only what is necessary, and in few words. Rarely, when the occasion calls for it, do speak, but not about any of the usual subjects: not about gladiators, not about horse races, not about athletes, not about food or drink, the things talked about everywhere; and above all not about people, blaming or praising or comparing them.

If you can, then, by your own conversation bring that of your companions round to what is fitting. But if you happen to find yourself among strangers, be silent.

Do not laugh much, nor at many things, nor without restraint.

Refuse to take an oath, altogether if you can, and if not, as far as you can. Avoid the dinners given by outsiders and ordinary people.

But if the occasion ever arises, keep your attention strained, so that you do not slip into ordinary ways. For know that if your companion is dirty, the man who rubs up against him is bound to get dirty too, even if he happens to be clean himself.

As for things that concern the body, take them only so far as bare need requires: food, drink, clothing, house, servants. Cut out entirely everything that is for show or luxury. As for sex, keep yourself pure before marriage as far as you can.

But if you do indulge in it, do only what is lawful. Do not, however, be offensive or censorious to those who indulge in it, and do not keep bringing up the fact that you yourself do not.

If someone tells you that so-and-so is speaking badly of you, do not defend yourself against what was said, but answer: “He did not know my other faults, or he would not have mentioned only these.”

There is no need to go to the theatre often. But if the occasion ever arises, do not seem to be anyone’s partisan but your own; that is, want only what happens to happen, and only the winner to win; for then you will not be frustrated. Keep away completely from shouting, and from laughing at anyone, and from getting very worked up. And after you leave, do not talk much about what happened, so far as it does nothing for your own improvement; for that would show that you were dazzled by the show.

Do not go to people’s public readings at random or readily. But when you do go, keep your dignity and composure, and at the same time do not be disagreeable.

When you are about to meet someone, especially one of those who are thought to be important, put this question to yourself: “What would Socrates or Zeno have done here?” And you will not be at a loss how to deal properly with what comes up.

When you go to see one of the very powerful, put it to yourself that you will not find him in, that you will be shut out, that the doors will be slammed in your face, that he will pay no attention to you. And if, with all this, it is your duty to go, go, and bear what happens; and never say to yourself, “It was not worth it.” For that is the mark of an ordinary man, and of one at odds with external things.

In company, avoid talking at length and without measure about your own deeds or dangers. For however pleasant it is for you to recall your dangers, it is not as pleasant for others to hear about what happened to you.

Avoid also trying to raise a laugh. For it is a slippery habit that slides into vulgarity, and at the same time it is enough to lower the respect your neighbours have for you.

It is risky, too, to drift into foul talk. So when anything of the kind happens, if the moment is right, even rebuke the man who has drifted into it; if not, at least by falling silent, and blushing, and looking stern, make it clear that you dislike the talk.

## 34

When you get the impression of some pleasure, as with other impressions, guard yourself so as not to be carried away by it. Let the thing wait for you, and give yourself a little delay. Then call to mind both times: the time when you will enjoy the pleasure, and the time afterwards when, having enjoyed it, you will regret it and reproach yourself. And set against these how glad you will be if you hold back, and how you will praise yourself. But if it seems the right moment to set about the thing, take care that its charm and sweetness and attraction do not overcome you; but set against all this how much better it is to know that you have won this victory.

## 35

When you do something, having decided that it ought to be done, never avoid being seen doing it, even if most people are going to think badly of it. For if you are not acting rightly, avoid the act itself; but if you are acting rightly, why are you afraid of those who will rebuke you wrongly?

## 36

Just as the statement “It is day, and it is night” has great value as an either-or, but none as a both-and, so too, at a dinner, taking the larger portion may have value for the body, but has none for keeping up the fellowship that should be kept. So when you eat with someone else, remember to look not only at the value of the dishes for your body, but also to keep up your respect for your host.

## 37

If you take on a role beyond your powers, you both disgrace yourself in it, and neglect the role you could have filled.

## 38

Just as, when you walk about, you take care not to step on a nail or twist your foot, so take care not to harm your own ruling mind. And if we watch for this in every task, we shall set about the task more safely.

## 39

The measure of possessions for each person is the body, as the foot is the measure of the shoe. If you keep to this, you will keep the measure; but if you go beyond it, you are bound from then on to be carried over the edge of a cliff. So it is with the shoe: if you go beyond the foot, the shoe becomes gilded, then purple, then embroidered. For once you go beyond the measure, there is no limit.

## 40

Women, from the age of fourteen, are at once called “ladies” by men. So, seeing that they have nothing else to offer but to go to bed with men, they begin to make themselves pretty and put all their hopes in this. It is worth taking care, then, that they come to see that they are honoured for nothing else than for being seen to be modest and self-respecting.

## 41

It is a sign of a dull nature to spend a long time on things that concern the body: to exercise a great deal, eat a great deal, drink a great deal, spend a great deal of time in the lavatory or in sex. These things should be done on the side; let all your attention go to your mind.

## 42

When someone treats you badly or speaks badly of you, remember that he does or says it because he thinks it is right for him. He cannot follow what seems right to you, but only what seems right to him; so if it seems wrong to him, it is he who is harmed, since he is the one who is deceived. For if someone takes a true but complex statement to be false, it is not the statement that is harmed, but the man who is deceived. Starting from this, then, you will be gentle with the man who abuses you. Say to yourself each time: “It seemed so to him.”

## 43

Everything has two handles: one by which it can be carried, and one by which it cannot. If your brother wrongs you, do not take hold of it by this handle, that he is wronging you (for this is the handle by which it cannot be carried); take it rather by the other, that he is your brother, that he was brought up with you; and you will be taking hold of it by the handle by which it can be carried.

## 44

These arguments do not follow: “I am richer than you, therefore I am better than you”; “I am more eloquent than you, therefore I am better than you.” These follow rather: “I am richer than you, therefore my property is better than yours”; “I am more eloquent than you, therefore my speech is better than yours.” But you are neither property nor speech.

## 45

Someone bathes quickly: do not say that he bathes badly, but that he bathes quickly. Someone drinks a lot of wine: do not say that he drinks badly, but that he drinks a lot. For until you have understood his reasons, how do you know whether he is doing it badly? In this way you will not find yourself grasping one thing clearly and giving your assent to another.

## 46

Never call yourself a philosopher, and do not talk much among ordinary people about principles; but act on the principles. For example, at a dinner do not say how one ought to eat, but eat as one ought. For remember that Socrates had so completely got rid of any wish to show off that people used to come to him wanting him to introduce them to philosophers, and he would take them along.

So well did he put up with being overlooked. And if talk arises among ordinary people about some principle, be silent for the most part; for there is a great danger that you will at once throw up what you have not digested. And when someone tells you that you know nothing, and you are not stung, then know that you have begun the work. For sheep do not bring their fodder to the shepherds to show how much they have eaten; they digest the grass inside, and outside they produce wool and milk. So you too, do not show your principles to ordinary people, but show them the deeds that come from the principles once they are digested.

## 47

When you have got your body used to simple living, do not pride yourself on it; and if you drink water, do not say at every opportunity that you drink water. And if you ever want to train yourself to endure hardship, do it for yourself and not for the people outside. Do not go hugging statues. But some time, when you are very thirsty, take a mouthful of cold water, and spit it out, and tell no one.

## 48

The position and character of an ordinary man: he never looks for help or harm from himself, but from things outside. The position and character of a philosopher: he looks for all help and harm from himself.

Signs of someone who is making progress: he blames no one, praises no one, finds fault with no one, accuses no one, says nothing about himself as if he were somebody or knew something. When he is hindered or prevented in anything, he accuses himself. If anyone praises him, he laughs to himself at the man who praises him; and if anyone blames him, he does not defend himself. He goes about like an invalid, careful not to disturb any part that is healing before it has grown firm.

He has removed all desire from himself, and has transferred his aversion to those things only, among the things up to us, that are contrary to nature. He uses impulse towards everything gently. If he seems foolish or ignorant, he does not care. In a word, he keeps watch over himself as over an enemy lying in wait.

## 49

When someone prides himself on being able to understand and explain the books of Chrysippus, say to yourself: “If Chrysippus had not written obscurely, this man would have nothing to pride himself on.” But what do I want? To understand nature and to follow it. So I look for someone to explain it; and hearing that Chrysippus does, I go to him. But I do not understand what he has written; so I look for someone to explain it. So far there is nothing to be proud of. And when I find the person who explains it, what is left is to put what is taught into practice: this alone is something to be proud of. But if I admire the explaining itself, what have I become but a literary scholar instead of a philosopher? Except that I explain Chrysippus instead of Homer. Rather, when someone says to me, “Read me some Chrysippus,” I blush, when I cannot show deeds that match his words and agree with them.

## 50

Whatever rules are set before you, keep to them as laws, as if it would be impious to break them. And whatever anyone says about you, do not turn round to listen; for that is no longer yours.

## 51

How much longer will you put off thinking yourself worthy of the best, and never going against what reason marks out? You have received the principles you ought to accept, and you have accepted them. What teacher, then, are you still waiting for, so that you can put off setting yourself right until he comes? You are no longer a boy, but already a grown man. If you are careless now and lazy, and keep making plan after plan, and fixing one day after another after which you will attend to yourself, you will fail to notice that you are making no progress, and you will go on being an ordinary person, in life and in death.

So decide now that you are worthy to live as a grown man and one who is making progress; and let everything that seems best to you be a law you do not break. And if anything hard or pleasant, glorious or inglorious, is put before you, remember that the contest is now, that the Olympic games are already here, that you can no longer put things off, and that progress is lost or saved by a single day and a single act.

That is how Socrates became what he was: in everything that came to him, he paid attention to nothing but reason. And you, even if you are not yet Socrates, ought to live as one who wants to be Socrates.

## 52

The first and most necessary part of philosophy is the one that deals with putting principles into practice, for example, not to lie. The second deals with proofs, for example, why one ought not to lie. The third confirms and analyses these, for example, why this is a proof. For what is a proof, what is a consequence, what is a contradiction, what is true, what is false?

So the third part is necessary because of the second, and the second because of the first; but the most necessary, and the one where we ought to rest, is the first. But we do the opposite. We spend our time on the third part, and all our effort goes into it, and we neglect the first altogether. And so we lie; but we have ready to hand the proof of why one ought not to lie.

## 53

On every occasion we should have these thoughts ready to hand:

> Lead me, O Zeus, and you, my Destiny, wherever you have once appointed me. For I will follow, and not hesitate; and if I will not, and turn coward, I shall follow none the less.
— Cleanthes

> Whoever has given way well to necessity is wise in our eyes, and knows the things of God.
— Euripides

> But, Crito, if this is how it pleases the gods, so let it be.
— Socrates, in Plato’s Crito

> Anytus and Meletus can kill me, but they cannot harm me.
— Socrates, in Plato’s Apology
