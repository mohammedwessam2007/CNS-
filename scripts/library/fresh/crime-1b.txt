## CHAPTER II

Raskolnikov was not used to crowds and, as has been said, shunned all company, especially lately. But now something suddenly drew him to people. Something seemed to be happening in him that was new, and along with it he felt a kind of thirst for people. He was so tired after a whole month of this concentrated misery and gloomy excitement that he wanted, even for one minute, to breathe in another world, whatever world it might be; and, in spite of all the filth of the place, he was glad now to stay in the tavern.

The owner of the establishment was in another room, but he often came into the main one, coming down into it by some steps from somewhere, and as he did so the first thing to appear was always his dandyish blacked boots with big red tops. He wore a peasant’s coat and a terribly greasy black satin waistcoat, with no tie, and his whole face looked as if it had been oiled, like an iron lock. Behind the counter was a boy of about fourteen, and there was another, younger boy, who served whatever was asked for. There were chopped cucumbers, black rusks and fish cut into pieces; all this smelled very bad. It was so stuffy that it was almost unbearable to sit there, and everything was so soaked in the smell of drink that it seemed one could get drunk in five minutes from the air alone.

There are some meetings, even with people completely unknown to us, in which we begin to take an interest at first sight, somehow suddenly, before we have said a word. That was exactly the impression made on Raskolnikov by the customer who sat a little way off and looked like a retired official. The young man often recalled this first impression afterwards, and even put it down to a presentiment. He kept glancing at the official, of course partly because the man himself was staring at him persistently, and it was plain that he very much wanted to start a conversation. At the others in the tavern, not excluding the owner, the official looked in a habitual and even bored way, and at the same time with a shade of haughty disdain, as at people of lower station and development with whom he had nothing to talk about. He was a man already over fifty, of medium height and solid build, greying, with a large bald patch, with a yellow, even greenish face swollen from constant drinking, and with puffy eyelids, from behind which shone tiny eyes like slits, reddish but full of life. But there was something very strange about him; there seemed to be a light of something like rapture in his look (perhaps there was sense and intelligence too), but at the same time something like madness flickered in it. He was dressed in an old black tailcoat, completely in tatters, which had lost its buttons. Only one still held on somehow, and that was the one he fastened, evidently not wishing to abandon propriety. A shirt front stuck out from under his nankeen waistcoat, all crumpled, dirty and stained. His face was shaved in an official’s way, but long ago, so that a thick bluish stubble had begun to show. And there really was something solidly official in his manners. But he was restless; he ruffled his hair and sometimes, in misery, propped his head on both hands, resting his torn elbows on the wet and sticky table. At last he looked straight at Raskolnikov and said loudly and firmly:

“May I make so bold, my dear sir, as to address you with a decent conversation? For although you are not of distinguished appearance, my experience picks you out as an educated man and one not used to drink. I myself have always respected education joined with feelings of the heart, and, moreover, I am a titular councillor. Marmeladov: such is my name; titular councillor. May I make so bold as to ask whether you have been in the service?”

“No, I am studying...” the young man answered, somewhat surprised both by the peculiar flowery tone of the speech and by being addressed so directly, point-blank. In spite of his momentary wish, a little before, for some company of people, whatever it might be, at the first word really addressed to him he suddenly felt his usual unpleasant and irritable feeling of disgust towards every stranger who touched, or even only wanted to touch, his person.

“A student, then, or a former student!” cried the official. “Just as I thought! Experience, my dear sir, repeated experience!” And by way of boasting he put his finger to his forehead. “You have been a student or have followed a learned course! But allow me...” He half got up, swayed, picked up his bottle and glass, and sat down beside the young man, a little at an angle to him. He was drunk, but he talked fluently and briskly, only now and then losing the thread a little here and there and dragging out his speech. He fell upon Raskolnikov with a kind of greed, as if he too had not talked to anyone for a whole month.

“My dear sir,” he began almost solemnly, “poverty is not a vice: that is a truth. I know that drunkenness is not a virtue either, and that all the more. But destitution, my dear sir, destitution is a vice. In poverty you still keep the nobility of your innate feelings, but in destitution never, nobody. For destitution they do not even drive you out of human company with a stick; they sweep you out with a broom, to make it all the more insulting; and rightly, for in destitution I am the first to be ready to insult myself. And hence the drinking! My dear sir, a month ago Mr Lebezyatnikov beat my wife, and my wife is not like me! Do you understand? Allow me to ask you something else, if only out of simple curiosity: have you ever spent the night on the Neva, on the hay barges?”

“No, I have not,” answered Raskolnikov. “What is that?”

“Well, that is where I have come from, and it is the fifth night now...”

He poured himself a glass, drank it, and fell to thinking. And indeed, wisps of hay could be seen clinging here and there to his clothes and even in his hair. It was very likely that he had not undressed or washed for five days. His hands especially were dirty, greasy and red, with black nails.

His talk seemed to have roused general, if lazy, attention. The boys behind the counter began to snigger. The owner seemed to have come down from the upper room on purpose to listen to the “joker”, and sat a little way off, yawning lazily but importantly. Evidently Marmeladov had long been known here. And he had probably acquired his taste for flowery speech from the habit of frequent tavern conversations with various strangers. In some drinkers this habit turns into a need, especially in those who are treated strictly at home and ordered about. That is why, in the company of drinkers, they always try as it were to win themselves some justification, and if possible even respect.

“Joker!” said the owner loudly. “And why don’t you work, why aren’t you in the service, if you’re an official?”

“Why am I not in the service, my dear sir?” Marmeladov took him up, addressing himself only to Raskolnikov, as if it were he who had asked him the question. “Why am I not in the service? And does my heart not ache that I grovel to no purpose? When Mr Lebezyatnikov, a month ago, beat my wife with his own hands, and I lay there drunk, did I not suffer? Allow me, young man, have you ever... hm... well, ever had to ask for a loan hopelessly?”

“I have... that is, what do you mean, hopelessly?”

“That is, completely hopelessly, knowing beforehand that nothing will come of it. You know, for instance, beforehand and thoroughly that this man, this most well-meaning and most useful citizen, will on no account give you any money, for why, I ask, should he give it? He knows I will not pay it back. Out of compassion? But Mr Lebezyatnikov, who follows the new ideas, explained the other day that compassion in our time is even forbidden by science, and that this is already how things are done in England, where they have political economy. Why, then, I ask, should he give it? And so, knowing beforehand that he will not give, you set off all the same and...”

“Why go, then?” put in Raskolnikov.

“But if there is no one, if there is nowhere else to go! For every man must have somewhere he can go. For there are times when one absolutely must go somewhere! When my only daughter went out for the first time with the yellow ticket, I went out then too... (for my daughter lives by the yellow ticket...),” he added in parenthesis, looking at the young man with some uneasiness. “It is nothing, my dear sir, nothing!” he hastened at once to declare, apparently calmly, when both boys behind the counter snorted and the owner himself smiled. “It is nothing! I am not troubled by this shaking of heads, for everything is already known to everyone, and every secret is made plain; and I regard it not with contempt but with humility. Let it be! Let it be! ‘Behold the man!’ Allow me, young man: can you... But no, to put it more strongly and vividly: not can you, but dare you, looking at me at this hour, say for certain that I am not a swine?”

The young man did not answer a word.

“Well, sir,” the orator went on, having waited solidly and even this time with heightened dignity for the sniggering that again followed in the room. “Well, sir, let me be a swine, but she is a lady! I have the image of a beast, but Katerina Ivanovna, my wife, is a person of education and a staff officer’s daughter by birth. Let me be a scoundrel, let me be; but she is full of a lofty heart and feelings ennobled by upbringing. And yet... oh, if only she would pity me! My dear sir, my dear sir, for every man must have at least one place where he is pitied too! But Katerina Ivanovna, though she is a generous lady, is unjust... And although I myself understand that when she pulls my hair she pulls it only out of pity of the heart (for, I repeat without embarrassment, she pulls my hair, young man,” he confirmed with doubled dignity, hearing the sniggering again), “but, God, what if she only once... But no! No! All that is in vain, and there is nothing to say! nothing to say!.. for what I wished has happened more than once already, and more than once already they have pitied me, but... such is my nature, and I am a born brute!”

“I should say so!” remarked the owner, yawning.

Marmeladov struck the table firmly with his fist.

“Such is my nature! Do you know, do you know, my dear sir, that I have even drunk away her stockings? Not her shoes, for that would be at least something like the order of things, but her stockings, I have drunk away her stockings! Her little goat’s-down shawl I drank away too, a present, from before, her own, not mine; and we live in a cold corner, and this winter she caught a chill and began to cough, with blood now. And we have three little children, and Katerina Ivanovna works from morning to night, scrubbing and washing and washing the children, for she has been used to cleanliness from childhood, and with a weak chest and inclined to consumption, and I feel it. Do I not feel it? And the more I drink, the more I feel it. That is why I drink: because in this drinking I seek compassion and feeling. Not merriment, but sorrow alone I seek... I drink because I want to suffer doubly!” And, as if in despair, he laid his head on the table.

“Young man,” he went on, raising his head again, “in your face I read as it were a certain sorrow. As soon as you came in I read it, and that is why I spoke to you at once. For in telling you the story of my life I do not want to make a spectacle of myself before these idlers, who know it all already, but I seek a man of feeling and education. Know, then, that my wife was educated in a noble provincial institute for daughters of the gentry, and at her leaving she danced the shawl dance before the governor and other persons, for which she received a gold medal and a certificate of merit. The medal... well, the medal was sold... long ago... hm... the certificate of merit still lies in her trunk, and only lately she showed it to our landlady. And although she has the most incessant quarrels with the landlady, she wanted to be proud before someone at least and to tell of her happy days gone by. And I do not condemn it, I do not condemn it, for that is the last thing left her in her memories, and all the rest has turned to dust! Yes, yes; a lady who is hot-tempered, proud and unbending. She washes the floor herself and lives on black bread, but she will not allow disrespect to herself. That is why she would not let Mr Lebezyatnikov’s rudeness pass, and when Mr Lebezyatnikov beat her for it, she took to her bed, not so much from the blows as from her feelings. I married her when she was a widow already, with three children, each smaller than the last. She married her first husband, an infantry officer, for love, and ran away with him from her parents’ house. She loved her husband beyond measure, but he took to cards, was brought to trial, and with that he died. He used to beat her at the end; and though she did not let him get away with it, as I know for certain and from documents, she still remembers him with tears to this day and reproaches me with him, and I am glad, I am glad, for in her imaginings at least she sees herself as once happy... And after him she was left with three small children in a remote and savage district, where I was too at the time, and she was left in such hopeless destitution that, though I have seen many adventures of various kinds, I am not even able to describe it. Her relations all refused to help. And she was proud, too proud... And it was then, my dear sir, then that I, a widower too, with a fourteen-year-old daughter by my first wife, offered her my hand, for I could not look on at such suffering. You can judge from this how far her misfortunes had gone, that she, educated and well brought up and of a known family, agreed to marry me! But she did! Weeping and sobbing and wringing her hands, she did! For there was nowhere to go. Do you understand, do you understand, my dear sir, what it means when there is nowhere else to go? No! That you do not understand yet... And for a whole year I fulfilled my duty piously and in holiness, and did not touch this” (he jabbed his finger at the bottle), “for I have feeling. But even so I could not please her; and then I lost my post, and again through no fault of mine, but because of a change in the staff, and then I touched it!.. It will be a year and a half ago now that we found ourselves at last, after wanderings and numerous misfortunes, in this magnificent capital, adorned with numerous monuments. And here I got a post... I got it and lost it again. Do you understand? This time I lost it through my own fault, for my nature came upon me... And now we live in a corner, at the landlady Amalia Fyodorovna Lippewechsel’s, and what we live on and what we pay with, I do not know. Many others live there besides us... A Sodom, sir, most hideous... hm... yes... And meanwhile my daughter by my first marriage grew up, and what she had to bear, my daughter, from her stepmother as she grew up, of that I will keep silent. For although Katerina Ivanovna is full of generous feelings, she is a hot-tempered and irritable lady, and she snaps... Yes, sir! Well, there is no point in remembering that! As you can imagine, Sonya received no education. I tried, four years or so ago, to go through geography and world history with her; but since I myself was not strong in that knowledge, and there were no proper textbooks for it either, for what little books we had... hm!.. well, those little books are gone now, so that was the end of all the teaching. We stopped at Cyrus of Persia. Later, when she had reached a mature age, she read a few books of a romantic kind, and lately, through Mr Lebezyatnikov, one more little book, Lewes’s ‘Physiology’, do you know it, sir? She read it with great interest and even read bits of it aloud to us: and that is all her enlightenment. Now I will turn to you, my dear sir, on my own account with a private question: how much, in your opinion, can a poor but honest girl earn by honest work?.. She will not earn fifteen kopecks a day, sir, if she is honest and has no special talents, and that working without stopping! And even so, Civil Councillor Klopstock, Ivan Ivanovich (have you heard of him?), not only has not paid her to this day for sewing half a dozen linen shirts, but even drove her out with insults, stamping his feet and calling her improper names, on the pretext that the shirt collars were not made to measure and were crooked. And there are the little ones hungry... And there is Katerina Ivanovna walking about the room wringing her hands, and red spots coming out on her cheeks, as always happens in that illness: ‘You live with us,’ she says, ‘you sponger, you eat and drink and enjoy the warmth,’ but what is there to eat and drink when even the little ones do not see a crust for three days! I was lying there then... well, what of it! I was lying there drunk, sir, and I hear my Sonya say (she never answers back, and she has such a gentle little voice... fair-haired, her little face always pale and thin), she says: ‘Well, Katerina Ivanovna, am I really to go and do that?’ For Darya Frantsevna, a woman of evil intent and well known to the police many times over, had already been asking three times through the landlady. ‘Well, why not,’ answers Katerina Ivanovna mockingly, ‘what is there to keep? Such a treasure!’ But do not blame her, do not blame her, my dear sir, do not blame her! It was not said in her right mind, but with her feelings in turmoil, in her illness, with the children crying who had not eaten, and it was said more to insult than in its exact meaning... For that is Katerina Ivanovna’s character, and when the children cry, even from hunger, she starts beating them at once. And I see, about six o’clock, Sonechka get up, put on her headscarf, put on her little cloak and go out of the flat, and at nine o’clock she came back again. She came in and went straight to Katerina Ivanovna and laid thirty roubles on the table before her in silence. She did not say a word as she did it, she did not even look at her, but only took our big green drap-de-dames shawl (we have one shawl like that for all of us, drap-de-dames), covered her head and face with it completely, and lay down on the bed with her face to the wall, only her little shoulders and her whole body kept trembling... And I was lying there in the same state as before, sir... And then I saw, young man, I saw how Katerina Ivanovna, also without saying a word, went up to Sonechka’s bed and knelt at her feet the whole evening, kissed her feet, would not get up, and then they both fell asleep like that together, in each other’s arms... both of them... both... yes, sir... and I... lay there drunk.”

Marmeladov fell silent, as if his voice had failed. Then he suddenly poured hastily, drank, and cleared his throat.

“Since then, my dear sir,” he went on after a short silence, “since then, through an unfortunate circumstance and through information given by ill-intentioned persons (to which Darya Frantsevna especially contributed, supposedly because she had not been shown proper respect), since then my daughter, Sofya Semyonovna, has been obliged to take the yellow ticket, and because of that she could no longer stay with us. For the landlady, Amalia Fyodorovna, would not allow it (though she herself had helped Darya Frantsevna before), and Mr Lebezyatnikov too... hm... It was over Sonya that that business with Katerina Ivanovna happened to him. At first he was after Sonechka himself, and then suddenly he got on his high horse: ‘What,’ he says, ‘am I, such an enlightened man, to live in the same flat with one of that sort?’ But Katerina Ivanovna would not let it pass, she stood up for her... and so it happened... And now Sonechka comes to us mostly at dusk, and helps Katerina Ivanovna, and brings what means she can... She lives in lodgings at the tailor Kapernaumov’s, she rents a room from them, and Kapernaumov is lame and has a stammer, and all his very numerous family stammer too. And his wife stammers too... They all live in one room, and Sonya has her own, separate, with a partition... Hm, yes... The poorest of people, and stammerers... yes... Only, I got up that morning, sir, put on my rags, raised my hands to heaven and went to His Excellency Ivan Afanasyevich. Do you know His Excellency Ivan Afanasyevich?.. No? Well then, you do not know a man of God! He is wax... wax before the face of the Lord; as wax melts!.. He even shed tears when he had heard it all. ‘Well, Marmeladov,’ he says, ‘you have deceived my expectations once already... I take you on once more, on my own personal responsibility’: that is what he said. ‘Remember,’ he says, ‘go!’ I kissed the dust of his feet, in my mind, for in reality he would not have allowed it, being a dignitary and a man of new political and educated ideas; I went home, and when I announced that I had been taken back into the service and would be getting a salary, Lord, what happened then!..”

Marmeladov stopped again in great agitation. At that moment a whole party of drinkers came in from the street, already drunk as it was, and at the entrance there sounded a hired barrel organ and a child’s cracked seven-year-old voice singing “The Little Farm”. It became noisy. The owner and the servers were busy with the newcomers. Marmeladov, paying no attention to them, went on with his story. He seemed already very weak, but the drunker he got, the more talkative he became. The memory of his recent success in the service seemed to have revived him, and was even reflected in his face as a kind of radiance. Raskolnikov listened attentively.

“This was, my dear sir, five weeks ago. Yes... As soon as they both found out, Katerina Ivanovna and Sonechka, Lord, it was as if I had moved into the kingdom of God. It used to be: lie there like a brute, nothing but abuse! But now: they walk on tiptoe, they hush the children: ‘Semyon Zakharych is tired from his work, he is resting, shh!’ They give me coffee before I go to work, they boil cream! They began to get real cream, do you hear! And where they scraped together eleven roubles fifty kopecks for a decent uniform for me, I do not understand. Boots, calico shirt fronts, most magnificent, a uniform coat: they made it all up for eleven and a half in the most excellent style. I came home from work the first day, and I look: Katerina Ivanovna has cooked two courses, soup and salt beef with horseradish, of which until then there had been no idea at all. She has no dresses of any kind... that is, none at all, sir, and yet it was as if she were going visiting; she dressed up, and not with anything in particular, but they know how to make everything out of nothing: they do their hair, a clean little collar of some sort, little cuffs, and there you have quite a different person, younger and prettier. Sonechka, my little dove, only helped with money, but for me myself, she says, it is not proper for now to come to you often, only perhaps at dusk, so that no one sees. Do you hear, do you hear? I came in after dinner to have a sleep, and what do you think? Katerina Ivanovna could not contain herself: only a week before she had quarrelled with the landlady, with Amalia Fyodorovna, in the worst way, and now she invited her for a cup of coffee. They sat for two hours and kept whispering: ‘How Semyon Zakharych is in the service now and gets a salary, and went to His Excellency himself, and His Excellency came out himself, told everyone to wait, and led Semyon Zakharych past everyone by the hand into his study.’ Do you hear, do you hear? ‘I, of course,’ he says, ‘Semyon Zakharych, remembering your services, and although you have indulged in that frivolous weakness, since you now promise, and since besides things have gone badly here without you’ (do you hear, do you hear!), ‘so I rely now,’ he says, ‘on your word of honour.’ That is, all this, I tell you, she simply made up, and not out of frivolity, just for boasting, sir! No, sir, she believes it all herself, she comforts herself with her own imaginings, by God, sir! And I do not condemn it; no, that I do not condemn!.. And when, six days ago, I brought home my first salary, twenty-three roubles forty kopecks, in full, she called me her little darling: ‘You little darling, you!’ she says. And when we were alone, sir, do you understand? Well, what beauty is there in me, I ask you, and what sort of husband am I? No, she pinched my cheek: ‘You little darling, you!’ she says.”

Marmeladov stopped and was about to smile, but suddenly his chin began to twitch. He controlled himself, however. This tavern, his depraved appearance, five nights on the hay barges, and the bottle, and at the same time this sick love for his wife and family, bewildered his listener. Raskolnikov listened intently, but with a painful feeling. He was annoyed that he had come in here.

“My dear sir, my dear sir!” exclaimed Marmeladov, recovering himself. “Oh, my dear sir, perhaps all this is a joke to you, as it is to the others, and I am only troubling you with the stupidity of all these wretched details of my home life; but to me it is no joke! For I can feel all this... And all through that heavenly day of my life and all that evening I passed my time in fleeting dreams myself: how I would arrange it all, and dress the little ones, and give her rest, and bring my only daughter back from dishonour into the bosom of the family... And much, much more... It is permissible, sir. Well, sir, my dear sir” (Marmeladov suddenly seemed to start, raised his head and looked point-blank at his listener), “well, sir, and on the very next day after all these dreams (that is, exactly five days ago), towards evening, by cunning deceit, like a thief in the night, I stole the key of her trunk from Katerina Ivanovna, took out what was left of the salary I had brought, how much I no longer remember, and there you are, look at me, all of you! The fifth day away from home, and they are looking for me there, and the service is finished, and the uniform is lying in a tavern by the Egyptian Bridge, in exchange for which I got these clothes... and everything is finished!”

Marmeladov struck himself on the forehead with his fist, clenched his teeth, shut his eyes, and leaned heavily with his elbow on the table. But a minute later his face suddenly changed, and with a kind of put-on slyness and affected insolence he glanced at Raskolnikov, laughed and said:

“And today I was at Sonya’s; I went to ask for something for a hair of the dog! He-he-he!”

“Did she really give it to you?” someone shouted from among the newcomers; he shouted and roared with laughter.

“This very bottle was bought with her money,” said Marmeladov, addressing himself only to Raskolnikov. “She brought me out thirty kopecks with her own hands, her last, all she had, I saw it myself... She said nothing, she only looked at me in silence... Like that, not on earth but up there... they grieve over people, they weep, but they do not reproach, they do not reproach! And that hurts more, sir, it hurts more, when they do not reproach!.. Thirty kopecks, yes, sir. And she needs them now herself, eh? What do you think, my dear sir? For now she has to keep herself clean. That cleanliness costs money, that special kind, you understand? You understand? Well, and she must buy pomade too, she cannot do without; starched petticoats, a little boot of the smart sort, to show her foot when she has to step over a puddle. Do you understand, do you understand, sir, what that cleanliness means? Well, sir, and here am I, her own father, and I took those thirty kopecks for a hair of the dog! And I am drinking it! And I have drunk it already!.. Well, who will pity a man like me? Eh? Do you pity me now, sir, or not? Tell me, sir, do you pity me or not? He-he-he-he!”

He was about to pour, but there was nothing left. The bottle was empty.

“And why should anyone pity you?” shouted the owner, who was beside them again.

There was laughter and even swearing. Those who had listened and those who had not laughed and swore, just from looking at the figure of the retired official.

“Pity! Why pity me!” Marmeladov suddenly cried out, getting up with his hand stretched out in front of him, in absolute inspiration, as if he had only been waiting for these words. “Why pity, you say? Yes! There is nothing to pity me for! I ought to be crucified, crucified on a cross, not pitied! But crucify, judge, crucify and, having crucified, pity him! And then I myself will come to you to be crucified, for it is not merriment I thirst for, but sorrow and tears!.. Do you think, you seller, that this bottle of yours was sweet to me? Sorrow, sorrow I sought at the bottom of it, sorrow and tears, and I have tasted them and found them; but he will pity us who pitied all and who understood all and everything, he alone, he is the judge. He will come on that day and ask: ‘And where is the daughter who gave herself for a wicked, consumptive stepmother, for children who were not her own and small? Where is the daughter who pitied her earthly father, a worthless drunkard, not horrified at his brutishness?’ And he will say: ‘Come! I have forgiven you once... I have forgiven you once... Your sins, which are many, are forgiven you now too, for you loved much...’ And he will forgive my Sonya, he will forgive her, I know he will... I felt it in my heart just now, when I was at her place!.. And he will judge and forgive all, the good and the wicked, the wise and the meek... And when he has finished with all of them, then he will say to us too: ‘Come out, you too! Come out, you drunk ones, come out, you weak ones, come out, you shameless ones!’ And we shall all come out, without shame, and stand there. And he will say: ‘You swine! You are made in the image of the beast and bear its mark; but come, you too!’ And the wise will say, the clever will say: ‘Lord! Why do you receive these?’ And he will say: ‘I receive them, you wise ones, I receive them, you clever ones, because not one of them ever thought himself worthy of it...’ And he will stretch out his hands to us, and we shall fall down... and weep... and understand everything! Then we shall understand everything!.. and everyone will understand... and Katerina Ivanovna... she too will understand... Lord, thy kingdom come!”

And he sank down on the bench, exhausted and drained of strength, looking at no one, as if he had forgotten his surroundings and was deep in thought. His words made a certain impression; for a minute there was silence, but soon the same laughter and swearing broke out again:

“Passed judgement, has he!”

“Talking rubbish!”

“An official!”

And so on, and so on.

“Let us go, sir,” Marmeladov said suddenly, raising his head and turning to Raskolnikov. “Take me home... Kozel’s house, in the courtyard. It is time... to Katerina Ivanovna...”

Raskolnikov had long wanted to leave; and he had thought himself of helping him. Marmeladov turned out to be much weaker in the legs than in his speeches, and leaned heavily on the young man. It was two or three hundred paces. Confusion and fear took hold of the drunkard more and more as they came nearer to the house.

“It is not Katerina Ivanovna I am afraid of now,” he muttered in agitation, “and not that she will start pulling my hair. What is hair!.. Hair is nonsense! I say so! It is even better if she starts pulling it, it is not that I am afraid of... I... I am afraid of her eyes... yes... her eyes... I am afraid of the red spots on her cheeks too... and I am also afraid of her breathing... Have you seen how people breathe in that illness... when their feelings are in turmoil? I am afraid of the children crying too... For if Sonya has not fed them, then... I do not know what! I do not know! But I am not afraid of blows... Know, sir, that such blows are not only no pain to me, but even a pleasure... For I myself cannot do without them. It is better. Let her beat me, let her relieve her soul... it is better... And here is the house. Kozel’s house. A locksmith, a German, rich... lead on!”

They went in from the courtyard and up to the fourth floor. The further they went, the darker the stairs became. It was already almost eleven o’clock, and although at that time of year there is no real night in Petersburg, it was very dark at the top of the stairs.

A small, smoke-blackened door at the end of the stairs, at the very top, stood open. A candle end lit up a very poor room about ten paces long; the whole of it could be seen from the passage. Everything was scattered about in disorder, especially children’s rags of various kinds. A sheet full of holes was hung across the back corner. Behind it there was probably a bed. In the room itself there were only two chairs and a very tattered oilcloth sofa, in front of which stood an old pine kitchen table, unpainted and uncovered. On the edge of the table stood a tallow candle end burning down in an iron candlestick. It turned out that Marmeladov had a room of his own and not a corner, but his room was a passage room. The door into the further rooms or cubicles into which Amalia Lippewechsel’s flat was divided stood ajar. It was noisy and loud in there. People were roaring with laughter. They seemed to be playing cards and drinking tea. Now and then the most unceremonious words flew out.

Raskolnikov recognised Katerina Ivanovna at once. She was a terribly thin woman, slender, rather tall and well made, still with beautiful dark brown hair, and indeed with cheeks flushed to the point of red spots. She was pacing up and down her little room, her hands pressed to her breast, with parched lips, and breathing unevenly, in gasps. Her eyes glittered as if with fever, but her look was sharp and fixed, and this consumptive, agitated face made a painful impression in the last light of the dying candle end flickering on it. She seemed to Raskolnikov to be about thirty, and she really was no match for Marmeladov... She did not hear or notice them come in; she seemed to be in a kind of trance, not hearing and not seeing. The room was stuffy, but she had not opened the window; a stench came up from the stairs, but the door onto the stairs was not shut; from the inner rooms, through the door left ajar, came waves of tobacco smoke; she was coughing, but she did not shut the door. The smallest girl, about six, was asleep on the floor, somehow sitting, huddled up, with her head pressed into the sofa. A boy, a year older than her, was trembling all over in the corner and crying. He had probably just been beaten. The eldest girl, about nine, tall and thin as a matchstick, in nothing but a thin shift torn everywhere and with an old drap-de-dames cloak thrown over her bare shoulders, which had probably been made for her two years before, since it did not reach even to her knees now, was standing in the corner beside her little brother, with her long arm, dried up like a matchstick, around his neck. She seemed to be calming him, whispering something to him, doing everything she could to stop him from whimpering again somehow, and at the same time she was watching her mother fearfully with her big, big dark eyes, which seemed even bigger in her thin, frightened little face. Marmeladov, without going into the room, knelt down in the very doorway and pushed Raskolnikov forward. The woman, seeing a stranger, stopped absently in front of him, coming to herself for a moment and as if wondering: why has he come in? But she must have thought at once that he was going through to the other rooms, since theirs was a passage room. Having worked this out, and paying no more attention to him, she went to the door onto the landing to shut it, and suddenly cried out, seeing her husband on his knees right on the threshold.

“Ah!” she screamed in a frenzy, “he has come back! Convict! Monster!.. And where is the money? What is in your pocket, show me! And those are not your clothes! Where are your clothes? Where is the money? Speak!..”

And she rushed to search him. Marmeladov at once obediently and submissively spread out his arms on both sides to make the search of his pockets easier. There was not a kopeck.

“Where is the money?” she screamed. “Oh Lord, can he have drunk it all! There were twelve roubles left in the trunk!..” And suddenly, in a fury, she seized him by the hair and dragged him into the room. Marmeladov himself made her efforts easier, crawling meekly after her on his knees.

“And this is a pleasure to me! And this is no pain to me but a plea-sure, my dear s-i-r!” he cried out, shaken by the hair, and once even knocking his forehead on the floor. The child asleep on the floor woke up and began to cry. The boy in the corner could not bear it; he trembled, screamed, and rushed to his sister in terrible fright, almost in a fit. The elder girl was trembling like a leaf, still half asleep.

“He has drunk it! Drunk it all, all of it!” the poor woman screamed in despair. “And those are not his clothes! Hungry, hungry!” (and, wringing her hands, she pointed at the children). “Oh, accursed life! And you, are you not ashamed?” she suddenly fell upon Raskolnikov. “From the tavern! Have you been drinking with him? You have been drinking with him too! Get out!”

The young man hurried away without saying a word. Besides, the inner door opened wide, and several curious people peered out of it. Insolent, laughing heads stuck out, with cigarettes and pipes, in skullcaps. There were figures in dressing gowns, wide open, in summer clothes to the point of indecency, some with cards in their hands. They laughed especially merrily when Marmeladov, being dragged by the hair, cried out that it was a pleasure to him. They even began to come into the room; at last an ominous screech was heard: it was Amalia Lippewechsel herself pushing her way forward to put things in order in her own fashion and to frighten the poor woman for the hundredth time with an abusive order to clear out of the flat the very next day. As he left, Raskolnikov managed to slip his hand into his pocket, scooped up as many copper coins as came to hand, left from the rouble he had changed in the tavern, and laid them unnoticed on the window sill. Then, on the stairs, he thought better of it and was about to go back.

“What a stupid thing I have done,” he thought. “They have Sonya, and I need it myself.” But reasoning that it was impossible to take it back now, and that in any case he would not have taken it, he waved his hand and went home to his lodging. “Sonya needs pomade too,” he went on, striding along the street, and he smiled bitterly. “That cleanliness costs money... Hm! And perhaps Sonechka will go bankrupt herself today, since it is the same risk, hunting big game... digging for gold... so they will all be sitting with nothing tomorrow without my money... Hurrah for Sonya! What a well they have managed to dig, though! And they are making use of it! Yes, they are making use of it! And they have got used to it. They wept a little, and they got used to it. Man is a scoundrel; he gets used to everything!”

He fell to thinking.

“Well, but what if I have lied,” he suddenly exclaimed involuntarily, “what if man is not really a scoundrel, man in general, the whole human race, that is: then it means that all the rest is prejudice, nothing but fears put into us, and there are no barriers of any kind, and that is how it should be!..”
