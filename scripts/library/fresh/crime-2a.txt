## CHAPTER III

He woke late the next day, after a troubled sleep, but the sleep had not refreshed him. He woke bilious, irritable, angry, and looked at his little room with hatred. It was a tiny cell about six paces long, which looked most pitiful with its yellowish, dusty wallpaper peeling off the walls everywhere, and so low that a man only a little tall felt uneasy in it and always seemed about to knock his head on the ceiling. The furniture matched the room: there were three old chairs, not quite sound, a painted table in the corner on which lay a few notebooks and books (from the mere fact of how dusty they were one could see that no hand had touched them for a long time), and finally a clumsy big sofa, which took up almost the whole wall and half the width of the room, once covered in chintz but now in tatters, and which served Raskolnikov as a bed. He often slept on it just as he was, without undressing, without a sheet, covering himself with his old, worn-out student’s coat, with one small pillow under his head, under which he put all the linen he had, clean and dirty, to make the head of the bed higher. In front of the sofa stood a little table.

It would have been hard to sink lower or grow more slovenly; but in his present state of mind Raskolnikov even found it pleasant. He had withdrawn from everyone completely, like a tortoise into its shell, and even the face of the servant girl whose job it was to wait on him, and who sometimes looked into his room, stirred bile and convulsions in him. This happens with some monomaniacs who have concentrated too much on something. His landlady had stopped sending up his meals two weeks before, and he had not yet thought of going down to have it out with her, although he was going without dinner. Nastasya, the landlady’s cook and only servant, was partly glad of the lodger’s mood and had stopped tidying and sweeping his room altogether; only once a week or so, by chance, would she sometimes take up a broom. It was she who woke him now.

“Get up, why are you asleep!” she shouted over him. “It’s past nine. I’ve brought you some tea; do you want some tea? You must be starving?”

The lodger opened his eyes, started, and recognised Nastasya.

“Is the tea from the landlady, then?” he asked, slowly and with a sickly look raising himself on the sofa.

“From the landlady, indeed!”

She put her own cracked teapot in front of him, with tea that had already been brewed once, and laid two yellow lumps of sugar beside it.

“Here, Nastasya, take this, please,” he said, fumbling in his pocket (he had slept in his clothes) and pulling out a handful of coppers. “Go and buy me a roll. And get a little sausage at the sausage shop, the cheaper kind.”

“I’ll bring you the roll this very minute; but wouldn’t you like some cabbage soup instead of the sausage? Good soup, yesterday’s. I put some aside for you yesterday, but you came in late. Good soup.”

When the soup had been brought and he had begun on it, Nastasya sat down beside him on the sofa and began to chatter. She was a country woman and a very talkative one.

“Praskovya Pavlovna wants to complain about you to the police,” she said.

He frowned hard.

“To the police? What does she want?”

“You don’t pay and you don’t move out. It’s plain what she wants.”

“Eh, that’s all I needed,” he muttered, grinding his teeth. “No, that would be... inconvenient for me just now... She’s a fool,” he added aloud. “I’ll go and see her today and talk to her.”

“A fool she is, the same as me; but what about you, clever one, lying there like a sack, and nothing to show for it? You used to go and teach children, you say; why don’t you do anything now?”

“I am doing something...” Raskolnikov said reluctantly and sternly.

“Doing what?”

“Work...”

“What work?”

“I am thinking,” he answered seriously, after a pause.

Nastasya fairly rolled with laughter. She was one of those who laugh easily, and when anyone made her laugh she laughed silently, heaving and shaking all over, until she felt quite sick.

“Thought up a lot of money, have you?” she managed to say at last.

“One can’t teach children without boots. And to hell with it anyway.”

“Don’t spit in the well you drink from.”

“They pay coppers for teaching children. What can you do with kopecks?” he went on reluctantly, as if answering his own thoughts.

“And you’d like the whole fortune at once?”

He looked at her strangely.

“Yes, the whole fortune,” he answered firmly, after a pause.

“Well, go easy, or you’ll frighten me; you’re terrible scary. Shall I go for the roll or not?”

“As you like.”

“Oh, I forgot! A letter came for you yesterday while you were out.”

“A letter! For me! From whom?”

“Who from, I don’t know. I gave the postman three kopecks of my own. Will you pay me back?”

“Then bring it, for God’s sake, bring it!” Raskolnikov cried, all in agitation. “Lord!”

A minute later the letter appeared. Just as he thought: from his mother, from R. province. He even turned pale as he took it. He had not had a letter for a long time; but now something else too suddenly gripped his heart.

“Nastasya, go away, for God’s sake; here are your three kopecks, only, for God’s sake, go away quickly!”

The letter trembled in his hands; he did not want to open it in front of her: he wanted to be alone with this letter. When Nastasya had gone, he quickly raised it to his lips and kissed it; then for a long time he gazed at the handwriting of the address, at the small, slanting handwriting of his mother, so familiar and dear to him, who had once taught him to read and write. He hesitated; he even seemed afraid of something. At last he opened it: it was a long, thick letter, weighing two lots; two large sheets of notepaper were covered with very, very small writing.

“My dear Rodya,” his mother wrote, “it is two months and more now since I talked with you in writing, and I have suffered from it myself, and some nights I have not even slept for thinking. But surely you will not blame me for this silence, which I could not help. You know how I love you; you are the only one we have, Dunya and I, you are our everything, all our hope and trust. What became of me when I learned that you had left the university some months ago for lack of means to keep yourself, and that your lessons and your other means had come to an end! How could I help you with my pension of a hundred and twenty roubles a year? The fifteen roubles I sent you four months ago I borrowed, as you know yourself, against this same pension, from our local merchant here, Afanasy Ivanovich Vakhrushin. He is a kind man and was a friend of your father’s. But having given him the right to draw my pension for me, I had to wait until the debt was paid off, and that has only just happened now, so that I could send you nothing all this time. But now, thank God, I think I can send you something more, and in general we can now even boast of good fortune, which I hasten to tell you about. And, first of all, can you guess, dear Rodya, that your sister has been living with me for six weeks now, and we shall not be parted again in future. Thank the Lord, her torments are over; but I will tell you everything in order, so that you may know how it all happened and what we have kept from you until now. When you wrote to me two months ago that you had heard from someone that Dunya was suffering a great deal of rudeness in the Svidrigailovs’ house, and asked me for exact explanations, what could I write to you then in reply? If I had written you the whole truth, you would perhaps have thrown everything up and come to us, even on foot, for I know your character and your feelings, and you would not let your sister be wronged. I was in despair myself, but what could I do? I did not know the whole truth myself then. The chief difficulty was that Dunechka, when she entered their house last year as a governess, had taken a whole hundred roubles in advance, on condition that it was deducted monthly from her salary, and so she could not leave the place without paying off the debt. And she took this sum (now I can explain everything to you, my precious Rodya) chiefly in order to send you the sixty roubles you needed so badly then, and which you received from us last year. We deceived you then; we wrote that it was from Dunechka’s old savings, but that was not so, and now I tell you the whole truth, because everything has now changed suddenly for the better, by God’s will, and so that you may know how Dunya loves you and what a priceless heart she has. Mr Svidrigailov did indeed treat her very rudely at first, and was uncivil to her in various ways, and mocked her at table... But I do not want to go into all these painful details, so as not to upset you for nothing, now that it is all over. In short, in spite of the kind and noble treatment of Marfa Petrovna, Mr Svidrigailov’s wife, and of the whole household, Dunechka had a very hard time, especially when Mr Svidrigailov, from his old regimental habit, was under the influence of Bacchus. But what came out afterwards? Imagine, this madman had long since conceived a passion for Dunya, but hid it all under a show of rudeness and contempt for her. Perhaps he was ashamed himself and horrified, seeing himself already getting on in years and the father of a family, with such frivolous hopes, and so he could not help being angry with Dunya. Or perhaps he only wanted to hide the whole truth from others by the rudeness of his manner and his mockery. But at last he could not restrain himself and dared to make Dunya an open and vile proposal, promising her various rewards and, besides, to throw up everything and go away with her to another estate, or perhaps abroad. You can imagine all her suffering! To leave the place at once was impossible, not only because of the debt, but also to spare Marfa Petrovna, who might suddenly have become suspicious, and so discord would have been sown in the family. And it would have been a great scandal for Dunechka too; it could not have been avoided. There were many other reasons as well, so that Dunya could not possibly hope to get away from that terrible house in less than six weeks. Of course you know Dunya, you know how clever she is and what a firm character she has. Dunechka can bear a great deal, and even in the most extreme cases she finds enough generosity in herself not to lose her firmness. She did not even write to me about it all, so as not to upset me, though we often sent each other news. But the end came unexpectedly. Marfa Petrovna by chance overheard her husband pleading with Dunechka in the garden, and, understanding it all wrongly, blamed her for everything, thinking that she was the cause of it all. There was a terrible scene between them, right there in the garden: Marfa Petrovna even struck Dunya, would not listen to anything, shouted for a whole hour herself, and at last ordered Dunya to be taken back at once to me in town, in a plain peasant’s cart, into which they threw all her things, her linen, her dresses, everything just as it came, not tied up or packed. And then a pouring rain came on, and Dunya, insulted and disgraced, had to ride a whole seventeen versts with a peasant in an open cart. Think now, what could I have written to you in a letter, in reply to yours, which I received two months ago, and what was there to write about? I was in despair myself; I did not dare write you the truth, because you would have been very unhappy, grieved and indignant, and what could you have done? Perhaps ruined yourself into the bargain; and Dunechka forbade it too; and I could not fill a letter with trifles and anything else, when there was such grief in my soul. For a whole month the gossip about this affair went round the whole town, and it came to such a point that Dunya and I could not even go to church because of the contemptuous looks and whispering, and there was even talk out loud in front of us. All our acquaintances drew away from us, they all stopped even bowing to us, and I learned for certain that some shop assistants and some clerks meant to do us a low insult by smearing the gate of our house with tar, so that the landlords began to demand that we move out. The cause of all this was Marfa Petrovna, who had managed to blame and blacken Dunya in every house. She knows everyone here, and that month she kept coming into town, and since she is a little talkative and likes to tell about her family affairs, and especially to complain about her husband to each and every one, which is very wrong, she spread the whole story in a short time, not only in the town but all over the district. I fell ill; but Dunechka was firmer than I, and if you had seen how she bore it all and comforted me and encouraged me! She is an angel! But, by God’s mercy, our torments were cut short: Mr Svidrigailov thought better of it and repented, and, probably pitying Dunya, presented Marfa Petrovna with full and evident proofs of Dunechka’s complete innocence, namely: a letter which Dunya, even before Marfa Petrovna found them in the garden, had been forced to write and give him, to refuse the private explanations and secret meetings he insisted on, and which remained in Mr Svidrigailov’s hands after Dunechka left. In this letter she reproached him in the most ardent way and with complete indignation precisely for the ignobility of his conduct towards Marfa Petrovna; she pointed out to him that he was a father and a family man, and finally how vile it was of him to torment and make unhappy a girl who was already unhappy and defenceless. In a word, dear Rodya, this letter is written so nobly and touchingly that I sobbed as I read it, and to this day I cannot read it without tears. Besides this, in Dunya’s defence, there came at last the evidence of the servants too, who had seen and known far more than Mr Svidrigailov himself supposed, as always happens. Marfa Petrovna was completely thunderstruck and ‘killed all over again’, as she herself admitted to us; but on the other hand she was entirely convinced of Dunechka’s innocence, and the very next day, Sunday, she drove straight to the cathedral and on her knees and in tears prayed to Our Lady to give her strength to bear this new trial and to do her duty. Then, straight from the cathedral, without calling on anyone, she came to us, told us everything, wept bitterly and, in complete repentance, embraced Dunya and begged her to forgive her. That same morning, without the least delay, she went straight from us round all the houses in the town, and everywhere, in the most flattering terms for Dunechka and shedding tears, she restored her innocence and the nobility of her feelings and conduct. More than that, she showed everyone and read aloud Dunechka’s own letter to Mr Svidrigailov, and even let them make copies of it (which, it seems to me, was going too far). So she had to go round everyone in the town for several days running, since some began to take offence that others had been given preference, and so turns were arranged, so that in each house they were already waiting in advance and everyone knew that on such and such a day Marfa Petrovna would be reading this letter at such and such a place, and at every reading, again, even those gathered who had already heard the letter several times both in their own houses and at other acquaintances’ in turn. My opinion is that much, very much of this was unnecessary; but that is Marfa Petrovna’s character. At least she completely restored Dunechka’s honour, and all the vileness of this business fell as an indelible disgrace on her husband, as the chief culprit, so that I even feel sorry for him; they have treated that madman too severely. Dunya was at once invited to give lessons in some houses, but she refused. And in general everyone suddenly began to treat her with special respect. All this chiefly led to the unexpected event through which, one may say, our whole fate is now changing. Know, dear Rodya, that a suitor has asked for Dunya’s hand and that she has already given her consent, which I hasten to tell you as soon as possible. And although this has been done without your advice, you will probably not hold it against me or your sister, since you will see for yourself, from the matter itself, that it would have been impossible for us to wait and put it off until we had your answer. And you could not have judged everything exactly from a distance yourself. This is how it happened. He is already a court councillor, Pyotr Petrovich Luzhin, and a distant relation of Marfa Petrovna’s, who helped a great deal in this. He began by expressing through her a wish to make our acquaintance; he was received properly, drank coffee, and the very next day sent a letter in which he very politely set out his proposal and asked for a quick and decisive answer. He is a man of business and busy, and is hurrying now to Petersburg, so that he values every minute. Of course we were very taken aback at first, since it all happened too quickly and unexpectedly. We considered and thought it over together all that day. He is a reliable man, well provided for; he holds two posts and already has capital of his own. It is true he is forty-five already, but he is of rather pleasant appearance and can still be attractive to women, and in general he is a very solid and respectable man, only a little gloomy and as it were haughty. But perhaps that only seems so at first sight. And I warn you, dear Rodya, when you meet him in Petersburg, which will happen very soon, do not judge too quickly and hotly, as you are inclined to, if at first sight something in him does not seem right to you. I say this just in case, although I am sure he will make a pleasant impression on you. And besides, to come to know anyone at all, one must approach him gradually and carefully, so as not to fall into error and prejudice, which are very hard to correct and smooth over afterwards. And Pyotr Petrovich, at least by many signs, is a most estimable man. On his very first visit he told us that he is a practical man, but that in many things he shares, as he put it himself, ‘the convictions of our newest generations’ and is an enemy of all prejudices. He said a great deal more, because he is somewhat vain and very much likes to be listened to, but that is hardly a vice. I, of course, understood little, but Dunya explained to me that he is a man of not much education, but clever, and, it seems, kind. You know your sister’s character, Rodya. She is a girl who is firm, sensible, patient and generous, though with an ardent heart, as I have come to know well. Of course there is no special love here on either her side or his; but Dunya, besides being a clever girl, is at the same time a creature as noble as an angel, and she will make it her duty to make the happiness of her husband, who in his turn will care for her happiness, and of that, for the present, we have no great reason to doubt, although, I confess, the thing was done rather quickly. Besides, he is a very calculating man, and will surely see for himself that his own happiness in marriage will be the surer, the happier Dunechka is with him. And as for some unevenness of character, some old habits, and even some disagreement in ideas (which cannot be avoided even in the happiest marriages), on this point Dunechka herself told me that she relies on herself; that there is nothing to worry about here, and that she can bear a great deal, on condition that their relations afterwards are honest and just. To me too, for instance, he seemed at first rather abrupt; but that may come precisely from his being a straightforward man, and surely it does. For instance, at his second visit, after he had already received her consent, he said in the course of conversation that even before, without knowing Dunya, he had resolved to take a girl who was honest but without a dowry, and certainly one who had already known hardship; because, as he explained, a husband ought to owe nothing to his wife, and it is much better if the wife regards her husband as her benefactor. I will add that he put it rather more gently and kindly than I have written it, because I have forgotten the actual words and remember only the idea; and besides, he said it not at all on purpose, but evidently letting it slip in the heat of conversation, so that he even tried afterwards to correct himself and soften it; but it still seemed to me a little abrupt, and I told Dunya afterwards. But Dunya even answered me with vexation that ‘words are not deeds’, and that, of course, is true. Before she decided, Dunechka did not sleep all night, and, thinking I was already asleep, she got out of bed and walked up and down the room all night; at last she knelt down and prayed long and fervently before the icon, and in the morning she told me that she had made up her mind.

“I have already mentioned that Pyotr Petrovich is now going to Petersburg. He has big affairs there, and he wants to open a public law office in Petersburg. He has long been occupied with pursuing various claims and lawsuits, and only the other day he won an important lawsuit. And he must go to Petersburg also because he has an important case there before the Senate. So, dear Rodya, he may be very useful to you too, even in everything, and Dunya and I have already decided that you might, from this very day, begin your future career definitely and consider your fate as clearly settled. Oh, if only this came true! It would be such an advantage that it must be regarded as nothing less than a direct mercy of the Almighty to us. Dunya dreams of nothing else. We have already ventured to say a few words on this subject to Pyotr Petrovich. He expressed himself cautiously and said that, of course, since he cannot do without a secretary, it is naturally better to pay a salary to a relation than to a stranger, if only he proves capable of the post (as if you would not prove capable!), but at the same time he expressed a doubt that your university studies would leave you time for work in his office. That was as far as it went this time, but Dunya thinks of nothing but this now. For several days now she has been simply in a kind of fever, and she has already drawn up a whole plan for how, later on, you can be Pyotr Petrovich’s associate and even partner in his legal business, all the more since you are in the law faculty yourself. I, Rodya, entirely agree with her and share all her plans and hopes, seeing every likelihood in them; and, in spite of Pyotr Petrovich’s present reserve, which is very understandable (because he does not know you yet), Dunya is firmly convinced that she will achieve everything by her good influence on her future husband, and of this she is sure. Of course we have taken care not to let slip a word to Pyotr Petrovich about any of these further dreams of ours, and above all that you will be his partner. He is a practical man and might take it very coldly, since all this would seem to him nothing but dreams. In the same way, neither Dunya nor I have said half a word to him yet about our strong hope that he will help us to help you with money while you are at the university; we have not said it because, first, it will come about of itself later, and he will surely offer it himself without unnecessary words (as if he would refuse Dunechka that), all the sooner since you yourself can become his right hand in the office and receive this help not as a charity but as a salary you have earned. That is how Dunechka wants to arrange it, and I entirely agree with her. And secondly, we have not said it because I particularly wanted to put you on an equal footing with him at our coming meeting. When Dunya spoke to him about you with enthusiasm, he answered that one must first look at any man oneself and more closely before judging him, and that he reserves the right to form his own opinion of you when he has made your acquaintance. Do you know, my precious Rodya, it seems to me, for certain reasons (not in the least to do with Pyotr Petrovich, though, but just for certain of my own personal, perhaps even old-womanish, womanish whims), it seems to me that I shall perhaps do better to live on my own after their marriage, as I live now, and not with them. I am quite sure that he will be so noble and delicate that he will invite me himself and offer that I should not be parted from my daughter any more, and if he has not said so yet, it is of course because it is understood without words; but I shall refuse. I have noticed more than once in life that mothers-in-law are not much to husbands’ liking, and I not only do not want to be the slightest burden to anyone, but I want to be completely free myself, as long as I have some bit of my own and such children as you and Dunechka. If possible I shall settle near you both, because, Rodya, I have saved the nicest thing for the end of the letter: know, my dear friend, that perhaps very soon we shall all come together again and embrace, all three of us, after a separation of almost three years! It is already settled for certain that Dunya and I are setting off for Petersburg; exactly when, I do not know, but in any case very, very soon, perhaps even in a week. Everything depends on the arrangements of Pyotr Petrovich, who will let us know as soon as he has looked about him in Petersburg. For certain reasons of his own he wants to hasten the marriage ceremony as much as possible, and even, if possible, to have the wedding during the present meat-eating season, and if that cannot be managed, because the time is so short, then immediately after the Assumption fast. Oh, with what happiness I shall press you to my heart! Dunya is all excited with joy at seeing you, and she said once, as a joke, that she would have married Pyotr Petrovich for that alone. She is an angel! She is not adding anything to this letter now, but only told me to write that she has so much, so much to talk to you about, that she cannot bring herself to take up her pen now, because one can write nothing in a few lines and only upset oneself; but she told me to give you a tight hug and send you countless kisses. But although we may perhaps meet in person very soon, I shall still send you some money in a day or two, as much as I can. Now that everyone knows that Dunechka is marrying Pyotr Petrovich, my credit has suddenly grown too, and I know for certain that Afanasy Ivanovich will now trust me, against my pension, even up to seventy-five roubles, so that I may perhaps send you twenty-five roubles or even thirty. I would send more, but I am afraid for our travelling expenses; and although Pyotr Petrovich has been so kind as to take on part of the expenses of our journey to the capital, namely, he has offered of his own accord, at his own expense, to send our luggage and the big trunk (somehow through acquaintances of his), still we have to reckon on our arrival in Petersburg, where one cannot appear without a penny, at least for the first days. Dunechka and I, however, have already worked it all out exactly, and it turned out that the journey will not cost much. It is only ninety versts from us to the railway, and we have already, just in case, arranged with a peasant driver we know; and from there Dunechka and I will ride very comfortably third class. So that perhaps I shall manage to send you not twenty-five but surely thirty roubles. But enough; I have filled two sheets all round, and there is no more room; our whole story; well, and so many things have happened! And now, my precious Rodya, I embrace you until our meeting, which is near, and give you my mother’s blessing. Love Dunya, your sister, Rodya; love her as she loves you, and know that she loves you boundlessly, more than herself. She is an angel, and you, Rodya, you are everything to us, all our hope and all our trust. If only you are happy, we shall be happy. Do you pray to God, Rodya, as you used to, and do you believe in the goodness of our Creator and Redeemer? I am afraid in my heart that the newest fashionable unbelief may have visited you too. If so, I pray for you. Remember, my dear, how in your childhood, when your father was alive, you used to lisp your prayers on my knees, and how happy we all were then! Goodbye, or, better, until we meet! I embrace you tight, tight, and kiss you countless times.

“Yours till death,

“Pulkheria Raskolnikova.”

Almost the whole time Raskolnikov was reading, from the very beginning of the letter, his face was wet with tears; but when he had finished, it was pale, twisted with a spasm, and a heavy, bilious, malicious smile wound over his lips. He laid his head on his thin, worn pillow and thought, thought for a long time. His heart was beating hard, and his thoughts were in great turmoil. At last he felt stifled and cramped in this yellow little room, like a cupboard or a trunk. His eyes and his mind asked for space. He seized his hat and went out, this time no longer afraid of meeting anyone on the stairs; he had forgotten about it. He set off towards Vasilyevsky Island along V. Prospect, as if hurrying there on business; but, as was his habit, he walked without noticing where he was going, whispering to himself and even talking aloud to himself, which greatly surprised the passers-by. Many took him for a drunk.
