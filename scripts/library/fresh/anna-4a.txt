## Part One · Chapter XIX

When Anna came into the room, Dolly was sitting in the little drawing-room with a plump, white-headed little boy, who already looked like his father, and was hearing his lesson in French reading. The boy was reading, twisting in his hand a button of his jacket that was barely holding on and trying to tear it off. Several times his mother took his hand away, but the plump little hand took hold of the button again. His mother tore off the button and put it in her pocket.

“Keep your hands still, Grisha,” she said, and took up her blanket again, a piece of work she had had on hand for a long time, which she always took up in hard moments; she was knitting it now nervously, flicking the yarn over with her finger and counting the stitches. Although she had sent word to her husband the day before that it was nothing to her whether his sister came or not, she had got everything ready for her arrival and was waiting for her sister-in-law with agitation.

Dolly was crushed by her grief, entirely swallowed up in it. Still, she remembered that Anna, her sister-in-law, was the wife of one of the most important persons in Petersburg, and a Petersburg grande dame. And thanks to this circumstance she had not done what she had told her husband, that is, she had not forgotten that her sister-in-law was coming. “And, after all, Anna is not to blame for anything,” thought Dolly. “I know nothing of her but what is very good, and towards me I have seen nothing from her but kindness and friendship.” True, as far as she could remember her impression of the Karenins’ house in Petersburg, she had not liked the house itself; there was something false in the whole pattern of their family life. “But why should I not receive her? If only she doesn’t take it into her head to console me!” thought Dolly. “All the consolations and exhortations and Christian forgiveness, I have thought it all over a thousand times already, and none of it is any use.”

All these days Dolly had been alone with the children. She did not want to talk about her grief, and with that grief in her soul she could not talk about anything else. She knew that one way or another she would pour everything out to Anna; and now she was glad at the thought of how she would pour it out, now angry at the necessity of talking about her humiliation with her, his sister, and of hearing from her ready-made phrases of exhortation and consolation.

As often happens, she had been watching the clock and expecting her every minute, and missed exactly the minute when the visitor arrived, so that she did not hear the bell.

Hearing the rustle of a dress and light steps already at the door, she looked round, and her tormented face expressed in spite of herself not joy but surprise. She got up and embraced her sister-in-law.

“What, here already?” she said, kissing her.

“Dolly, how glad I am to see you!”

“And I am glad,” said Dolly, smiling faintly and trying to tell from the expression of Anna’s face whether she knew. “She must know,” she thought, noticing the sympathy in Anna’s face. “Well, come, I’ll take you to your room,” she went on, trying to put off as long as possible the moment of explanation.

“Is this Grisha? My goodness, how he has grown!” said Anna, and, kissing him, without taking her eyes off Dolly, she stopped and blushed. “No, let us not go anywhere.”

She took off her scarf and her hat, and, catching it in a lock of her black hair, which curled everywhere, she shook her head to free it.

“And you are radiant with happiness and health!” said Dolly, almost with envy.

“I?… Yes,” said Anna. “Goodness, Tanya! The same age as my Seryozha,” she added, turning to the little girl, who had run in. She took her in her arms and kissed her. “A lovely little girl, lovely! Show me all of them.”

She named them, and remembered not only the names but the years, the months, the characters and the illnesses of all the children, and Dolly could not help appreciating it.

“Well then, let us go to them,” she said. “It’s a pity Vasya is asleep now.”

When they had seen the children, they sat down, alone now, in the drawing-room over coffee. Anna took hold of the tray and then pushed it away.

“Dolly,” she said, “he has told me.”

Dolly looked coldly at Anna. She was waiting now for phrases of pretended sympathy; but Anna said nothing of the kind.

“Dolly, darling!” she said. “I don’t want to speak to you for him, or to console you; that’s impossible. But, my dear, I am simply sorry, sorry for you with all my heart!”

From under the thick lashes of her shining eyes tears suddenly appeared. She moved closer to her sister-in-law and took her hand in her own energetic little hand. Dolly did not draw away, but her face did not change its dry expression. She said:

“There is no consoling me. Everything is lost after what has happened, everything is gone!”

And as soon as she had said this, the expression of her face suddenly softened. Anna lifted Dolly’s dry, thin hand, kissed it and said:

“But, Dolly, what is to be done, what is to be done? How is it best to act in this terrible situation? That is what we must think about.”

“Everything is over, and that’s all,” said Dolly. “And the worst of it, you understand, is that I can’t leave him; there are the children, I am tied. And I can’t live with him; it is torture to me to see him.”

“Dolly, darling, he has told me, but I want to hear it from you; tell me everything.”

Dolly looked at her questioningly. Unfeigned sympathy and love could be seen in Anna’s face.

“Very well,” she said suddenly. “But I shall tell it from the beginning. You know how I married. With maman’s upbringing I was not only innocent, I was stupid. I knew nothing. They say, I know, that husbands tell their wives about their former lives, but Stiva…” She corrected herself. “Stepan Arkadyich told me nothing. You won’t believe it, but until now I thought I was the only woman he had known. That is how I lived for eight years. You must understand that not only did I not suspect unfaithfulness, I thought it impossible; and then, imagine, with such ideas, suddenly to learn all the horror, all the vileness… You must understand me. To be completely sure of one’s happiness, and suddenly…” Dolly went on, holding back her sobs, “and to receive a letter… his letter to his mistress, to my governess. No, it is too horrible!” She hurriedly took out her handkerchief and covered her face with it. “I could understand being carried away,” she went on, after a silence, “but to deceive me deliberately, cunningly… and with whom?… To go on being my husband together with her… it is horrible! You can’t understand…”

“Oh no, I do understand! I understand, dear Dolly, I understand,” said Anna, pressing her hand.

“And do you think he understands all the horror of my position?” Dolly went on. “Not in the least! He is happy and contented.”

“Oh, no!” Anna interrupted quickly. “He is pitiful, he is crushed with remorse…”

“Is he capable of remorse?” Dolly interrupted, peering attentively into her sister-in-law’s face.

“Yes, I know him. I could not look at him without pity. We both know him. He is kind, but he is proud, and now he is so humiliated. What touched me most” (and here Anna guessed the main thing that could touch Dolly) “is that two things torment him: that he is ashamed before the children, and that he, loving you… yes, yes, loving you more than anything in the world,” she hurriedly interrupted Dolly, who wanted to object, “has hurt you, has struck you down. ‘No, no, she will not forgive,’ he keeps saying.”

Dolly looked thoughtfully past her sister-in-law as she listened to her words.

“Yes, I understand that his position is terrible; it is worse for the guilty one than for the innocent,” she said, “if he feels that all the unhappiness comes from his guilt. But how can I forgive, how can I be his wife again after her? To live with him now will be torture to me, precisely because I loved him as I did, because I love my past love for him…”

And sobs cut off her words.

But, as though on purpose, every time she softened she began talking again of what exasperated her.

“She is young, you know, she is pretty,” she went on. “Do you understand, Anna, who has taken my youth and my beauty? He and his children. I have served my time with him, and everything that was mine has gone in that service, and now of course a fresh, vulgar creature is more pleasing to him. They must have talked about me together, or, worse still, kept silent about me, do you understand?” Again her eyes kindled with hatred. “And after that he will tell me… What, shall I believe him? Never. No, everything is finished, everything that was a comfort, the reward of work, of suffering… Would you believe it? I was teaching Grisha just now: it used to be a joy, and now it is torture. Why do I try, why do I toil? What are the children for? The terrible thing is that suddenly my soul has turned over, and instead of love and tenderness I have nothing but anger for him, yes, anger. I could kill him and…”

“Darling, Dolly, I understand, but don’t torment yourself. You are so hurt, so worked up, that you see many things wrongly.”

Dolly grew quiet, and for two minutes or so they were silent.

“What is to be done? Think, Anna, help me. I have thought everything over and I see nothing.”

Anna could think of nothing, but her heart responded directly to every word, to every expression of her sister-in-law’s face.

“I will say one thing,” Anna began. “I am his sister, I know his character, this capacity of forgetting everything, everything” (she made a gesture in front of her forehead), “this capacity of being completely carried away, but then also of complete repentance. He doesn’t believe, he doesn’t understand now, how he could have done what he did.”

“No, he understands, he understood!” Dolly interrupted. “But I… you forget me… is it any easier for me?”

“Wait! When he talked to me, I confess I did not yet understand all the horror of your position. I saw only him, and that the family was in disorder; I was sorry for him; but now that I have talked with you, I, as a woman, see something else; I see your suffering, and I can’t tell you how sorry I am for you! But, Dolly darling, I understand your suffering completely; only one thing I don’t know: I don’t know… I don’t know how much love there still is for him in your soul. That you know: whether there is enough to make forgiveness possible. If there is, then forgive!”

“No,” Dolly began; but Anna interrupted her, kissing her hand once more.

“I know the world better than you,” she said. “I know these men, like Stiva, how they look at these things. You say that he talked to her about you. That never happened. These men are unfaithful, but their own hearth and their wife are sacred to them. Somehow with them these women remain in contempt and don’t interfere with the family. They draw some line that can’t be crossed between the family and that. I don’t understand it, but that is how it is.”

“Yes, but he kissed her…”

“Dolly, wait, darling. I saw Stiva when he was in love with you. I remember that time, when he came to me and cried as he talked about you, and what poetry and what a height you were for him; and I know that the longer he lived with you, the higher you became for him. Why, we used to laugh at him because he added to every word: ‘Dolly is a wonderful woman.’ You always were and still are a divinity for him, and this infatuation was not of his soul…”

“But what if this infatuation happens again?”

“It can’t, as I understand it…”

“Yes, but would you forgive?”

“I don’t know. I can’t judge… Yes, I can,” said Anna, after thinking; and, grasping the situation in her mind and weighing it on her inner scales, she added: “Yes, I can, I can, I can. Yes, I would forgive. I would not be the same, no; but I would forgive, and forgive as though it had not happened, had not happened at all.”

“Well, of course,” Dolly broke in quickly, as though saying what she had thought more than once, “otherwise it would not be forgiveness. If one forgives, then completely, completely. Well, come, I’ll take you to your room,” she said, getting up; and on the way Dolly embraced Anna. “My dear, how glad I am you have come, how glad. I feel better, much better.”

## Chapter XX

All that day Anna spent at home, that is, at the Oblonskys’, and received no one, though some of her acquaintances, having already heard of her arrival, called that same day. Anna spent the whole morning with Dolly and the children. She only sent a little note to her brother, telling him he must dine at home. “Come; God is merciful,” she wrote.

Oblonsky dined at home; the conversation was general, and his wife talked to him and called him “thou”, which she had not done before. In the relations of husband and wife the same estrangement remained, but there was no longer any talk of separation, and Stepan Arkadyich saw the possibility of an explanation and a reconciliation.

Immediately after dinner Kitty came. She knew Anna Arkadyevna, but very little, and she was coming now to her sister’s not without some fear of how she would be received by this Petersburg society lady whom everyone praised so. But Anna Arkadyevna liked her; she saw that at once. Anna was evidently admiring her beauty and youth, and before Kitty knew where she was, she felt herself not only under her influence but in love with her, as young girls are capable of falling in love with married and older women. Anna was not like a society lady or the mother of a son of eight; she would rather have seemed a girl of twenty, by the suppleness of her movements, her freshness, and the animation settled in her face that broke out now in a smile, now in a glance, if it had not been for the serious, sometimes sad expression of her eyes, which struck Kitty and drew her. Kitty felt that Anna was perfectly simple and hid nothing, but that there was in her some other, higher world of interests, complex and poetic, out of Kitty’s reach.

After dinner, when Dolly had gone to her room, Anna got up quickly and went up to her brother, who was lighting a cigar.

“Stiva,” she said to him, winking gaily, making the sign of the cross over him and pointing to the door with her eyes. “Go, and God help you.”

He threw down the cigar, understanding her, and disappeared through the door.

When Stepan Arkadyich had gone, she went back to the sofa, where she had been sitting surrounded by the children. Whether because the children saw that mama loved this aunt, or because they themselves felt a special charm in her, the elder two, and the younger ones after them, as often happens with children, had attached themselves to the new aunt even before dinner and would not leave her. And something like a game had come about among them, which consisted in sitting as close to their aunt as possible, touching her, holding her little hand, kissing it, playing with her ring, or at least touching the flounce of her dress.

“Come, come, let us sit the way we were sitting before,” said Anna Arkadyevna, sitting down in her place.

And again Grisha pushed his head under her arm and leaned his head against her dress, and beamed with pride and happiness.

“So, when is the ball now?” she said, turning to Kitty.

“Next week, and a splendid ball. One of those balls where it is always gay.”

“And are there balls where it is always gay?” said Anna with tender mockery.

“It’s strange, but there are. At the Bobrishchevs’ it is always gay, and at the Nikitins’ too, but at the Mezhkovs’ it is always dull. Haven’t you noticed?”

“No, my dear, for me there are no longer any balls where it is gay,” said Anna, and Kitty saw in her eyes that special world which was not open to her. “For me there are some where it is less hard and dull…”

“How can you be bored at a ball?”

“Why can’t I be bored at a ball?” asked Anna.

Kitty noticed that Anna knew what answer would follow.

“Because you are always the best of all.”

Anna had the ability to blush. She blushed and said:

“In the first place, never; and in the second, even if it were so, what would it matter to me?”

“Will you go to this ball?” asked Kitty.

“I think it will be impossible not to. Here, take this,” she said to Tanya, who was pulling the ring, which came off easily, from her white finger, slender at the tip.

“I shall be very glad if you go. I should so like to see you at the ball.”

“At least, if I have to go, I shall comfort myself with the thought that it will give you pleasure… Grisha, don’t pull, please, it’s all coming down as it is,” she said, putting back a loose lock of hair that Grisha was playing with.

“I imagine you at the ball in lilac.”

“Why lilac, of all things?” asked Anna, smiling. “Well, children, run along, run along. Do you hear? Miss Hull is calling you to tea,” she said, tearing the children away from her and sending them to the dining-room.

“And I know why you want me at the ball. You expect a great deal from this ball, and you want everyone to be there, everyone to take part.”

“How do you know? Yes.”

“Oh, what a good time yours is,” Anna went on. “I remember and know that blue mist, like the mist on the mountains in Switzerland. That mist that covers everything in that blissful time when childhood is just about to end, and out of that vast circle, happy and gay, a path is made, narrower and narrower, and it is gay and frightening to go into that long suite of rooms, though it is bright and beautiful… Who has not been through that?”

Kitty smiled in silence. “But how did she go through it? How I should like to know her whole romance,” thought Kitty, remembering the unpoetic appearance of Alexei Alexandrovich, her husband.

“I know something. Stiva told me, and I congratulate you; I like him very much,” Anna went on. “I met Vronsky at the railway station.”

“Ah, was he there?” asked Kitty, blushing. “What did Stiva tell you?”

“Stiva blabbed everything to me. And I should be very glad. I travelled yesterday with Vronsky’s mother,” she went on, “and his mother talked to me about him without stopping; he is her favourite; I know how partial mothers are, but…”

“What did his mother tell you?”

“Oh, a great deal! And I know he is her favourite, but still one can see he is a knight… Well, for instance, she told me that he wanted to give his whole fortune to his brother, that when he was still a child he did something extraordinary, saved a woman from drowning. In a word, a hero,” said Anna, smiling and remembering the two hundred roubles he had given at the station.

But she did not tell about the two hundred roubles. For some reason it was unpleasant for her to remember it. She felt that there was something in it that concerned her, something that ought not to have been.

“She begged me very much to go and see her,” Anna went on, “and I shall be glad to see the old lady, and I shall go to her tomorrow. But, thank God, Stiva is staying a long time in Dolly’s room,” Anna added, changing the subject and getting up, displeased about something, as it seemed to Kitty.

“No, me first! No, me!” shouted the children, who had finished their tea, running out to Aunt Anna.

“All together!” said Anna, and, laughing, she ran to meet them, put her arms round them and tumbled over the whole heap of wriggling children, squealing with delight.

## Chapter XXI

Dolly came out of her room for the grown-ups’ tea. Stepan Arkadyich did not come out. He must have left his wife’s room by the back way.

“I am afraid you will be cold upstairs,” Dolly remarked, turning to Anna. “I want to move you downstairs, and we shall be nearer.”

“Oh, please don’t worry about me,” answered Anna, peering into Dolly’s face and trying to make out whether there had been a reconciliation or not.

“It will be lighter for you here,” answered her sister-in-law.

“I tell you, I sleep anywhere and always like a marmot.”

“What is all this?” said Stepan Arkadyich, coming out of the study and turning to his wife.

From his tone both Kitty and Anna understood at once that the reconciliation had taken place.

“I want to move Anna downstairs, but the curtains must be hung differently. No one will manage it; I must do it myself,” answered Dolly, turning to him.

“God knows whether they have been fully reconciled,” thought Anna, hearing her tone, cold and calm.

“Oh, come now, Dolly, you are always making difficulties,” said her husband. “Well, if you like, I’ll do it all…”

“Yes, they must have been reconciled,” thought Anna.

“I know how you will do it all,” answered Dolly. “You’ll tell Matvei to do what can’t be done, and go off yourself, and he will get it all muddled.” And the habitual mocking smile wrinkled the corners of Dolly’s lips as she said it.

“A full, full reconciliation, full,” thought Anna. “Thank God!” And, glad that she had been the cause of it, she went up to Dolly and kissed her.

“Not at all; why do you despise Matvei and me so?” said Stepan Arkadyich, smiling just perceptibly and turning to his wife.

All the evening Dolly, as always, was a little mocking towards her husband, and Stepan Arkadyich was contented and cheerful, but only so far as not to show that, being forgiven, he had forgotten his guilt.

At half past nine the especially joyful and pleasant evening family talk round the tea-table at the Oblonskys’ was broken by an event that was, to all appearances, the simplest, yet this simple event for some reason seemed strange to everyone. While they were talking about common acquaintances in Petersburg, Anna got up quickly.

“I have her in my album,” she said; “and by the way, I’ll show you my Seryozha,” she added with a proud mother’s smile.

Towards ten o’clock, when she usually said good night to her son, and often put him to bed herself before going to a ball, she felt sad at being so far from him; and whatever they talked about, she kept returning in her thoughts to her curly-headed Seryozha. She wanted to look at his photograph and talk about him. Seizing the first pretext, she got up and went for the album with her light, decided step. The stairs up to her room came out onto the landing of the big, warm front staircase.

Just as she was leaving the drawing-room, the bell rang in the hall.

“Who can that be?” said Dolly.

“It’s early to fetch me, and late for anyone else,” remarked Kitty.

“Papers, I expect,” added Stepan Arkadyich; and when Anna was passing the head of the staircase, a servant was running up to announce the visitor, and the visitor himself was standing by the lamp. Anna, glancing down, at once recognized Vronsky, and a strange feeling of pleasure and at the same time of fear of something suddenly stirred in her heart. He was standing without taking off his overcoat and getting something out of his pocket. At the moment when she reached the middle of the staircase he raised his eyes and saw her, and something ashamed and frightened came into the expression of his face. Bending her head slightly, she passed on, and after her could be heard Stepan Arkadyich’s loud voice calling him to come in, and the quiet, soft, calm voice of Vronsky refusing.

When Anna came back with the album he was no longer there, and Stepan Arkadyich was telling them that he had called to find out about the dinner they were giving the next day for a visiting celebrity.

“He wouldn’t come in for anything. He’s a strange fellow,” Stepan Arkadyich added.

Kitty blushed. She thought that she alone understood why he had come and why he had not come in. “He has been to our house,” she thought, “and didn’t find me, and thought I was here; but he didn’t come in because he thought it was late, and Anna is here.”

They all looked at one another without saying anything, and began looking at Anna’s album.

There was nothing either unusual or strange in a man calling on a friend at half past nine to find out the details of a dinner being planned, and not coming in; but it seemed strange to everyone. More than to anyone, it seemed strange and not right to Anna.
