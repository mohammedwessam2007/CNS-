## The Handbook (Encheiridion) of Epictetus

## 1

Some things are up to us, and some are not up to us. Up to us are our judgements, our impulses, our desires, our aversions; in a word, everything that is our own doing. Not up to us are our body, our property, our reputation, our positions of power; in a word, everything that is not our own doing.

The things that are up to us are by nature free, unhindered and unobstructed; the things that are not up to us are weak, slavish, hindered, and belong to others.

Remember, then: if you take what is by nature slavish to be free, and what belongs to others to be your own, you will be hindered, you will grieve, you will be troubled, and you will blame both gods and men. But if you take only what is yours to be yours, and what belongs to others, as it really does, to belong to others, no one will ever compel you, no one will hinder you; you will blame no one, accuse no one, do nothing against your will; no one will harm you; you will have no enemy; for you will suffer no harm at all.

So, aiming at such great things, remember that you must not go after them with only moderate effort: some things you must give up altogether, and others you must put off for the present. But if you want these things and also want power and wealth, you may perhaps not even get those, because you are also aiming at the first; and you will certainly fail to get the first, which alone bring freedom and happiness.

So, to every harsh impression, practise saying at once: “You are an impression, and not at all what you appear to be.” Then examine it and test it by the rules you have, and by this first and above all: does it concern the things that are up to us, or the things that are not up to us? And if it concerns something that is not up to us, have this answer ready: “It is nothing to me.”

## 2

Remember that desire promises that you will get what you desire, and aversion promises that you will not fall into what you avoid; and the person who fails to get what he desires is unfortunate, while the person who falls into what he avoids is miserable. So if you avoid only those things contrary to nature among the things that are up to you, you will never fall into anything you avoid; but if you try to avoid illness, or death, or poverty, you will be miserable.

So take away your aversion from everything that is not up to us, and transfer it to the things contrary to nature among those that are up to us. And for the present, do away with desire altogether: for if you desire something that is not up to us, you are bound to be unfortunate; and of the things that are up to us, which it would be good to desire, none is yet within your reach. Use only impulse towards things and away from them, but lightly, with reservation, and without strain.

## 3

With each of the things that delight you, or are useful to you, or that you love, remember to say to yourself what sort of thing it is, starting with the smallest things. If you love a jug, say: “I love a jug.” Then, if it breaks, you will not be troubled. If you kiss your child or your wife, say to yourself that you are kissing a human being; then, if they die, you will not be troubled.

## 4

When you are about to start on some activity, remind yourself what sort of activity it is. If you are going out to bathe, picture to yourself what happens at the baths: people splashing, people jostling, people insulting each other, people stealing. And so you will start on the activity more securely if you say to yourself at once: “I want to bathe, and also to keep my will in harmony with nature.” And so with every activity. For then, if anything gets in the way of your bathing, you will have this ready: “But this was not all I wanted; I also wanted to keep my will in harmony with nature; and I will not keep it so if I get angry at what happens.”

## 5

What troubles people is not things, but their judgements about things. Death, for example, is nothing terrible (or Socrates too would have thought so); the terrible thing is the judgement about death, that it is terrible. So when we are hindered, or troubled, or grieved, let us never blame anyone else, but ourselves, that is, our own judgements. To blame others for one’s own misfortunes is the mark of an uneducated person; to blame oneself is the mark of one whose education has begun; to blame neither another nor oneself is the mark of one whose education is complete.

## 6

Do not be elated at any excellence that is not your own. If a horse were elated and said, “I am beautiful,” it would be bearable; but when you say, elated, “I have a beautiful horse,” know that you are elated at something good that belongs to the horse. What, then, is yours? The use you make of impressions. So when you are in harmony with nature in the use of your impressions, then be elated: for then you will be elated at something good that is your own.

## 7

On a voyage, when the ship has anchored, if you go ashore to fetch water, you may pick up a little shellfish or a little bulb along the way; but your mind must be fixed on the ship, and you must keep turning round in case the captain calls; and if he calls, you must leave all those things, so as not to be thrown on board bound like the sheep. So too in life: if instead of a little bulb or a little shellfish you are given a wife and a child, nothing prevents it; but if the captain calls, run to the ship, leaving all those things, without even looking back. And if you are old, do not go far from the ship at all, in case you fail to answer when he calls.

## 8

Do not seek for things to happen as you wish, but wish them to happen as they do happen, and your life will flow smoothly.

## 9

Illness is an obstacle to the body, but not to the will, unless the will itself chooses. Lameness is an obstacle to the leg, but not to the will. And say this to yourself at everything that befalls you: you will find it an obstacle to something else, but not to yourself.

## 10

At everything that befalls you, remember to turn to yourself and ask what power you have for dealing with it. If you see a beautiful man or woman, you will find the power to deal with it: self-control. If hard work is set before you, you will find endurance. If abuse, you will find patience. And if you train yourself in this way, your impressions will not carry you away.

## 11

Never say of anything, “I have lost it,” but “I have given it back.” Has your child died? It has been given back. Has your wife died? She has been given back. “My land has been taken from me.” Then that too has been given back. “But the man who took it is wicked.” What does it matter to you through whom the giver asked for it back? As long as he gives it to you, take care of it as something that is not yours, as travellers treat an inn.

## 12

If you want to make progress, give up reasoning like this: “If I neglect my affairs, I shall have nothing to live on.” “If I do not punish my slave, he will turn out bad.” For it is better to die of hunger, free from grief and fear, than to live in plenty but troubled. And it is better for the slave to be bad than for you to be unhappy.

So begin with little things. A little oil is spilt, a little wine is stolen: say to yourself, “This is the price of calm, this is the price of peace of mind; nothing comes for free.” And when you call your slave, keep in mind that he may not hear, and if he hears, may not do anything you want; but he is not in so good a position that your peace of mind should depend on him.

## 13

If you want to make progress, be content to seem foolish and stupid in external matters; do not wish to seem to know anything; and if some people think you are someone, distrust yourself. For know that it is not easy to keep your will in harmony with nature and also to keep external things; whoever attends to the one must necessarily neglect the other.

## 14

If you want your children and your wife and your friends to live for ever, you are a fool: for you want things that are not up to you to be up to you, and things that belong to others to be yours. In the same way, if you want your slave never to do wrong, you are a fool: for you want vice not to be vice, but something else. But if you want never to fail to get what you desire, that you can do. So practise what you can do.

Each person’s master is whoever has power over what that person wants or does not want, to give it or take it away. So whoever wants to be free, let him neither want nor avoid anything that depends on others; otherwise he is bound to be a slave.

## 15

Remember that you must behave in life as at a banquet. Something is being passed round and comes to you: stretch out your hand and take a modest share. It passes by: do not hold it back. It has not come yet: do not stretch your desire out towards it, but wait until it reaches you. So with children, so with a wife, so with positions of power, so with wealth; and one day you will be worthy to dine with the gods. And if you do not take even what is set before you, but look past it, then you will not only share the gods’ banquet, but share their rule as well. It was by doing this that Diogenes and Heraclitus and those like them were rightly divine, and rightly called so.

## 16

When you see someone weeping in grief, because a child has gone away or because he has lost his property, take care that the impression does not carry you away into thinking that he is in trouble because of external things; but have this ready at once: “What distresses this man is not what has happened (for it does not distress another), but his judgement about it.” As far as words go, however, do not hesitate to sympathise with him, and even, if it so happens, to groan with him; but take care not to groan inwardly as well.

## 17

Remember that you are an actor in a play, of whatever kind the playwright chooses: if short, a short one; if long, a long one. If he wants you to play a beggar, play even that part well; or a cripple, or a ruler, or a private citizen. For this is your business: to play well the part you are given; but to choose it belongs to another.

## 18

When a raven croaks inauspiciously, do not let the impression carry you away; but at once draw a distinction within yourself and say: “None of this is a sign for me, but either for my poor body, or my bit of property, or my bit of reputation, or my children, or my wife. For me every sign is favourable, if I so wish; for whatever comes of these things, it is up to me to benefit from it.”

## 19

You can be invincible, if you never enter any contest in which victory is not up to you.

Take care that, when you see someone honoured above others, or very powerful, or otherwise highly regarded, you are not carried away by the impression and call him happy. For if the essence of the good lies in the things that are up to us, there is no room for envy or jealousy; and you yourself will not want to be a general, or a magistrate, or a consul, but free. And there is only one road to that: contempt for the things that are not up to us.

## 20

Remember that it is not the man who abuses you or strikes you who insults you, but your judgement that these things are insulting. So when someone provokes you, know that it is your own opinion that has provoked you. Therefore try above all not to be carried away by the impression; for once you gain time and delay, you will more easily master yourself.

## 21

Let death and exile and all the things that seem terrible be before your eyes every day, and death most of all; and you will never think any mean thought, or desire anything too much.

## 22

If you desire philosophy, prepare yourself from the start to be laughed at, to be mocked by many, who will say, “Suddenly he has come back to us a philosopher!” and “Where did he get that superior look?” But do not put on a superior look; hold fast to what seems best to you, as if you had been posted to this place by God. And remember that if you stand firm in it, the very people who laughed at you at first will afterwards admire you; but if you give way to them, you will be laughed at twice over.
