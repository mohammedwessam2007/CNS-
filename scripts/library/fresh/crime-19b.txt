## CHAPTER V

Raskolnikov followed him.

“What’s this!” cried Svidrigailov, turning round. “I think I said...”

“It means that I won’t leave you alone now.”

“Wha-a-at?”

Both stopped, and for a minute both looked at each other, as if taking each other’s measure.

“From all your half-drunken stories,” Raskolnikov cut him short sharply, “I have concluded positively that not only have you not given up your vilest designs on my sister, but you are even more taken up with them than ever. I know that my sister received some letter this morning. You couldn’t sit still the whole time... Suppose you have dug up some wife on the way; that means nothing. I wish to make sure personally...”

Raskolnikov could hardly have defined himself what exactly he wanted now and what exactly he wished to make sure of personally.

“So that’s it! Would you like me to call the police right now?”

“Call them!”

Again they stood facing each other for a minute. At last Svidrigailov’s face changed. Having made sure that Raskolnikov was not frightened by the threat, he suddenly took on the merriest and friendliest air.

“What a fellow! I deliberately didn’t speak to you about your affair, though of course curiosity torments me. It is a fantastic affair. I had put it off till another time, but really, you could provoke a dead man... Well, come along, only I’ll tell you in advance: I am going home now only for a minute, to get some money; then I lock up the flat, take a cab and go to the Islands for the whole evening. Well, why should you come after me?”

“For the time being I am going to the house, and not to you, but to Sofya Semyonovna, to apologise for not being at the funeral.”

“As you please, but Sofya Semyonovna is not at home. She has taken all the children to a certain lady, a certain distinguished old lady, an old acquaintance of mine from long ago and a manager of some orphan institutions. I charmed this lady by paying her the money for all three of Katerina Ivanovna’s nestlings; besides, I donated more money to the institutions; finally, I told her Sofya Semyonovna’s story, with all the honours, hiding nothing. It produced an indescribable effect. That is why Sofya Semyonovna was told to come today, straight to the — Hotel, where my lady is staying for the time being, down from her summer place.”

“No matter, I’ll call all the same.”

“As you like, only I am no companion for you there; but what is it to me! Here we are at home now. Tell me, I am convinced you look at me suspiciously because I myself was delicate enough not to trouble you with questions until now... you understand? It seemed to you something extraordinary; I bet it did! Well, after that, be delicate.”

“And listen at doors!”

“Ah, you mean that!” laughed Svidrigailov. “Yes, I would have been surprised if, after everything, you had let it pass without a remark. Ha-ha! Though I did understand something of what you got up to that time... there... and told Sofya Semyonovna yourself, still, what is it all? Perhaps I am quite a backward man and can understand nothing any more. Explain, for God’s sake, my dear! Enlighten me with the newest principles.”

“You couldn’t have heard anything; you are lying about it all!”

“But I don’t mean that, I don’t mean that (though I did hear a thing or two, by the way); no, I mean that you keep groaning and groaning! The Schiller in you is troubled every minute. And now, it seems, one mustn’t listen at doors. If so, go and declare to the authorities that, well, it was like this and like this, such an accident happened to me: a small mistake came out in my theory. But if you are convinced that one mustn’t listen at doors, but that one may crack old women with whatever comes to hand, for one’s own pleasure, then go away somewhere quickly, to America! Run, young man! Perhaps there is still time. I speak sincerely. Have you no money? I will give you some for the journey.”

“I am not thinking of that at all,” Raskolnikov interrupted with disgust.

“I understand (don’t trouble yourself, though: if you like, don’t say much); I understand what questions are going round in you: moral ones, are they? questions of citizen and man? But you push them aside; what do you want with them now? He-he! Because you are still a citizen and a man? But if so, you shouldn’t have poked your nose in; no use taking on what isn’t your business. Well, shoot yourself; what, don’t you feel like it?”

“You seem to want to irritate me on purpose, just so that I’ll leave you alone now...”

“What a strange fellow, but we have arrived; welcome to the stairs. You see, here is the entrance to Sofya Semyonovna’s; look, there is no one! You don’t believe me? Ask Kapernaumov; she gives them her key. Here is madame de Kapernaumov herself, eh? What? (she is a little deaf) gone out? Where? Well, there, did you hear now? She isn’t in and won’t be until late in the evening, perhaps. Well, now come to my place. You wanted to come to me too, didn’t you? Well, here we are at my place. Madame Resslich isn’t at home. That woman is always busy, but she is a good woman, I assure you... perhaps she might be of use to you, if you were somewhat more sensible. Well, kindly look: I take this five-per-cent bond out of the bureau (see how many more I have!), and this one will go to the money-changer today. Well, did you see? I have no more time to lose. The bureau is locked, the flat is locked, and we are on the stairs again. Well, shall we take a cab? I am going to the Islands, you know. Would you care for a drive? Here, I take this carriage to Yelagin; what? You refuse? You couldn’t hold out? Let’s go for a drive, never mind. It seems rain is coming on; never mind, we’ll put the hood up...”

Svidrigailov was already sitting in the carriage. Raskolnikov decided that his suspicions were unjust, at least at this moment. Without answering a word, he turned and walked back in the direction of the Haymarket. If he had turned round even once on the way, he would have had time to see how Svidrigailov, having driven no more than a hundred paces, paid off the carriage and found himself on the pavement. But he could see nothing any more and had already gone round the corner. Deep disgust drew him away from Svidrigailov. “And I could expect anything, even for a moment, from that coarse villain, that lustful profligate and scoundrel!” he exclaimed involuntarily. It is true that Raskolnikov pronounced his judgement too hastily and lightly. There was something in Svidrigailov’s whole situation that at least gave him a certain originality, if not mystery. As for his sister in all this, Raskolnikov remained convinced, all the same, that Svidrigailov would not leave her in peace. But it was becoming too heavy and unbearable to think and think again about all this!

As was his habit, when he was left alone, after twenty paces he fell into deep thought. Going up onto the bridge, he stopped at the railing and began looking at the water. And meanwhile Avdotya Romanovna was standing over him.

He had met her at the entrance to the bridge, but had walked past without making her out. Dunechka had never yet met him like this in the street and was struck to the point of fright. She stopped and did not know whether to call out to him or not. Suddenly she noticed Svidrigailov hurrying towards her from the direction of the Haymarket.

But he seemed to be approaching mysteriously and carefully. He did not go up onto the bridge, but stopped to one side, on the pavement, trying with all his might not to let Raskolnikov see him. He had long since noticed Dunya and began making signs to her. It seemed to her that with his signs he was begging her not to call out to her brother and to leave him alone, and was calling her to him.

And Dunya did so. She quietly went round her brother and came up to Svidrigailov.

“Let us go quickly,” Svidrigailov whispered to her. “I don’t wish Rodion Romanych to know of our meeting. I warn you that I was sitting with him not far from here, in a tavern, where he sought me out himself, and I barely got rid of him. For some reason he knows of my letter to you and suspects something. Surely it wasn’t you who revealed it to him? And if not you, then who?”

“Now we have turned the corner,” Dunya interrupted, “now my brother won’t see us. I declare to you that I will not go any further with you. Tell me everything here; all this can be said in the street too.”

“In the first place, it cannot possibly be said in the street; in the second, you must hear Sofya Semyonovna too; in the third, I will show you certain documents... Well, and finally, if you don’t agree to come into my place, I refuse all explanations and leave at once. At the same time I will ask you not to forget that a very curious secret of your beloved brother’s is entirely in my hands.”

Dunya stopped in indecision and looked at Svidrigailov with a piercing look.

“What are you afraid of!” he remarked calmly. “The city is not the country. And even in the country you did more harm to me than I to you, and here...”

“Has Sofya Semyonovna been told?”

“No, I haven’t said a word to her, and I am not even quite sure whether she is at home now. But probably she is. She buried her relative today: it’s not a day for paying visits. For the time being I don’t want to tell anyone about this, and I even partly regret having told you. The slightest carelessness here amounts to informing. I live right here, in this house; here we are coming to it. This is our house’s caretaker; the caretaker knows me very well; see, he is bowing; he sees that I am walking with a lady, and of course he has had time to notice your face, and that will be of use to you if you are very afraid and suspect me. Forgive me for speaking so crudely. I myself rent a room from tenants. Sofya Semyonovna lives wall to wall with me, also renting from tenants. The whole floor is let to lodgers. Why should you be afraid, like a child? Or am I really so very terrible?”

Svidrigailov’s face twisted into a condescending smile; but he was in no mood for smiling now. His heart was pounding and his breath was catching in his chest. He was deliberately speaking more loudly, so as to hide his growing agitation; but Dunya did not manage to notice this special agitation; she was far too irritated by the remark that she was afraid of him like a child and that he was so terrible for her.

“Though I know that you are a man... without honour, I am not in the least afraid of you. Go ahead,” she said, apparently calmly, but her face was very pale.

Svidrigailov stopped at Sonya’s door.

“Allow me to find out whether she is in. No. Bad luck! But I know that she may come very soon. If she has gone out, it can only be to a certain lady, about her orphans. Their mother has died. I have got mixed up in this too and made arrangements. If Sofya Semyonovna doesn’t come back within ten minutes, I will send her to you myself, today if you like; well, and here is my room. Here are my two rooms. Behind the door lives my landlady, Madame Resslich. Now look here; I will show you my main documents: from my bedroom this door leads into two completely empty rooms, which are to let. Here they are... you need to look at this a little more attentively...”

Svidrigailov occupied two furnished, fairly spacious rooms. Dunechka looked round mistrustfully, but noticed nothing special either in the furnishings or in the arrangement of the rooms, though one might have noticed something, for instance, that Svidrigailov’s lodging was somehow placed between two almost uninhabited flats. The entrance to his rooms was not directly from the corridor, but through two of the landlady’s rooms, almost empty. And from the bedroom Svidrigailov, unlocking a door that was locked with a key, showed Dunechka another empty flat that was to let. Dunechka stopped on the threshold, not understanding why she was being invited to look, but Svidrigailov hastened to explain:

“Here, look here, into this second large room. Note this door; it is locked with a key. By the door stands a chair, the only chair in both rooms. I brought it from my lodging, so as to listen more comfortably. Right there behind the door stands Sofya Semyonovna’s table; she sat there and talked with Rodion Romanych. And I listened here, sitting on the chair, two evenings running, both times for two hours or so, and of course I could have learned something; what do you think?”

“You listened?”

“Yes, I listened; now let’s go to my room; there is nowhere even to sit here.”

He led Avdotya Romanovna back into his first room, which served him as a sitting room, and invited her to sit down on a chair. He sat down himself at the other end of the table, at least seven feet from her, but probably the same flame was already shining in his eyes that had once so frightened Dunechka. She shuddered and looked round mistrustfully once more. Her gesture was involuntary; she evidently did not want to show mistrust. But the isolated position of Svidrigailov’s lodging struck her at last. She wanted to ask whether at least his landlady was at home, but she did not ask... out of pride. Besides, there was another suffering in her heart, incomparably greater than fear for herself. She was in unbearable torment.

“Here is your letter,” she began, laying it on the table. “Is what you write possible? You hint at a crime supposedly committed by my brother. You hint too clearly; you dare not back out of it now. Know, then, that I had heard this stupid tale even before you, and I don’t believe a single word of it. It is a vile and ridiculous suspicion. I know the story, and how and why it was invented. You cannot have any proofs. You promised to prove it: speak, then! But know beforehand that I don’t believe you! I don’t believe you!..”

Dunechka said this in a rapid patter, hurrying, and for an instant colour rushed into her face.

“If you didn’t believe it, could it have happened that you would risk coming to me alone? Why did you come, then? Out of mere curiosity?”

“Don’t torment me; speak, speak!”

“There is no need to say that you are a brave girl. By God, I thought you would ask Mr Razumikhin to accompany you here. But he was neither with you nor anywhere around you; I did look; that was daring; so you wanted to spare Rodion Romanych. But then, everything in you is divine... As for your brother, what shall I tell you? You saw him yourself just now. What was he like?”

“Surely you don’t base it on that alone?”

“No, not on that, but on his own words. Here, two evenings running, he came to Sofya Semyonovna. I showed you where they sat. He made her his full confession. He is a murderer. He killed the old woman, an official’s widow, a moneylender, with whom he himself had pawned things; he also killed her sister, a dealer named Lizaveta, who came in by chance during the murder of her sister. He killed them both with an axe which he had brought with him. He killed them to rob them, and he did rob them; he took money and some things... He told all this himself, word for word, to Sofya Semyonovna, who alone knows the secret, but took no part in the murder either by word or deed; on the contrary, she was as horrified as you are now. Rest assured, she will not give him away.”

“It cannot be!” Dunechka muttered with pale, deadened lips; she was gasping. “It cannot be; there is no reason, not the slightest reason, no occasion... It is a lie! A lie!”

“He robbed them; that is the whole reason. He took money and things. It is true that, by his own admission, he made no use of either the money or the things, but carried them off somewhere under a stone, where they are lying now. But that is because he did not dare make use of them.”

“But is it likely that he could steal, rob? That he could even think of it?” cried Dunya, and jumped up from her chair. “Why, you know him, you have seen him? Could he be a thief?”

She was as if beseeching Svidrigailov; she had forgotten all her fear.

“Here, Avdotya Romanovna, there are thousands and millions of combinations and sortings. A thief steals, but then he knows in himself that he is a scoundrel; but I have heard of a certain noble man who broke open the mail; so who knows, perhaps he really thought he had done a decent thing! Of course I would not have believed it myself, any more than you, if it had been passed on to me from outside. But I believed my own ears. He explained all his reasons to Sofya Semyonovna as well; but at first she didn’t believe her ears either, but at last she believed her eyes, her own eyes. He told her personally himself, after all.”

“What... reasons!”

“It is a long story, Avdotya Romanovna. Here, how shall I put it to you, there is a theory of a kind, the same sort of thing by which I find, for example, that a single villainy is permissible if the main aim is good. A single evil and a hundred good deeds! It is also, of course, galling for a young man of merit and boundless vanity to know that if there were, for instance, only three thousand, his whole career, his whole future in his aim in life, would take shape differently, and yet there are not those three thousand. Add to this the irritation of hunger, of a cramped lodging, of rags, of a vivid awareness of the beauty of his social position, and at the same time the position of his sister and mother. Most of all, though, vanity, pride and vanity; but then, God knows, perhaps even with good inclinations... I don’t blame him, please don’t think so; and it’s none of my business. There was also a little theory of his own here, a so-so sort of theory, according to which people are divided, you see, into material and special people, that is, people for whom, because of their high position, the law is not written; on the contrary, they themselves compose laws for the rest of people, the material, the rubbish. Never mind, a so-so little theory; une théorie comme une autre, a theory like any other. Napoleon carried him away terribly; that is, properly speaking, what carried him away was that very many men of genius did not look at a single evil, but stepped over it without thinking. He seems to have imagined that he too was a man of genius; that is, he was sure of it for a certain time. He has suffered very much and suffers even now from the thought that he knew how to compose the theory, but is not able to step over without thinking, and so is not a man of genius. Well, and that is humiliating for a young man with vanity, especially in our age...”

“And the pangs of conscience? So you deny him any moral feeling? Is he really like that?”

“Ah, Avdotya Romanovna, everything has become muddled now; that is, it was never in any particular order anyway. Russian people in general are broad people, Avdotya Romanovna, broad as their land, and extremely inclined to the fantastic, to the disorderly; but it is a misfortune to be broad without special genius. And do you remember how much we talked together in this vein and on this same subject, sitting in the evenings on the terrace in the garden, every time after supper? You reproached me for this very breadth. Who knows, perhaps we were talking at the very time when he was lying here thinking over his own thing. In our educated society, you know, there are no especially sacred traditions, Avdotya Romanovna: unless someone puts something together for himself from books... or derives something from the chronicles. But those are mostly scholars and, you know, all fools in their way, so that it is not even proper for a man of the world. However, you know my opinions in general; I accuse absolutely no one. I myself am a man who keeps his hands clean, and I stick to that. But we have talked about this more than once. I even had the happiness of interesting you with my opinions... You are very pale, Avdotya Romanovna!”

“I know this theory of his. I read his article in a journal about people to whom everything is permitted... Razumikhin brought it to me...”

“Mr Razumikhin? Your brother’s article? In a journal? Is there such an article? I didn’t know. That must be curious! But where are you going, Avdotya Romanovna?”

“I want to see Sofya Semyonovna,” Dunechka said in a weak voice. “How does one get to her? Perhaps she has come back; I absolutely want to see her now. Let her...”

Avdotya Romanovna could not finish; her breath literally failed her.

“Sofya Semyonovna will not come back until night. That is what I suppose. She should have come very soon, but if not, then very late...”

“Ah, so you are lying! I see... you lied... you lied about everything!.. I don’t believe you! I don’t believe you! I don’t believe you!” Dunechka shouted in a real frenzy, losing her head completely.

Almost fainting, she fell onto a chair which Svidrigailov hastened to place for her.

“Avdotya Romanovna, what is the matter, come to yourself! Here is water. Drink one sip...”

He sprinkled water on her. Dunechka shuddered and came to herself.

“It has had a strong effect!” Svidrigailov muttered to himself, frowning. “Avdotya Romanovna, calm yourself! Know that he has friends. We will save him, rescue him. Would you like me to take him abroad? I have money; I can get a ticket in three days. And as for his having killed, he will still do many good deeds, so that all this will be smoothed over; calm yourself. He may still be a great man. Well, what is the matter with you? How do you feel?”

“Wicked man! He is still mocking. Let me go...”

“Where are you going? Where are you going?”

“To him. Where is he? Do you know? Why is this door locked? We came in through this door, and now it is locked with a key. When did you manage to lock it?”

“One could hardly shout through all the rooms what we were talking about here. I am not mocking at all; I am only tired of talking in this language. Well, where are you going in such a state? Or do you want to betray him? You will drive him to frenzy, and he will give himself up. Know that they are already watching him; they are already on his track. You will only give him away. Wait: I saw him and spoke with him just now; he can still be saved. Wait, sit down, let us think it over together. That is why I called you, to talk about this alone and think it over properly. Do sit down!”

“How can you save him? Can he be saved?”

Dunya sat down. Svidrigailov sat down beside her.

“All this depends on you, on you, on you alone,” he began, with flashing eyes, almost in a whisper, losing the thread and not even getting some words out from agitation.

Dunya drew further away from him in fright. He too was trembling all over.

“You... one word from you, and he is saved! I... I will save him. I have money and friends. I will send him off at once, and I will take a passport myself, two passports. One his, the other mine. I have friends; I have men of business... Do you want it? I will get a passport for you too... for your mother... what do you want with Razumikhin? I love you too... I love you infinitely. Let me kiss the hem of your dress, let me! let me! I can’t bear hearing it rustle. Tell me: do this, and I will do it! I will do everything. I will do the impossible. What you believe, I will believe too. I will do everything, everything! Don’t look, don’t look at me like that! Do you know that you are killing me...”

He was even beginning to rave. Something had suddenly happened to him, as if it had suddenly gone to his head. Dunya jumped up and rushed to the door.

“Open! open!” she shouted through the door, calling for someone and shaking the door with her hands. “Open, then! Is there really no one?”

Svidrigailov stood up and came to himself. A malicious and mocking smile was slowly forcing itself onto his still trembling lips.

“There is no one at home there,” he said quietly and deliberately. “The landlady has gone out, and it is wasted effort shouting like that: you only agitate yourself for nothing.”

“Where is the key? Open the door at once, at once, you low man!”

“I have lost the key and cannot find it.”

“Ah! So this is violence!” cried Dunya; she turned pale as death and rushed into the corner, where she quickly shielded herself with a little table that happened to be at hand. She did not scream; but she fixed her eyes on her tormentor and watched his every movement keenly. Svidrigailov did not move from his place either, and stood facing her at the other end of the room. He had even mastered himself, at least outwardly. But his face was as pale as before. The mocking smile did not leave him.

“You said ‘violence’ just now, Avdotya Romanovna. If it is violence, you can judge for yourself that I have taken measures. Sofya Semyonovna is not at home; it is very far to the Kapernaumovs’, five locked rooms. Finally, I am at least twice as strong as you, and besides, I have nothing to fear, because you cannot complain afterwards either: you won’t really want to betray your brother, will you? And no one will believe you anyway: why on earth would a girl go alone to a single man’s lodging? So that even if you sacrifice your brother, you will prove nothing even then: violence is very hard to prove, Avdotya Romanovna.”

“Scoundrel!” Dunya whispered in indignation.

“As you like, but note that I was speaking only by way of supposition. In my own personal conviction you are perfectly right: violence is a vileness. I said it only to show that absolutely nothing would remain on your conscience, even if... even if you wanted to save your brother voluntarily, in the way I propose to you. You would simply have submitted to circumstances, well, to force, finally, if one can’t do without that word. Think about it; the fate of your brother and your mother is in your hands. And I will be your slave... all my life... I will wait here...”

Svidrigailov sat down on the sofa, about eight paces from Dunya. For her there was no longer the slightest doubt of his unshakeable resolve. Besides, she knew him...

Suddenly she took a revolver out of her pocket, cocked it and lowered her hand with the revolver onto the little table. Svidrigailov jumped up from his place.

“Aha! So that’s how it is!” he cried in surprise, but smiling maliciously. “Well, that changes the course of things completely! You are making things extremely easy for me yourself, Avdotya Romanovna! But where did you get a revolver? Surely not from Mr Razumikhin? Ba! But the revolver is mine! An old acquaintance! And how I searched for it then!.. Our country shooting lessons, which I had the honour of giving you, were not wasted after all.”

“It is not your revolver, but Marfa Petrovna’s, whom you killed, you villain! You had nothing of your own in her house. I took it when I began to suspect what you were capable of. Dare to take even one step, and I swear I will kill you!”

Dunya was in a frenzy. She held the revolver ready.

“Well, and your brother? I ask out of curiosity,” said Svidrigailov, still standing in his place.

“Inform on him if you like! Don’t move! Don’t come near! I’ll shoot! You poisoned your wife, I know it; you are a murderer yourself!..”

“And are you firmly convinced that I poisoned Marfa Petrovna?”

“You! You hinted at it yourself; you talked to me about poison... I know you went to fetch it... you had it ready... It was certainly you... scoundrel!”

“Even if it were true, it would be because of you... all the same you would have been the cause.”

“You’re lying! I always hated you, always...”

“Ehe, Avdotya Romanovna! You seem to have forgotten how in the heat of propaganda you were already inclining and melting... I saw it in your little eyes; remember, in the evening, in the moonlight, and the nightingale was singing too?”

“You’re lying!” (fury flashed in Dunya’s eyes) “You’re lying, you slanderer!”

“Lying? Well, perhaps I am lying. I lied. One shouldn’t mention these little things to women.” (He smiled.) “I know you will shoot, you pretty little wild thing. Well, shoot!”

Dunya raised the revolver and, deathly pale, her lower lip gone white and trembling, her big black eyes flashing like fire, she looked at him, resolved, measuring and waiting for the first movement on his part. Never had he seen her so beautiful. The fire that flashed from her eyes at the moment she raised the revolver seemed to scorch him, and his heart contracted with pain. He took a step, and the shot rang out. The bullet grazed his hair and struck the wall behind. He stopped and laughed quietly:

“The wasp has stung! Aims straight at the head... What is this? Blood!” He took out a handkerchief to wipe off the blood that was running in a thin trickle down his right temple; the bullet had probably just grazed the skin of his skull. Dunya lowered the revolver and looked at Svidrigailov not so much in fear as in a kind of wild bewilderment. It was as if she herself no longer understood what she had done and what was happening!

“Well, a miss! Shoot again, I am waiting,” Svidrigailov said quietly, still smiling, but somehow gloomily. “At this rate I shall have time to seize you before you cock it!”

Dunechka shuddered, quickly cocked the revolver and raised it again.

“Leave me alone!” she said in despair. “I swear I will shoot again... I... will kill you!..”

“Well, what of it... at three paces you can’t help killing. Well, but if you don’t kill me... then...” His eyes flashed, and he took two more steps.

Dunechka fired: a misfire!

“You loaded it carelessly. Never mind! You have another cap there. Put it right; I’ll wait.”

He stood two steps away from her, waiting and looking at her with wild resolve, with an inflamed, passionate, heavy look. Dunya understood that he would sooner die than let her go. “And... and of course she will kill him now, at two paces!..”

Suddenly she threw the revolver away.

“She has thrown it away!” Svidrigailov said in surprise, and drew a deep breath. Something seemed to have lifted from his heart all at once, and perhaps not only the weight of mortal fear; indeed, he hardly felt it at that moment. It was a deliverance from another, more sorrowful and gloomy feeling, which he could not have defined in all its force himself.

He went up to Dunya and quietly put his arm round her waist. She did not resist, but, trembling all over like a leaf, looked at him with imploring eyes. He wanted to say something, but his lips only twisted, and he could not get it out.

“Let me go!” Dunya said imploringly. Svidrigailov shuddered: this “you”, the familiar “you”, was spoken somehow not as it had been before.

“So you don’t love me?” he asked quietly.

Dunya shook her head.

“And... you can’t?.. Never?” he whispered in despair.

“Never!” whispered Dunya.

There passed an instant of terrible, mute struggle in Svidrigailov’s soul. He looked at her with an inexpressible look. Suddenly he took away his arm, turned away, walked quickly to the window and stood before it.

Another instant passed.

“Here is the key!” (He took it out of the left pocket of his coat and put it on the table behind him, without looking or turning to Dunya.) “Take it; go quickly!..”

He was looking stubbornly out of the window.

Dunya went to the table to take the key.

“Quickly! Quickly!” repeated Svidrigailov, still not moving and not turning round. But in this “quickly”, evidently, some terrible note had sounded.

Dunya understood it, seized the key, rushed to the door, quickly unlocked it and broke out of the room. A minute later, like a madwoman, not knowing what she was doing, she ran out onto the canal and ran in the direction of the — Bridge.

Svidrigailov stood at the window about three minutes more; at last he slowly turned round, looked about him and quietly passed his palm over his forehead. A strange smile twisted his face, a pitiful, sad, weak smile, a smile of despair. The blood, already drying, had stained his palm; he looked at the blood angrily; then he wetted a towel and washed his temple. The revolver, which Dunya had thrown away and which had flown to the door, suddenly caught his eye. He picked it up and examined it. It was a small, pocket, three-shot revolver of an old make; there were still two charges in it and one cap. It could be fired once. He thought, thrust the revolver into his pocket, took his hat and went out.
