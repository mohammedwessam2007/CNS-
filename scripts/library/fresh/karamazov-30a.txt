## Book Eleven, Chapter VIII · The Third and Last Meeting with Smerdyakov

He was still only halfway there when a sharp, dry wind rose, like the one early that morning, and a fine, thick, dry snow began to fall. It fell on the ground without sticking to it; the wind whirled it about, and soon there was a regular blizzard. In the part of the town where Smerdyakov lived there are hardly any street lamps. Ivan Fyodorovich strode on in the darkness, not noticing the blizzard, making out the way by instinct. His head ached and his temples throbbed tormentingly. In his wrists, he could feel it, there were spasms. A little before he reached Marya Kondratyevna’s cottage, Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly met a solitary drunk, a little peasant in a patched coat, walking in zigzags, grumbling and swearing, then suddenly breaking off his swearing and beginning a song in a hoarse, drunken voice:

> Oh, Vanka’s gone to Petersburg,
> I won’t wait for him to come!

But he kept breaking off at the second line and swearing at someone again, then suddenly striking up the same song again. Ivan Fyodorovich had long been feeling a terrible hatred for him, before he had even thought about him, and suddenly he became aware of him. At once he felt an irresistible desire to knock the little peasant down with his fist. Just at that moment they came abreast, and the little peasant, swaying heavily, suddenly lurched against Ivan with all his weight. Ivan pushed him off furiously. The little peasant flew back and fell like a log on the frozen ground, gave only one painful moan, “O-oh!”, and was silent. Ivan stepped up to him. He lay on his back, quite motionless, unconscious. “He’ll freeze!” thought Ivan, and strode on again to Smerdyakov’s.

In the passage Marya Kondratyevna, who had run out with a candle in her hand to open the door, whispered to him that Pavel Fyodorovich (that is, Smerdyakov) was very ill, sir; not that he was in bed, sir, but almost as if he were not in his right mind, sir, and he had even told her to take away the tea; he didn’t want to drink it.

“What, is he making a row?” Ivan Fyodorovich asked rudely.

“Oh no, on the contrary, he is quite quiet, sir; only don’t talk with him very long…” Marya Kondratyevna begged.

Ivan Fyodorovich opened the door and stepped into the room.

It was heated as before, but some changes could be noticed in the room: one of the side benches had been taken out, and in its place stood a large old leather sofa of imitation mahogany. A bed had been made up on it, with fairly clean white pillows. On the bed sat Smerdyakov, in the same dressing gown. The table had been moved in front of the sofa, so that the room had become very cramped. On the table lay some thick book in a yellow cover, but Smerdyakov was not reading it; he seemed to be sitting and doing nothing. He met Ivan Fyodorovich with a long, silent look and evidently was not in the least surprised at his coming. His face had changed very much; he had grown very thin and yellow. His eyes were sunken, the lower lids blue.

“Why, you really are ill?” Ivan Fyodorovich stopped. “I won’t keep you long; I won’t even take off my overcoat. Where can one sit down here?”

He went round to the other end of the table, drew a chair up to it and sat down.

“Why do you look at me and say nothing? I have come with only one question, and I swear I won’t leave without an answer: has the young lady, Katerina Ivanovna, been to see you?”

Smerdyakov was silent for a long time, still looking quietly at Ivan as before, but suddenly he waved his hand and turned his face away from him.

“What is it?” exclaimed Ivan.

“Nothing.”

“What do you mean, nothing?”

“Well, she has been; well, and it makes no difference to you. Leave me alone, sir.”

“No, I won’t leave you alone! Tell me, when was she here?”

“Why, I have quite forgotten to remember her,” Smerdyakov smiled contemptuously, and suddenly, turning his face to Ivan again, he stared at him with a sort of frenzied, hateful look, the very look with which he had looked at him at their meeting a month before.

“You seem to be ill yourself; see how you’ve fallen away; you look dreadful,” he said to Ivan.

“Never mind my health; tell me what you are asked.”

“And why have your eyes gone yellow? The whites are quite yellow. Are you tormenting yourself very much, or what?”

He smiled contemptuously, and suddenly laughed outright.

“Listen, I told you I won’t leave without an answer!” Ivan shouted in terrible irritation.

“Why do you keep on at me, sir? Why do you torment me?” Smerdyakov said with suffering.

“Oh, the devil! I have nothing to do with you. Answer the question and I’ll go at once.”

“I have nothing to answer you!” Smerdyakov looked down again.

“I assure you I will make you answer!”

“Why are you always so uneasy?” Smerdyakov suddenly stared at him, not exactly with contempt, but almost with a kind of disgust now. “Is it because the trial begins tomorrow? Why, nothing will happen to you; be sure of that at last! Go home, go to bed and sleep peacefully; don’t be afraid of anything.”

“I don’t understand you… what have I to be afraid of tomorrow?” Ivan said in surprise, and suddenly a kind of fright really did breathe coldly on his soul. Smerdyakov measured him with his eyes.

“You don’t un-der-stand?” he drawled reproachfully. “What a pleasure it must be for a clever man to play such a farce!”

Ivan looked at him in silence. This unexpected tone alone, quite unprecedentedly haughty, in which his former servant now addressed him, was extraordinary. There had been no such tone even the last time.

“I tell you, you have nothing to be afraid of. I shan’t say anything against you; there is no evidence. Look how your hands are shaking. Why are your fingers twitching? Go home; it wasn’t you who killed him.”

Ivan started; he remembered Alyosha.

“I know it wasn’t I…” he began to murmur.

“You kno-ow?” Smerdyakov caught him up again.

Ivan jumped up and seized him by the shoulder.

“Tell me everything, you reptile! Tell me everything!”

Smerdyakov was not in the least frightened. He only riveted his eyes on him with insane hatred.

“Why, it was you who killed him, if that’s how it is,” he whispered to him furiously.

Ivan sank down on the chair, as if he had reasoned something out. He smiled spitefully.

“You mean that business of that time? The same as last time?”

“Last time too you stood before me and understood everything, and you understand now.”

“All I understand is that you are mad.”

“Doesn’t a man ever get tired of it! Here we are sitting face to face; why should we fool each other, it seems, and play a farce? Or do you still want to throw it all on me alone, to my face? You killed him; you are the chief murderer, and I was only your accomplice, your faithful servant Lichard, and I did the deed on your word.”

“Did the deed? Was it you who killed him, then?” Ivan went cold.

Something seemed to shake in his brain, and he began to tremble all over with a fine, cold trembling. At this Smerdyakov himself looked at him in surprise: probably Ivan’s fright had struck him at last by its sincerity.

“Can you really not have known anything?” he murmured distrustfully, smiling crookedly in his face.

Ivan kept looking at him; he seemed to have lost his tongue.

> Oh, Vanka’s gone to Petersburg,
> I won’t wait for him to come,

suddenly rang in his head.

“Do you know what: I am afraid that you are a dream, that you are a phantom sitting before me?” he murmured.

“There is no phantom here, sir, besides the two of us, sir, and a certain third. Without a doubt he is here now, that third one, between the two of us.”

“Who is he? Who is here? Who is the third?” Ivan Fyodorovich said in fright, looking round and hurriedly searching with his eyes for someone in all the corners.

“That third is God, sir, providence itself, sir; it is here now beside us, sir; only don’t look for it; you won’t find it.”

“You lied that you killed him!” Ivan yelled furiously. “You are either mad or teasing me, as you did last time!”

Smerdyakov, as before, not frightened at all, kept watching him searchingly. He still could not overcome his distrust; it still seemed to him that Ivan “knew everything” and was only pretending, so as “to throw it all on him alone, to his face”.

“Wait, sir,” he said at last in a weak voice, and suddenly, drawing his left leg out from under the table, he began to roll up his trouser leg. The leg turned out to be in a long white stocking and shod in a slipper. Without hurrying, Smerdyakov took off his garter and thrust his fingers deep into the stocking. Ivan Fyodorovich looked at him and suddenly began to shake in convulsive fright.

“Madman!” he yelled, and, jumping up quickly, staggered back so that he struck his back against the wall and seemed to stick to the wall, drawn out straight as a thread. He was looking at Smerdyakov in insane horror. Smerdyakov, not in the least disturbed by his fright, was still fumbling in his stocking, as if still trying to catch hold of something in it with his fingers and pull it out. At last he caught hold of it and began to pull. Ivan Fyodorovich saw that it was some papers, or a bundle of papers. Smerdyakov pulled it out and laid it on the table.

“There, sir!” he said quietly.

“What?” Ivan answered, shaking.

“Kindly look, sir,” Smerdyakov said just as quietly.

Ivan stepped up to the table, took hold of the bundle and began to unfold it, but suddenly jerked his fingers away as if from the touch of some loathsome, terrible reptile.

“Your fingers keep trembling, sir, in a spasm,” Smerdyakov remarked, and unhurriedly unfolded the paper himself. Under the wrapping there turned out to be three packets of hundred-rouble rainbow notes.

“It’s all here, sir, all three thousand; you needn’t even count it. Take it, sir,” he invited Ivan, nodding at the money. Ivan sank down on the chair. He was white as a sheet.

“You frightened me… with that stocking…” he said, grinning somehow strangely.

“Can you, can you really not have known till now?” Smerdyakov asked once more.

“No, I didn’t know. I always thought it was Dmitri. Brother! Brother! Ah!” He suddenly clutched his head in both hands. “Listen: did you kill him alone? Without my brother, or with him?”

“Only together with you, sir; I killed him together with you, sir; and Dmitri Fyodorovich is as innocent as can be, sir.”

“All right, all right… About me later. Why do I keep trembling?… I can’t get a word out.”

“You were always bold then, sir; ‘everything is permitted’, you used to say, sir; and now see how frightened you are!” Smerdyakov murmured, wondering. “Won’t you have some lemonade? I’ll order it at once, sir. It can be very refreshing. Only this ought to be covered up first, sir.”

And he nodded at the packets again. He moved to get up and call through the door to Marya Kondratyevna to make some lemonade and bring it; but, looking for something to cover the money with, so that she would not see it, he first took out his handkerchief, and since it again proved to be thoroughly soiled with blowing his nose, he took from the table the one thick yellow book lying on it, which Ivan had noticed when he came in, and weighted the money down with it. The title of the book was The Sayings of Our Holy Father Isaac the Syrian. Ivan Fyodorovich managed to read the title mechanically.

“I don’t want lemonade,” he said. “About me later. Sit down and tell me: how did you do it? Tell me everything…”

“You might at least take off your overcoat, sir, or you’ll be all in a sweat.”

Ivan Fyodorovich, as if only now realising it, tore off his overcoat and threw it, without getting up from the chair, onto the bench.

“Speak, please, speak!”

He seemed to have grown calm. He waited with assurance for Smerdyakov to tell everything now.

“About how it was done, sir?” Smerdyakov sighed. “It was done in the most natural way, sir, from those very words of yours…”

“About my words later,” Ivan interrupted again, but no longer shouting as before, pronouncing his words firmly and as if he had quite mastered himself. “Just tell me in detail how you did it. Everything in order. Don’t forget anything. Details, details above all. I beg you.”

“You went away, and I fell into the cellar then, sir…”

“In a fit, or did you sham?”

“Of course I shammed, sir. I shammed all of it. I went down the steps calmly, sir, to the very bottom, sir, and lay down calmly, sir, and as soon as I lay down, then I began to howl. And I thrashed about until they carried me out.”

“Wait! And all the time, and afterwards, and in the hospital, were you shamming all the time?”

“Not at all, sir. The very next day, in the morning, before the hospital, a real one struck me, and so violent that there hadn’t been one like it for many years. For two days I was completely unconscious.”

“All right, all right. Go on.”

“They laid me on that bed, sir; I knew it would be behind the partition, sir, because every time I was ill Marfa Ignatyevna always put me to bed for the night behind that very partition in her rooms, sir. She was always tender to me, ever since I was born, sir. At night I moaned, sir, only quietly. I kept waiting for Dmitri Fyodorovich.”

“Waiting how? For him to come to you?”

“Why to me? I was waiting for him to come to the house, because I had no doubt at all that he would come that very night; for, having lost me and having no information, he would certainly have to climb into the house himself over the fence, sir, as he knew how, sir, and do something or other.”

“And if he hadn’t come?”

“Then nothing would have happened, sir. I wouldn’t have dared without him.”

“All right, all right… speak more plainly; don’t hurry; above all, don’t leave anything out!”

“I was expecting him to kill Fyodor Pavlovich, sir… that for certain, sir. Because I had prepared him so… in the last days, sir… and above all he had come to know those signals. With his suspiciousness and the fury that had built up in him in those days, he was bound to get into the house itself by means of the signals, sir. That was certain. That is how I was expecting him, sir.”

“Wait,” Ivan interrupted. “If he had killed him, he would have taken the money and carried it off; that is how you must have reasoned, surely? Then what would have been left for you after him? I don’t see.”

“But he would never have found the money, sir. It was only I who told him the money was under the mattress. Only that wasn’t true, sir. Before, it had been in a box; that is how it was, sir. And afterwards I taught Fyodor Pavlovich, since he trusted me alone of all mankind, to move that very envelope with the money to the corner behind the icons, because there nobody at all would guess, especially if he came in a hurry. So that envelope was lying there in his corner behind the icons, sir. And to keep it under the mattress would have been simply absurd; in a box at least it was under lock and key. And now everyone here believes it was under the mattress. A foolish idea, sir. So if Dmitri Fyodorovich had committed that very murder, then, finding nothing, he would either have run away in haste, afraid of every rustle, as always happens with murderers, or he would have been arrested, sir. So then I could always, sir, the next day or even that same night, sir, climb up behind the icons and carry off that very money, sir, and it would all have fallen on Dmitri Fyodorovich. That I could always count on.”

“Well, and if he hadn’t killed him, but only beaten him?”

“If he hadn’t killed him, then of course I wouldn’t have dared to take the money, and it would have come to nothing. But there was this calculation too: that he would beat him senseless, and I would have time to take it then, and afterwards I would report to Fyodor Pavlovich that it was none other than Dmitri Fyodorovich who had beaten him and stolen the money.”

“Wait… I am getting confused. So it was Dmitri who killed him after all, and you only took the money?”

“No, it wasn’t he who killed him, sir. Why, I could tell you even now that he is the murderer… but I don’t want to lie before you now, because… because if you really, as I see myself, didn’t understand anything till now and weren’t pretending before me, so as to throw your obvious guilt on me to my face, still you are guilty of everything, sir; for you knew about the murder, sir, and you charged me to kill, sir, and you yourself, knowing everything, went away. That is why I want to prove it to your face this evening: that the chief murderer in all this is you alone, sir, and I am only not the chief one, though it was I who killed him. And you are the most rightful murderer!”

“Why, why am I a murderer? Oh God!” Ivan could not bear it at last, forgetting that he had put off everything about himself till the end of the conversation. “Is it still that Chermashnya? Wait, tell me: why did you need my consent, if you had already taken Chermashnya for consent? How will you explain that now?”

“Being sure of your consent, I would have known that you would not raise an outcry over those three thousand being lost when you came back, if for some reason the authorities suspected me instead of Dmitri Fyodorovich, or as Dmitri Fyodorovich’s accomplice; on the contrary, you would have protected me from others… And having got the inheritance, you could reward me afterwards too, sometime, for all the rest of my life; because after all you would have got that inheritance through me; whereas if he had married Agrafena Alexandrovna, you would have got nothing but a fig.”

“Ah! So you meant to torment me afterwards too, all my life!” Ivan ground out. “And what if I hadn’t gone away then, but had informed against you?”

“And what could you have informed then? That I was persuading you to go to Chermashnya? Why, that is nonsense, sir. Besides, after our conversation you would either go or stay. If you stayed, then nothing would have happened; I would have known, sir, that you didn’t want this business, and I wouldn’t have undertaken anything. And if you went, that meant you had assured me that you wouldn’t dare inform against me in court and would forgive me those three thousand. And you couldn’t have prosecuted me afterwards at all, because then I would have told everything in court, sir; that is, not that I stole or killed, I wouldn’t have said that, sir, but that you yourself had egged me on to steal and kill, and only I didn’t agree. That is why I needed your consent then, so that you couldn’t corner me with anything, sir; because where is your proof? Whereas I could always corner you, sir, by revealing what a thirst you had for your father’s death; and I give you my word, the public would all have believed it, and you would have been ashamed all your life.”

“So I had it, I had that thirst, did I?” Ivan ground out again.

“You undoubtedly had it, sir, and by your consent you silently allowed me this business then, sir,” Smerdyakov looked firmly at Ivan. He was very weak and spoke quietly and wearily, but something inward and hidden was kindling him; he evidently had some intention. Ivan felt it.

“Go on,” he said to him. “Go on about that night.”

“Well, what next, sir! There I lay, and I heard what seemed like the master crying out. And Grigory Vasilyevich had suddenly got up before that and gone out, and suddenly he yelled, then everything was quiet, darkness. I lay there waiting; my heart was pounding; I couldn’t bear it. At last I got up and went, sir. I see the window on the left into the garden is open at his place; I took another step to the left, sir, to listen whether he was sitting there alive or not, and I hear the master tossing about and groaning; so he is alive, sir. Ah, I think! I went up to the window and called to the master: ‘It’s me,’ I said. And he says to me: ‘He was here, he was here, he ran away!’ That is, Dmitri Fyodorovich had been there, sir. ‘He has killed Grigory!’ ‘Where?’ I whisper to him. ‘There, in the corner,’ he points; he is whispering too. ‘Wait,’ I say. I went to the corner to look, and by the wall I stumbled on Grigory Vasilyevich lying there, all covered in blood, unconscious. So it is true that Dmitri Fyodorovich was here, leapt into my head at once, and at once and there I decided to finish it all off suddenly, sir, since Grigory Vasilyevich, even if he was still alive, lying unconscious, would see nothing for the time being. There was only one risk, sir: that Marfa Ignatyevna might suddenly wake up. I felt it at that moment, only that thirst had taken hold of me entirely, so that it took my breath away. I came back under the window to the master and said: ‘She is here; she has come; Agrafena Alexandrovna has come; she wants to be let in.’ Why, he started all over like a baby: ‘Where is she? Where?’ he groans, and still doesn’t believe it. ‘There,’ I say, ‘she is standing there; open the door!’ He looks at me through the window, and believes and doesn’t believe, and is afraid to open; it’s me he is afraid of now, I think. And it was funny, too: suddenly I thought of tapping out those very signals on the window frame then, that Grushenka had come, right before his eyes: he didn’t seem to believe the words, but as soon as I tapped out the signals he ran at once to open the door. He opened it. I made to go in, but he stood there and wouldn’t let me past with his body. ‘Where is she, where is she?’ He looks at me and trembles. Well, I think, if he is so afraid of me, it’s bad! And then my legs even went weak from fear myself, that he wouldn’t let me into the rooms, or would cry out, or Marfa Ignatyevna would come running, or something else would happen; I don’t remember now; I must have been standing before him pale. I whisper to him: ‘But she is there, there under the window; how is it you didn’t see her?’ ‘Bring her, bring her!’ ‘But she is afraid,’ I say; ‘the shouting frightened her; she has hidden in a bush; go and call her yourself from the study.’ He ran, went to the window and put the candle on the windowsill. ‘Grushenka,’ he cries, ‘Grushenka, are you there?’ He is crying out like that, but he won’t lean out of the window, won’t move away from me, from that very fear, because he had grown very much afraid of me, and so he dared not move away from me. ‘There she is,’ I say (I went up to the window and leaned right out myself); ‘there she is in the bush, laughing at you; do you see?’ Suddenly he believed it; he simply shook all over; he was terribly in love with her, sir; and he leaned right out of the window. Then I seized that very iron paperweight on his table, you remember, sir, it must weigh about three pounds; I swung it and hit him from behind with the corner right on the crown of his head. He didn’t even cry out. He only sank down suddenly, and I hit him a second time and a third. At the third I felt that I had smashed it. He suddenly fell over on his back, face upwards, all covered in blood. I looked myself over: there was no blood on me; it hadn’t spattered; I wiped the paperweight and put it back, went behind the icons, took the money out of the envelope, and threw the envelope on the floor, and that very pink ribbon beside it. I went down into the garden, trembling all over. Straight to that apple tree with the hollow; you know that hollow, and I had had my eye on it for a long time; there was a rag and some paper lying in it already; I had got them ready long before; I wrapped the whole sum in the paper, and then in the rag, and pushed it in deep. So it stayed there for over two weeks, that very sum, sir; I took it out afterwards, after the hospital. I went back to my bed, lay down and thought in fear: ‘Now if Grigory Vasilyevich has been killed outright, then something very bad may come of it; but if he isn’t killed and comes to, then it will turn out very well, because he will be a witness that Dmitri Fyodorovich came, and so it was he who killed him and carried off the money, sir.’ Then from doubt and impatience I began to moan, so as to wake Marfa Ignatyevna as soon as possible. At last she got up and rushed to me, but as soon as she saw that Grigory Vasilyevich was not there, she ran out, and I heard her yell in the garden. Well, then it all went on all night, sir, and I was quite at ease about everything.”

The narrator stopped. Ivan had listened to him all the time in deathly silence, without moving, without taking his eyes off him. Smerdyakov, as he told it, only glanced at him now and then, and looked aside more. Having finished his story, he was evidently agitated himself and was breathing heavily. Sweat appeared on his face. It was impossible to guess, however, whether he felt remorse or what.

“Wait,” Ivan caught him up, thinking. “And the door? If he opened the door only to you, how could Grigory have seen it open before you? For Grigory saw it before you, didn’t he?”

It is remarkable that Ivan asked in the most peaceable voice, even in quite a different tone, as it were, not spiteful at all, so that if anyone had opened their door now and looked at them from the threshold, he would certainly have concluded that they were sitting talking peaceably about some ordinary, though interesting, subject.

“As for that door, and Grigory Vasilyevich supposedly having seen it open, he only fancied it,” Smerdyakov smiled crookedly. “For he isn’t a man, I tell you, sir, but no better than a stubborn gelding: he didn’t see it, but he fancied he saw it, and now you will never shake him, sir. It is just our good luck, yours and mine, that he thought of it, because after that Dmitri Fyodorovich will certainly be convicted completely.”

“Listen,” said Ivan Fyodorovich, as if beginning to lose himself again and struggling to grasp something, “listen… There was a lot more I wanted to ask you, but I have forgotten… I keep forgetting and getting confused… Yes! Tell me just this one thing: why did you open the envelope and leave it there on the floor? Why didn’t you simply carry it off in the envelope?… When you were telling it, it seemed to me that you spoke of that envelope as if that was how it had to be done… but why it had to be so, I can’t understand…”

“I did that for a certain reason, sir. For if a man was knowing and practised, as I am, for instance, who had seen that money himself beforehand and perhaps had wrapped it in that envelope himself and watched with his own eyes how it was sealed and inscribed, then why on earth should such a man, sir, if he were the one who killed him, say, open that envelope after the murder, and in such a hurry too, knowing quite certainly without that that the money was in that envelope for sure, sir? On the contrary, if it were a thief like me, for instance, he would simply have stuffed that envelope into his pocket without opening it at all and made off with it as fast as possible, sir. It is quite different with Dmitri Fyodorovich: he only knew of the envelope by hearsay; he had never seen it himself; and so when he got hold of it, say from under the mattress, he would have opened it there and then as quickly as possible, to make sure the money really was in it. And he would have thrown the envelope down there and then, not having time to think that it would be left behind as evidence against him; because he is an unpractised thief, sir, and had never stolen anything openly before, being a gentleman born, sir; and if he had made up his mind to steal now, it was as if not to steal, but only to come and take back what was his own, since he had announced it to the whole town beforehand and had even boasted aloud before everyone in advance that he would go and take his property from Fyodor Pavlovich. I didn’t exactly say this very idea plainly to the prosecutor at my examination, but on the contrary led him up to it as if by a hint, sir, as though I didn’t understand it myself, and as though he had thought of it himself and I hadn’t prompted him, sir; so that the prosecutor’s mouth even watered at that hint of mine, sir…”

“Can it be, can it be that you thought all this out then and there on the spot?” exclaimed Ivan Fyodorovich, beside himself with amazement. He was again looking at Smerdyakov in fright.

“Have mercy, could anyone think it all up in such a hurry, sir? It was all thought out beforehand.”

“Well… well, then the devil himself was helping you!” Ivan Fyodorovich exclaimed again. “No, you are no fool; you are much cleverer than I thought…”

He got up, evidently meaning to walk about the room. He was in terrible anguish. But since the table blocked the way and one almost had to squeeze between the table and the wall, he only turned round where he stood and sat down again. Perhaps not having been able to walk about suddenly irritated him, so that, almost in his former frenzy, he suddenly yelled:

“Listen, you wretched, contemptible man! Can’t you understand that if I haven’t killed you yet, it is only because I am keeping you to answer at the trial tomorrow? God sees,” Ivan raised his hand on high, “perhaps I too was guilty; perhaps I really had a secret desire that… my father should die; but I swear to you, I was not so guilty as you think, and perhaps I did not egg you on at all. No, no, I did not egg you on! But no matter; I shall give evidence against myself, tomorrow, at the trial; I have decided! I shall tell everything, everything. But we shall appear together, you and I! And whatever you say against me at the trial, whatever evidence you give, I accept it and am not afraid of you; I shall confirm everything myself! But you too must confess before the court! You must, you must; we shall go together! So it shall be!”

Ivan said this solemnly and with energy, and it could be seen from his flashing eyes alone that so it would be.

“You are ill, I see, sir, quite ill, sir. Your eyes are quite yellow, sir,” said Smerdyakov, but without any mockery at all, even as if sympathising.

“We shall go together!” Ivan repeated. “And if you won’t go, I shall confess alone just the same.”

Smerdyakov was silent, as if thinking it over.

“None of this will happen, sir, and you won’t go, sir,” he decided at last, without appeal.

“You don’t understand me!” Ivan exclaimed reproachfully.

“You will be too ashamed, sir, if you confess everything against yourself. And what is more, it will be useless, quite useless, sir; because I shall say straight out that I never told you anything of the kind, sir, and that you are either in some illness (and that is what it looks like, sir), or you are so sorry for your brother that you have sacrificed yourself and made it up against me, since all your life you have regarded me as a midge anyway, and not as a man. Well, and who will believe you, and what single proof have you got?”

“Listen: you showed me that money just now, of course, to convince me.”

Smerdyakov took Isaac the Syrian off the packets and laid it aside.

“Take that money with you, sir, carry it away,” Smerdyakov sighed.

“Of course I’ll take it! But why do you give it to me, if you killed him for it?” Ivan looked at him in great surprise.

“I don’t want it at all, sir,” Smerdyakov said in a trembling voice, waving his hand. “I did have that idea before, sir, that I would begin a life with such money, in Moscow, or better still abroad; I had that dream, sir; and most of all because ‘everything is permitted’. That is what you really taught me, sir, for you used to talk to me a lot about it then: for if there is no infinite God, then there is no virtue either, and no need of it at all then. That was really you. That is how I reasoned it out.”

“You arrived at it with your own wits?” Ivan smiled crookedly.

“Under your guidance, sir.”

“And now, then, you have come to believe in God, since you are giving the money back?”

“No, sir, I haven’t come to believe, sir,” whispered Smerdyakov.

“Then why are you giving it back?”

“Stop it… there’s no point, sir!” Smerdyakov waved his hand again. “You yourself used to say then that everything is permitted; so why are you so alarmed now, sir, you yourself? You even want to go and give evidence against yourself… Only none of that will happen! You won’t go and give evidence!” Smerdyakov decided again firmly and with conviction.

“You’ll see!” said Ivan.

“It can’t be. You are very clever, sir. You love money, I know that, sir; you love respect too, because you are very proud; you love feminine charms excessively; and most of all you love to live in peaceful comfort and not to bow to anyone; that most of all, sir. You won’t want to spoil your life for ever by taking such shame on yourself at the trial. You are like Fyodor Pavlovich, most of all, sir; of all his children you have turned out most like him, with the same soul as he, sir.”

“You are no fool,” said Ivan, as if struck; the blood rushed to his face. “I used to think you were a fool. You are serious now!” he remarked, looking at Smerdyakov somehow in a new way all of a sudden.

“It was your pride that made you think I was a fool. Take the money, sir.”

Ivan took all three packets of notes and stuffed them into his pocket without wrapping them in anything.

“I shall show them at the trial tomorrow,” he said.

“Nobody will believe you there, sir, seeing that you have plenty of money of your own now; you took it out of your cashbox and brought it, sir.”

Ivan got up from his place.

“I repeat to you: if I haven’t killed you, it is only because you are needed for tomorrow; remember that; don’t forget it!”

“Well, kill me, sir. Kill me now,” Smerdyakov said suddenly, strangely, looking strangely at Ivan. “You won’t dare even that, sir,” he added, smiling bitterly. “You won’t dare anything, you who were so bold, sir!”

“Till tomorrow!” Ivan shouted, and moved to go.

“Wait… show them to me once more.”

Ivan took out the notes and showed them to him. Smerdyakov looked at them for about ten seconds.

“Well, go,” he said, waving his hand. “Ivan Fyodorovich!” he suddenly called after him again.

“What do you want?” Ivan turned round, already moving away.

“Goodbye, sir!”

“Till tomorrow!” Ivan shouted again, and went out of the room.

The blizzard was still going on. He took the first steps briskly, but suddenly he seemed to begin to stagger. “It’s something physical,” he thought with a smile. A sort of joy seemed to have descended into his soul now. He felt in himself an infinite firmness: an end to the wavering that had so terribly tormented him all this last time! The decision was taken, “and now it won’t change,” he thought with happiness. At that moment he suddenly stumbled over something and nearly fell. Stopping, he made out at his feet the little peasant he had knocked down, still lying in the same place, unconscious and motionless. The blizzard had already covered almost all his face. Ivan suddenly seized him and dragged him along on himself. Seeing a light in a little house on the right, he went up, knocked on the shutters, and asked the townsman who owned the little house and who answered to help him drag the peasant to the police station, promising to give him three roubles for it then and there. The townsman got ready and came out. I will not describe in detail how Ivan Fyodorovich managed then to achieve his aim and get the peasant settled at the police station, with a view to having him examined by a doctor at once, for which again he gave freely “for expenses”. I will say only that the business took almost a whole hour. But Ivan Fyodorovich remained very pleased. His thoughts were ranging and working. “If my decision for tomorrow had not been taken so firmly,” he suddenly thought with delight, “I wouldn’t have stopped for a whole hour to settle the little peasant, but would have passed him by and only spat at the thought that he would freeze… But how well I can observe myself,” he thought at the same moment with still greater delight, “and they have decided that I am going mad!” On reaching his house he suddenly stopped at a sudden question: “Oughtn’t I to go at once, now, to the prosecutor and tell everything?” He settled the question by turning back towards the house again. “Everything together tomorrow!” he whispered to himself; and, strange to say, almost all his joy, all his satisfaction with himself, passed in a moment. And when he went into his room, something icy suddenly touched his heart, like a memory, or rather a reminder, of something tormenting and loathsome that was in that room precisely now, at this moment, and had been there before too. He sank wearily onto his sofa. The old woman brought him the samovar; he made tea but did not touch it; he sent the old woman away till the morning. He sat on the sofa and felt giddy. He felt that he was ill and powerless. He began to fall asleep, but got up uneasily and walked about the room to drive away sleep. At moments it seemed to him that he was delirious. But it was not his illness that occupied him most; sitting down again, he began now and then to look round, as if looking out for something. So it happened several times. At last his gaze was fixed intently on one point. Ivan smiled, but a flush of anger flooded his face. He sat a long time in his place, his head propped firmly in both hands, and yet looking sideways all the while at the same point, at the sofa that stood against the opposite wall. Evidently something there was irritating him, some object; it troubled him, tormented him.
