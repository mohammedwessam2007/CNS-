In the department of... but it is better not to say which department. There is nothing more touchy than every kind of department, regiment, government office and, in a word, every kind of official body. Nowadays every private person considers the whole of society insulted in his own person. They say that quite recently a petition came in from a certain district police chief, I don’t remember of which town, in which he set out clearly that the laws of the state were perishing and that his sacred name was being taken absolutely in vain. And as proof he attached to his petition an enormous volume of some romantic work in which, every ten pages, a district police chief appears, in places even completely drunk. And so, to avoid any unpleasantness, we had better call the department in question a certain department. And so, in a certain department there served a certain clerk; a clerk who could not be called very remarkable: short, somewhat pockmarked, somewhat reddish-haired, even somewhat short-sighted to look at, with a small bald patch on his forehead, wrinkles on both cheeks, and the kind of complexion that is called haemorrhoidal... What can one do! The Petersburg climate is to blame. As for his rank (for with us the rank must be announced first of all), he was what is called an eternal titular councillor, a low rank which, as everyone knows, has been mocked and jeered at to their hearts’ content by various writers who have the praiseworthy habit of attacking those who cannot bite back. The clerk’s surname was Bashmachkin. From the very name it is clear that it once came from *bashmak*, a shoe; but when, at what time and in what way it came from a shoe, nothing of that is known. His father, his grandfather and even his brother-in-law, and absolutely all the Bashmachkins, went about in boots, having them resoled only three times a year. His name was Akaky Akakievich. Perhaps it will seem to the reader somewhat strange and far-fetched, but one can assure him that it was not looked for at all, but that circumstances arose of their own accord which made it quite impossible to give him any other name; and this is exactly how it happened. Akaky Akakievich was born towards night, if memory does not deceive, on the 23rd of March. His late mother, a clerk’s wife and a very good woman, arranged, as is proper, to have the child christened. His mother was still lying in bed facing the door, and on her right stood the godfather, a most excellent man, Ivan Ivanovich Yeroshkin, who served as head clerk in the Senate, and the godmother, the wife of a district police officer, a woman of rare virtues, Arina Semyonovna Belobryushkova. The mother was offered her choice of any of three names: Mokkius, Sossius, or naming the child after the martyr Khozdazat. “No,” thought the late lady, “those are all such names.” To please her, they opened the church calendar at another place; out came three names again: Trifily, Dula and Varakhasy. “What a punishment,” said the old woman, “what names they all are; I really have never heard such names. Varadat or Varukh, perhaps, but Trifily and Varakhasy!” They turned another page, and out came: Pavsikakhy and Vakhtisy. “Well, I see,” said the old woman, “that this must be his fate. If so, better let him be called after his father. His father was Akaky, so let the son be Akaky too.” And that is how Akaky Akakievich came about. The child was christened, and during it he cried and made such a face as if he foresaw that he would be a titular councillor. So that is how it all came about. We have told this so that the reader can see for himself that it happened entirely out of necessity, and that it was quite impossible to give him any other name. When and at what time he entered the department, and who appointed him, nobody could remember. However many directors and chiefs of every kind changed, he was always seen in one and the same place, in the same position, in the same post, the same copying clerk, so that people became convinced at last that he must have been born into the world already completely ready, in uniform and with a bald patch on his head. In the department no respect at all was shown him. The porters not only did not get up from their seats when he passed, but did not even glance at him, as if a simple fly had flown through the waiting room. His superiors treated him in a sort of coldly despotic way. Some assistant head clerk would shove papers right under his nose without even saying “copy this”, or “here is an interesting, nice little piece of work”, or anything pleasant, as is customary in well-mannered offices. And he would take it, looking only at the paper, without looking to see who had put it there or whether he had the right to. He would take it and at once settle down to copy it. The young clerks laughed and made jokes at him, as far as their office wit allowed, told all sorts of stories about him in front of him, invented about him; they said his landlady, an old woman of seventy, beat him; they asked when their wedding would be; they scattered bits of paper on his head and called it snow. But Akaky Akakievich answered not a word, as if there were no one in front of him; it did not even affect his work: amid all this pestering he did not make a single mistake in his copying. Only if the joke was too unbearable, when they jogged his arm and kept him from his work, would he say: “Leave me alone; why do you insult me?” And there was something strange in the words and in the voice in which they were spoken. Something in it moved one to pity, so much so that one young man, newly appointed, who, following the example of the others, had allowed himself to make fun of him, suddenly stopped as if pierced, and from then on everything seemed to change before him and appear in a different light. Some unnatural force pushed him away from the colleagues he had got to know, taking them for decent, well-bred people. And long afterwards, in the midst of his gayest moments, there would appear to him the little clerk with the bald patch on his forehead, with his piercing words: “Leave me alone; why do you insult me?”, and in those piercing words rang other words: “I am your brother.” And the poor young man would cover his face with his hand, and many times afterwards in his life he shuddered, seeing how much inhumanity there is in man, how much savage coarseness is hidden in refined, educated good manners, and, God! even in a man whom the world regards as noble and honourable...

It would be hard to find a man anywhere who lived so in his work. It is not enough to say that he served zealously; no, he served with love. There, in this copying, he saw a varied and pleasant world of his own. Delight showed in his face; certain letters were his favourites, and when he came to them he was beside himself: he chuckled and winked and helped with his lips, so that one could, it seemed, read in his face every letter his pen was forming. If he had been rewarded in proportion to his zeal, he might, to his own astonishment, have even become a state councillor; but all he earned, as his witty colleagues put it, was a buckle in his buttonhole and piles in his backside. However, it cannot be said that no attention was paid to him at all. One director, being a kind man and wishing to reward him for his long service, ordered that he be given something more important than ordinary copying: he was told to draw up, from a case already completed, a letter to another government office; all it involved was changing the heading and changing some of the verbs from the first person to the third. This gave him so much work that he broke into a sweat, rubbed his forehead and finally said: “No, better let me copy something.” From then on they left him to copy for ever. Outside this copying, nothing seemed to exist for him. He gave no thought at all to his clothes: his uniform was not green but some sort of rusty floury colour. Its collar was narrow and low, so that his neck, although it was not long, seemed extraordinarily long as it came out of the collar, like those plaster kittens with nodding heads that foreigners carry on their heads by the dozen to sell in Russia. And something was always sticking to his uniform: a wisp of hay, or a bit of thread; besides, he had a special knack, when walking in the street, of passing under a window just as some rubbish was being thrown out of it, and so he was forever carrying away melon and watermelon rinds and such nonsense on his hat. Not once in his life did he pay attention to what was happening and going on every day in the street, which, as everyone knows, his fellow young clerk will always look at, carrying the sharpness of his lively gaze so far that he will even notice whose trouser strap has come undone at the bottom on the other side of the pavement, which always brings a sly grin to his face.

But Akaky Akakievich, if he looked at anything at all, saw on everything his own clean lines, written in an even hand; and only if, coming from heaven knows where, a horse’s muzzle came to rest on his shoulder and blew a whole wind from its nostrils onto his cheek, only then did he notice that he was not in the middle of a line but rather in the middle of the street. When he got home he sat down at once at the table, hastily gulped his cabbage soup and ate a piece of beef with onion, without noticing their taste at all; he ate it all with the flies and with whatever else God sent at the time. Noticing that his stomach was beginning to swell, he would get up from the table, take out his bottle of ink and copy the papers he had brought home. If there were none, he would make a copy for himself, on purpose, for his own pleasure, especially if the paper was remarkable not for the beauty of its style but for being addressed to some new or important person.

Even in those hours when the grey Petersburg sky has gone quite dark and all the clerkly folk have eaten and dined, each as he could, according to the salary he received and his own fancy; when everyone has rested after the scratching of pens in the department, the running about, their own and other people’s necessary business, and all that restless man voluntarily sets himself, even more than is necessary; when the clerks hurry to give the rest of their time to pleasure: the livelier one rushes to the theatre; another to the street, to spend it looking at ladies’ little hats; another to an evening party, to waste it on compliments to some pretty girl, the star of a small clerkly circle; another, and this happens most often, simply goes to see his fellow clerk on the fourth or third floor, in two little rooms with a hall or a kitchen and some pretensions to fashion, a lamp or some other knick-knack that cost many sacrifices, going without dinners and outings; in a word, even at the time when all the clerks scatter to the little flats of their friends to play a stormy game of whist, sipping tea from glasses with kopeck rusks, drawing smoke from long pipes, and telling, while the cards are dealt, some gossip carried down from high society, which a Russian can never give up in any condition, or even, when there is nothing to talk about, retelling the eternal anecdote about the commandant who was told that the tail of the horse on Falconet’s monument had been cut off; in a word, even when everyone is seeking amusement, Akaky Akakievich gave himself up to no amusement whatever. No one could say they had ever seen him at any evening party. Having written to his heart’s content, he went to bed, smiling in advance at the thought of tomorrow: what would God send him to copy tomorrow? So flowed the peaceful life of a man who, with a salary of four hundred roubles, knew how to be content with his lot; and so it might have flowed on into deep old age, if it were not for the various calamities scattered along the road of life, not only for titular councillors, but even for privy, actual, court and every other kind of councillor, even those who never give anyone counsel or take any themselves.

There is in Petersburg a powerful enemy of all who receive a salary of four hundred roubles a year or thereabouts. This enemy is none other than our northern frost, although they do say it is very healthy. After eight in the morning, just at the hour when the streets fill with people going to their departments, it begins to give such strong and stinging flicks, indiscriminately, to every nose, that the poor clerks absolutely do not know where to put them. At this time, when even those in the highest posts have aching foreheads from the frost and tears coming to their eyes, the poor titular councillors are sometimes defenceless. All their salvation lies in running as fast as they can, in their skimpy little overcoats, across five or six streets, and then stamping their feet hard in the porter’s lodge until all their faculties and talents for official duties, frozen on the way, have thawed out. For some time Akaky Akakievich had begun to feel that he was being stung especially hard in the back and shoulder, although he tried to run across the legal distance as fast as he could. He began to wonder at last whether there were not some sins in his overcoat. Having examined it well at home, he discovered that in two or three places, namely on the back and the shoulders, it had become as thin as muslin; the cloth had worn so thin that one could see through it, and the lining had fallen apart. It must be said that Akaky Akakievich’s overcoat was also a target for the clerks’ mockery; they even took away from it the noble name of overcoat and called it a dressing gown. It really was strangely made: its collar grew smaller every year, because it went to patch the other parts. The patching did not show the tailor’s art and came out, indeed, baggy and ugly. Seeing how matters stood, Akaky Akakievich decided that the overcoat would have to be taken to Petrovich, a tailor who lived somewhere on the fourth floor up a back staircase, and who, in spite of his one blind eye and the pockmarks all over his face, did a pretty good business mending clerks’ and all sorts of other trousers and tailcoats; when he was sober, of course, and was not nursing some other enterprise in his head. About this tailor, of course, one ought not to say much, but since it is now the custom that in a story the character of every person must be fully described, there is nothing for it: let us have Petrovich here too. At first he was simply called Grigory and was a serf belonging to some gentleman; he began to call himself Petrovich from the time he got his freedom and began to drink rather heavily on every holiday, at first on the big ones, and then, without distinction, on all the church holidays, wherever there was a little cross in the calendar. In this respect he was faithful to the customs of his forefathers, and when quarrelling with his wife he would call her a worldly woman and a German. Since we have mentioned his wife, we must say a word or two about her too; but unfortunately not much was known about her, except that Petrovich had a wife, and that she even wore a cap and not a headscarf; but it seems she could not boast of beauty; at any rate, on meeting her only the soldiers of the Guards would peep under her cap, twitching their moustaches and making a peculiar sound.

Climbing the stairs that led to Petrovich’s, which, to do them justice, were all anointed with water and slops and soaked through and through with that alcoholic smell which stings the eyes and, as is well known, is ever present on all the back staircases of Petersburg houses; climbing the stairs, Akaky Akakievich was already wondering how much Petrovich would ask, and resolved in his mind not to give more than two roubles. The door was open, because the landlady, cooking some fish, had let so much smoke into the kitchen that one could not even see the cockroaches. Akaky Akakievich passed through the kitchen unnoticed even by the landlady herself, and came at last into the room, where he saw Petrovich sitting on a wide unpainted wooden table with his legs tucked under him like a Turkish pasha. His feet, as is the custom with tailors sitting at work, were bare. And the first thing that caught the eye was his big toe, very well known to Akaky Akakievich, with a deformed nail, thick and strong as a tortoise’s shell. Round Petrovich’s neck hung a skein of silk and thread, and on his knees lay some old rag. For about three minutes he had been trying to thread a needle, kept missing, and so was very angry with the darkness and even with the thread itself, grumbling under his breath: “It won’t go in, the savage; you’ve got the better of me, you rascal!” Akaky Akakievich was sorry to have come at exactly the moment when Petrovich was angry: he liked to give Petrovich orders when Petrovich was already somewhat in his cups, or, as his wife put it, “soaked in rotgut, the one-eyed devil”. In that state Petrovich usually gave way very readily and agreed, and even bowed and thanked him every time. Afterwards, it is true, his wife would come, weeping that her husband had been drunk and so had taken the job too cheaply; but one had only to add ten kopecks and the thing was settled. Now, however, Petrovich seemed to be sober, and therefore stiff, hard to deal with, and inclined to ask the devil knows what prices. Akaky Akakievich realised this and was about to beat a retreat, as they say, but the thing had already been begun. Petrovich screwed up his one eye at him very intently, and Akaky Akakievich involuntarily said:

“Good day, Petrovich!”

“Good day to you, sir,” said Petrovich, and squinted his eye at Akaky Akakievich’s hands, wanting to see what sort of booty he was carrying.

“Well, I’ve come to you, Petrovich, you know...”

It must be said that Akaky Akakievich expressed himself mostly in prepositions, adverbs and, finally, the kind of particles that have absolutely no meaning. And if the matter was very difficult, he even had the habit of not finishing his sentences at all, so that very often, having begun a speech with the words, “This, really, is altogether, you know...”, nothing more would follow, and he himself would forget, thinking he had said everything.

“What is it, then?” said Petrovich, and at the same time looked over his whole uniform with his one eye, from the collar to the sleeves, the back, the tails and the buttonholes, all of which was very familiar to him, since it was his own work. That is the custom with tailors: it is the first thing they do when they meet you.

“Well, I, you know, Petrovich... the overcoat, the cloth... you see, everywhere else it’s quite strong; it’s got a bit dusty and it looks as though it were old, but it’s new; only in one place it’s a little, you know... on the back, and here on one shoulder it’s worn a little, and on this shoulder a little; you see, that’s all. And it’s not much work...”

Petrovich took the dressing gown, spread it out first on the table, examined it for a long time, shook his head, and reached to the window sill for a round snuffbox with a portrait of some general on it, which general nobody knew, because the place where the face had been had been poked through with a finger and then pasted over with a square scrap of paper. Having taken a pinch of snuff, Petrovich held the dressing gown out at arm’s length, examined it against the light, and again shook his head. Then he turned it lining upwards and shook his head again; again he took the lid off the general with the paper pasted over him, stuffed his nose with snuff, closed the box, put it away, and at last said:
