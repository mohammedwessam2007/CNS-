## On the Believers in a World Behind

Once Zarathustra too cast his illusion beyond man, like all who believe in a world behind this one. The world then seemed to me the work of a suffering and tormented God.

The world then seemed to me a dream and the poem of a God; coloured smoke before the eyes of a divinely discontented one.

Good and evil, and joy and sorrow, and I and you: coloured smoke before creative eyes, it seemed to me. The creator wanted to look away from himself: so he created the world.

It is a drunken joy for the sufferer to look away from his suffering and lose himself. A drunken joy and a losing of oneself: so the world once seemed to me.

This world, eternally imperfect, the image of an eternal contradiction and an imperfect image, a drunken joy for its imperfect creator: so the world once seemed to me.

So I too once cast my illusion beyond man, like all who believe in a world behind. Beyond man, in truth?

Ah, my brothers, this God whom I created was human work and human madness, like all gods!

He was a man, and only a poor piece of man and of I: he came to me out of my own ashes and fire, this ghost; and truly, he did not come to me from beyond!

What happened, my brothers? I overcame myself, the sufferer; I carried my own ashes to the mountain; I invented a brighter flame for myself. And look! Then the ghost *withdrew* from me!

To believe in such ghosts would now be suffering and torment for me, the convalescent: it would now be suffering and humiliation. So I speak to the believers in a world behind.

It was suffering and incapacity that created all worlds behind this one; and that brief madness of happiness which only the greatest sufferer knows.

Weariness, which wants to reach the ultimate with a single leap, with a death leap; a poor, ignorant weariness that no longer even wants to want: that created all gods and worlds behind.

Believe me, my brothers! It was the body that despaired of the body; it groped with the fingers of the deluded spirit at the last walls.

Believe me, my brothers! It was the body that despaired of the earth; it heard the belly of being speaking to it.

And then it wanted to get through the last walls with its head, and not only with its head: over into “that world”.

But “that world” is well hidden from man, that dehumanised, inhuman world which is a heavenly nothing; and the belly of being does not speak to man at all, except as man.

Truly, all being is hard to prove and hard to make speak. Tell me, my brothers, is not the strangest of all things still the best proved?

Yes, this I, and the I’s contradiction and confusion, still speaks most honestly of its being: this creating, willing, valuing I, which is the measure and the value of things.

And this most honest being, the I, speaks of the body, and it still wants the body, even when it writes poetry and raves and flutters with broken wings.

The I learns to speak more and more honestly: and the more it learns, the more words and honours it finds for the body and the earth.

My I taught me a new pride, and I teach it to men: no longer to bury one’s head in the sand of heavenly things, but to carry it freely, an earthly head that creates meaning for the earth!

I teach men a new will: to want this way which man has walked blindly, and to call it good, and no longer to creep away from it like the sick and the dying!

It was the sick and the dying who despised the body and the earth and invented the heavenly and the redeeming drops of blood: but even these sweet and gloomy poisons they took from the body and the earth!

They wanted to run away from their misery, and the stars were too far for them. Then they sighed: “Oh, if only there were heavenly paths by which to creep into another being and another happiness!” Then they invented their sly ways and their little bloody potions!

Now they imagined themselves carried away from their body and this earth, these ungrateful ones. But to whom did they owe the spasm and bliss of their carrying away? To their body and this earth.

Zarathustra is gentle with the sick. Truly, he is not angry at their kinds of comfort and ingratitude. May they become convalescents and overcomers, and create a higher body for themselves!

Nor is Zarathustra angry with the convalescent when he looks tenderly at his illusion and creeps round the grave of his God at midnight: but sickness and a sick body remain for me even in his tears.

There have always been many sickly people among those who write poetry and long for God; furiously they hate the man of knowledge, and that youngest of virtues which is called honesty.

They always look back to dark ages: then, to be sure, illusion and faith were another thing; the frenzy of reason was likeness to God, and doubt was sin.

I know these godlike ones all too well: they want to be believed in, and doubt to be sin. I know all too well, too, what they themselves believe in most.

Truly, not in worlds behind and redeeming drops of blood: they too believe most in the body, and their own body is their thing-in-itself.

But it is a sickly thing to them, and they would gladly jump out of their skins. That is why they listen to the preachers of death and themselves preach worlds behind.

Listen rather, my brothers, to the voice of the healthy body: it is a more honest and purer voice.

The healthy body, the perfect and square-built, speaks more honestly and more purely: and it speaks of the meaning of the earth.

Thus spoke Zarathustra.

## On the Despisers of the Body

To the despisers of the body I will speak my word. I do not want them to learn differently or teach differently, but only to say farewell to their own bodies, and so fall silent.

“I am body and soul”: so speaks the child. And why should one not speak like children?

But the awakened one, the knowing one, says: I am body entirely, and nothing besides; and soul is only a word for something about the body.

The body is a great reason, a multiplicity with one sense, a war and a peace, a herd and a shepherd.

An instrument of your body is your little reason too, my brother, which you call “spirit”: a little instrument and toy of your great reason.

You say “I” and are proud of this word. But the greater thing, in which you do not want to believe, is your body and its great reason: it does not say I, but does I.

What the senses feel, what the spirit knows, never has its end in itself. But the senses and the spirit would like to persuade you that they are the end of all things: so vain are they.

The senses and the spirit are instruments and toys: behind them lies the Self. The Self seeks with the eyes of the senses too; it listens with the ears of the spirit too.

The Self is always listening and seeking: it compares, subdues, conquers, destroys. It rules, and is the ruler of the I as well.

Behind your thoughts and feelings, my brother, stands a mighty commander, an unknown sage: his name is Self. He lives in your body; he is your body.

There is more reason in your body than in your best wisdom. And who knows why your body needs precisely your best wisdom?

Your Self laughs at your I and its proud leaps. “What are these leaps and flights of thought to me?” it says to itself. “A detour to my purpose. I am the I’s leading-string and the prompter of its ideas.”

The Self says to the I: “Feel pain here!” And then it suffers and thinks how it may suffer no more; and that is just what it *is meant* to think.

The Self says to the I: “Feel pleasure here!” Then it rejoices and thinks how it may often rejoice again; and that is just what it *is meant* to think.

To the despisers of the body I want to say a word. That they despise is the result of their esteem. What is it that created esteem and contempt and value and will?

The creating Self created esteem and contempt for itself; it created pleasure and pain for itself. The creating body created spirit for itself as a hand of its will.

Even in your folly and contempt, you despisers of the body, you serve your Self. I tell you: your Self itself wants to die and turns away from life.

It can no longer do what it most wants to do: to create beyond itself. That is what it wants most; that is its whole fervour.

But now it has become too late for that: so your Self wants to go under, you despisers of the body.

Your Self wants to go under, and that is why you became despisers of the body! For you can no longer create beyond yourselves.

And that is why you are now angry with life and the earth. There is an unconscious envy in the sidelong glance of your contempt.

I do not go your way, you despisers of the body! You are no bridges to the Overman for me!

Thus spoke Zarathustra.

## On Joys and Passions

My brother, if you have a virtue, and it is your virtue, you have it in common with no one.

To be sure, you want to call it by name and caress it; you want to pull its ear and amuse yourself with it.

And look! Now you have its name in common with the people, and with your virtue you have become people and herd!

You would do better to say: “Unspeakable and nameless is what gives my soul torment and sweetness, and is even the hunger of my entrails.”

Let your virtue be too high for the familiarity of names: and if you must speak of it, do not be ashamed to stammer about it.

So speak and stammer: “This is *my* good; this I love; so it pleases me entirely; only so do I want the good.

“I do not want it as the law of a God; I do not want it as a human statute or necessity: let it be no signpost for me to worlds beyond and paradises.

“It is an earthly virtue that I love: there is little cleverness in it, and least of all the reason of everyone.

“But this bird built its nest with me: that is why I love it and hold it to my heart; now it sits with me on its golden eggs.”

So you shall stammer and praise your virtue.

Once you had passions and called them evil. But now you have only your virtues: they grew out of your passions.

You laid your highest goal on the heart of these passions: then they became your virtues and joys.

And whether you came from the race of the hot-tempered, or of the lustful, or of the fanatical, or of the vengeful:

In the end all your passions became virtues, and all your devils angels.

Once you had wild dogs in your cellar: but in the end they turned into birds and lovely singers.

Out of your poisons you brewed your balm; you milked your cow, Affliction: now you drink the sweet milk of her udder.

And nothing evil grows out of you any more, except the evil that grows out of the struggle of your virtues.

My brother, if you are lucky, you have one virtue and no more: then you go more easily over the bridge.

It is a distinction to have many virtues, but a hard lot; and many have gone into the desert and killed themselves because they were weary of being the battle and battlefield of virtues.

My brother, are war and battle evil? But this evil is necessary; necessary are envy and mistrust and slander among your virtues.

Look how each of your virtues craves the highest: it wants your whole spirit, so that it may be *its* herald; it wants your whole strength in anger, hatred and love.

Every virtue is jealous of the others, and jealousy is a terrible thing. Even virtues can perish of jealousy.

Whoever is encircled by the flame of jealousy will at last, like the scorpion, turn the poisoned sting against himself.

Ah, my brother, have you never seen a virtue slander and stab itself?

Man is something that must be overcome: and therefore you shall love your virtues, for you will perish of them.

Thus spoke Zarathustra.

## On the Pale Criminal

You do not want to kill, you judges and sacrificers, before the animal has nodded? Look, the pale criminal has nodded: out of his eye speaks the great contempt.

“My I is something that shall be overcome: my I is to me the great contempt of man”: so speaks that eye.

That he judged himself was his highest moment: do not let the exalted one sink back into his lowness!

There is no redemption for one who suffers so from himself, except a quick death.

Your killing, you judges, shall be pity and not revenge. And as you kill, see to it that you yourselves justify life!

It is not enough that you are reconciled with the one you kill. Let your sadness be love for the Overman: so you will justify your still-living!

“Enemy” you shall say, but not “villain”; “sick man” you shall say, but not “scoundrel”; “fool” you shall say, but not “sinner”.

And you, red judge, if you were to say aloud all that you have already done in your thoughts, everyone would cry: “Away with this filth and poisonous worm!”

But the thought is one thing, the deed another, and the image of the deed another again. The wheel of cause does not roll between them.

An image made this pale man pale. He was equal to his deed when he did it: but he could not bear its image once it was done.

From then on he always saw himself as the doer of one deed. I call this madness: the exception turned for him into his essence.

A chalk line spellbinds a hen; the blow he struck spellbound his poor reason: I call this the madness *after* the deed.

Listen, you judges! There is another madness besides: and it comes before the deed. Ah, you did not crawl deep enough into this soul!

So speaks the red judge: “Why did this criminal murder? He wanted to rob.” But I tell you: his soul wanted blood, not robbery: he thirsted for the happiness of the knife!

But his poor reason did not understand this madness, and persuaded him. “What does blood matter!” it said. “Don’t you want at least to make a robbery of it? To take revenge?”

And he listened to his poor reason: its words lay on him like lead; so he robbed when he murdered. He did not want to be ashamed of his madness.

And now the lead of his guilt lies on him again, and again his poor reason is so stiff, so paralysed, so heavy.

If only he could shake his head, his burden would roll off: but who will shake this head?

What is this man? A heap of diseases that reach out into the world through the spirit: there they want to take their prey.

What is this man? A tangle of wild snakes that are seldom at peace with one another: so they go off on their own and seek prey in the world.

Look at this poor body! What it suffered and craved, this poor soul interpreted for itself: it interpreted it as murderous lust and greed for the happiness of the knife.

Whoever falls ill now is overcome by the evil that is evil now: he wants to hurt with what hurts him. But there have been other times, and another evil and good.

Once doubt was evil, and the will to be oneself. Then the sick man became a heretic and a witch: as heretic and witch he suffered and wanted to make others suffer.

But this does not want to enter your ears: it harms your good people, you tell me. But what do your good people matter to me!

Much about your good people disgusts me, and truly not their evil. I wish they had a madness of which they would perish, like this pale criminal!

Truly, I wish their madness were called truth, or loyalty, or justice: but they have their virtue in order to live long, and in a wretched contentment.

I am a railing by the torrent: let whoever can grasp me, grasp me! But I am not your crutch.

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
