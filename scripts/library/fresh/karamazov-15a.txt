## Book Six, Chapter II, continued · (c) Recollections of Elder Zosima’s Youth and Young Manhood in the World · The Duel

I was a long time in Petersburg, in the Cadet Corps, nearly eight years, and with my new education I stifled many of my childhood impressions, though I forgot nothing. Instead I took on so many new habits and even opinions that I was transformed into a creature almost savage, cruel and absurd. Together with the French language I acquired a polish of courtesy and worldly manners; and the soldiers who served us in the Corps we all regarded as perfect cattle, and so did I. I perhaps more than anyone, for of all my comrades I was the most impressionable. When we came out as officers we were ready to shed our blood for the insulted honour of our regiment, but as for real honour, hardly any of us even knew what it was, and if he had found out, he would have been the first to laugh at it. We were all but proud of our drunkenness, our debauchery and our swagger. I will not say we were bad; all those young men were good, but they behaved badly, and I worst of all. The main thing was that I had come into my own capital, and so I threw myself into living for my pleasure, with all the impetuousness of youth, without restraint, under full sail. But here is the wonder of it: I read books then too, and even with great pleasure; the Bible alone I almost never opened at that time, yet I never parted with it either, but carried it everywhere with me: truly I was keeping that book, without knowing it, “for the day and the hour, the month and the year”. After serving in this way for about four years, I found myself at last in the town of K., where our regiment was stationed at the time. The town’s society was varied, numerous and merry, hospitable and rich, and I was well received everywhere, for I was cheerful by nature and besides was reputed to be not poor, which counts for no little in the world. And then something happened that was the beginning of everything. I became attached to a young and beautiful girl, intelligent and worthy, of a bright and noble character, the daughter of respectable parents. They were people of consequence, with wealth, influence and power, and they received me kindly and warmly. And it seemed to me that the girl was disposed towards me in her heart, and my heart was set aflame by the fancy. Afterwards I grasped for myself and fully realised that perhaps I did not love her with such force at all, but only honoured her mind and lofty character, as I could not fail to do. Self-love, however, prevented me from offering her my hand at the time: it seemed hard and frightening to part with the temptations of a dissolute, free bachelor’s life at so young an age, and with money too. I did, however, drop hints. At any rate, I put off every decisive step for a little while. And then suddenly I was sent on duty to another district for two months. I come back two months later and suddenly learn that the girl is already married, to a rich landowner from near the town, a man older than I but still young, who had connections in the capital and in the best society, which I had not, a very amiable man and, besides, educated, while I had no education at all. I was so struck by this unexpected event that my mind was even clouded. The main point was that, as I learned at the same time, this young landowner had long been her suitor, and I myself had met him many times in their house but noticed nothing, blinded by my own merits. But that was just what offended me most: how was it that almost everyone knew, and I alone knew nothing? And I suddenly felt an unbearable spite. With my face burning I began to recall how many times I had almost declared my love to her, and since she had not stopped me or warned me, it followed, I concluded, that she had been laughing at me. Later, of course, I realised and remembered that she had not been laughing at all; on the contrary, she herself had playfully broken off such conversations and started others in their place; but at the time I could not see it and burned for revenge. I remember with surprise that this revenge and my anger were extremely heavy and repugnant to me myself, because, having an easy temper, I could not stay angry with anyone for long, and so I kindled myself artificially, as it were, and became at last monstrous and absurd. I waited my time, and once, in a large company, I managed suddenly to insult my “rival”, as though over a quite unrelated matter, by laughing at an opinion of his about an event of importance at the time (it was in the year twenty-six), and laughed, people said, wittily and cleverly. Then I forced an explanation from him and behaved so rudely in it that he accepted my challenge, despite the enormous difference between us, for I was younger, of no importance and of low rank. I learned for certain afterwards that he accepted my challenge also from a feeling of jealousy towards me: he had been a little jealous of me before, over his wife, then still his betrothed; and now he thought that if she learned he had put up with an insult from me and had not dared to call me out, she might involuntarily despise him and her love might waver. I soon found a second, a comrade, a lieutenant of our regiment. Though duels were cruelly punished in those days, there was even a kind of fashion for them among the military, so savagely do prejudices sometimes grow and take root. It was the end of June, and our meeting was to be the next day, outside the town, at seven in the morning; and truly something fateful happened to me then. Coming home in the evening, ferocious and monstrous, I got angry with my orderly Afanasy and struck him twice in the face with all my strength, so that his face was covered with blood. He had not been with me long, and it had happened before that I struck him, but never with such bestial cruelty. And would you believe it, my dear ones, forty years have passed since then, and I still remember it with shame and torment. I went to bed, slept about three hours, and got up when the day was already beginning. I rose suddenly, did not want to sleep any more, went to the window and opened it (my window looked onto a garden); I see the sun rising; it is warm and beautiful; the birds have begun to sing. What is it, I think, that I feel in my soul, something shameful and base? Is it because I am going to shed blood? No, I think, it seems not that. Is it because I am afraid of death, afraid of being killed? No, not that at all, not that at all… And suddenly I guessed at once what it was: it was that I had beaten Afanasy the evening before! Everything suddenly came back to me as if it were happening again: he stands before me, and I strike him with all my force straight in the face, and he holds his hands at his sides, his head straight, his eyes staring as if on parade; he flinches at every blow and does not even dare raise his hands to shield himself. And a man has been brought to that, and a man is beating a man! What a crime! It was as if a sharp needle had gone right through my soul. I stand as if out of my mind, and the sun is shining, the little leaves are rejoicing and glittering, and the birds, the birds are praising God… I covered my face with both hands, fell on the bed and sobbed aloud. And then I remembered my brother Markel and his words to the servants before his death: “My dear ones, my precious ones, why do you wait on me, why do you love me, and am I worth being waited on?” “Yes, am I worth it?” suddenly leapt into my head. Indeed, what am I worth, that another man, just like me, the image and likeness of God, should serve me? That question pierced my mind then for the first time in my life. “Mother, my own heart’s blood, truly each is guilty before all for all, only people don’t know it, and if they knew it, there would be paradise at once!” “Lord, can that too be untrue?” I weep and think. “Truly, perhaps I am more guilty than all for all, and worse than all men in the world!” And suddenly the whole truth stood before me in all its light: what am I going to do? I am going to kill a good, intelligent, noble man, guilty of nothing towards me, and by that I shall deprive his wife of happiness for ever, torment her and kill her. I lay like that on the bed, face down in the pillow, and did not notice at all how the time went by. Suddenly my comrade the lieutenant comes in for me, with the pistols: “Ah,” he says, “good, you are up already; it’s time, let’s go.” I started rushing about and lost my head completely; still, we went out to get into the carriage. “Wait here a moment,” I say to him, “I’ll run back in a second, I’ve forgotten my purse.” And I ran back alone into the flat, straight to Afanasy’s little room: “Afanasy,” I say, “yesterday I struck you twice in the face; forgive me.” He started as if frightened and stared at me, and I see that it is not enough, not enough, and suddenly, just as I was, in my epaulettes, I threw myself at his feet with my forehead to the ground: “Forgive me!” I say. At that he was utterly dumbfounded: “Your honour, master, sir, how can you… am I worth…” and suddenly he began to cry himself, just as I had earlier; he covered his face with both hands, turned to the window and shook all over with tears; and I ran out to my comrade, leapt into the carriage and shouted, “Drive on!” “Have you ever seen a conqueror?” I shout to him. “There he is before you!” There is such rapture in me; I laugh, I talk, talk all the way; I no longer remember what I said. He looks at me: “Well, brother, you are a fine fellow; I can see you’ll do credit to the uniform.” So we arrived at the place, and they were already there, waiting for us. They placed us twelve paces apart; his was the first shot. I stand before him cheerful, face to face, without blinking an eye, looking at him lovingly; I know what I shall do. He fired, and the shot only grazed my cheek a little and caught my ear. “Thank God,” I shout, “no man has been killed!” And I seized my own pistol, turned round and flung it up and away into the wood: “That’s the place for you!” I shout. I turned to my adversary: “My dear sir,” I say, “forgive me, a foolish young man, for having offended you through my own fault and now making you fire at me. I am ten times worse than you, and perhaps more than that. Tell that to the person whom you honour above all in the world.” I had no sooner said it than all three of them began shouting. “Upon my word,” said my adversary, even getting angry, “if you did not want to fight, why did you trouble me?” “Yesterday,” I say to him, “I was still a fool, and today I have grown wiser,” I answer him cheerfully. “I believe you about yesterday,” he says, “but about today it is hard to judge from your opinion.” “Bravo,” I cry to him, clapping my hands, “I agree with you in that too; I deserved it!” “Will you fire, my dear sir, or not?” “I will not,” I say, “and you, if you like, may fire again, only it would be better for you not to.” The seconds shout too, mine especially: “How can you disgrace the regiment like this, asking forgiveness at the barrier; if only I had known!” Then I stood before them all and was no longer laughing: “Gentlemen,” I say, “is it really so astonishing in our time to meet a man who would repent of his own foolishness and own publicly to what he is guilty of?” “But not at the barrier,” my second shouts again. “That’s just it,” I answer them. “That is just what is astonishing, for I ought to have owned my fault as soon as we arrived here, even before his shot, and not led him into a great and mortal sin; but we have arranged ourselves in the world so monstrously,” I say, “that to act so was almost impossible, for only after I had stood his shot at twelve paces could my words mean anything to him now; whereas if I had said them before the shot, as soon as we arrived, they would simply have said: a coward, frightened of the pistol, no use listening to him. Gentlemen,” I suddenly cried from my whole heart, “look round at the gifts of God: the clear sky, the pure air, the tender grass, the birds; nature is beautiful and sinless, and we, only we, are godless and foolish and do not understand that life is paradise, for we have only to want to understand, and at once it will come in all its beauty, and we shall embrace one another and weep…” I wanted to go on, but I could not; my breath was taken away, so sweetly, so youthfully, and there was such happiness in my heart as I had never felt in all my life. “All this is very sensible and pious,” says my adversary, “and at any rate you are an original man.” “Laugh,” I laugh back at him, “but afterwards you will praise me yourself.” “Why, I am ready to praise you now,” he says. “Allow me, I will hold out my hand to you, for it seems you really are a sincere man.” “No,” I say, “not now; but later, when I have become better and earned your respect, then hold it out, and you will do well.” We went back home; my second swore all the way, and I kept kissing him. My comrades heard of it at once and gathered that same day to judge me: “He has soiled the uniform,” they said, “let him send in his papers.” There were defenders too: “He did stand the shot, though,” they said. “Yes, but he was afraid of more shots and asked forgiveness at the barrier.” “But if he had been afraid of the shots,” the defenders objected, “he would have fired his own pistol first before asking forgiveness, and he threw it into the wood still loaded. No, something else has happened here, something original.” I listen, looking at them cheerfully. “My dearest friends and comrades,” I say, “don’t worry about my sending in my papers, because I have done it already; I sent them in this very day at the office, in the morning; and when I get my discharge I shall go straight into a monastery; that is why I am sending in my papers.” As soon as I said it, every one of them burst out laughing: “You should have told us from the start! Well, now everything is explained; one can’t judge a monk.” They laughed and would not stop, and not mockingly at all, but kindly, merrily; all of them suddenly loved me, even my fiercest accusers, and all that month afterwards, until my discharge came through, they all but carried me in their arms: “Ah, you monk,” they would say. And each of them had a kind word for me; they began to try to talk me out of it, even to pity me: “What are you doing to yourself?” “No,” they would say, “he is brave, our man; he stood the shot and could have fired his own pistol; it’s that he had a dream the night before that he should become a monk; that’s why.” Almost exactly the same happened in the town’s society. Before, they had not taken much notice of me, only received me warmly; and now suddenly they all vied with one another to get to know me and invite me: they laughed at me themselves, and yet they loved me. I will note here that although everyone was talking openly about our duel at the time, the authorities hushed the matter up, for my adversary was a close relative of our general, and since the affair had ended without blood, as a kind of joke, and I had, after all, sent in my papers, they did indeed turn it into a joke. And then I began to speak aloud and without fear, in spite of their laughter, because their laughter was not spiteful but kind. All these conversations took place mostly in the evenings, in the company of ladies; the women liked to listen to me more then, and made the men listen too. “But how can it be that I am guilty for everyone?” each of them would laugh in my face. “Can I be guilty for you, for instance?” “How could you know it,” I answer them, “when the whole world has long since gone off on another road and we take a downright lie for the truth and demand the same lie from others? Here, for once in my life, I went and acted sincerely, and what happened? I became a kind of holy fool for you all: though you have come to love me, you still laugh at me,” I say. “But how could we not love you as you are?” the hostess laughs at me aloud, and there was a large gathering at her house. Suddenly I see, rising from among the ladies, that very young woman over whom I had issued the challenge and whom I had so recently meant for my bride; and I had not even noticed that she had come to the party. She rose, came up to me and held out her hand: “Allow me,” she says, “to tell you that I am the first not to laugh at you, but, on the contrary, I thank you with tears and declare my respect for you for what you did then.” Her husband came up then too, and then suddenly everyone came crowding round me and almost kissed me. I felt so joyful; but most of all I suddenly noticed then a certain gentleman, a man already elderly, who was also coming up to me, whom I knew by name, though I had never been acquainted with him and until that evening had never even exchanged a word with him.
