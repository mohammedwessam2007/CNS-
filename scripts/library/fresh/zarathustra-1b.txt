## 6

But then something happened that struck every mouth dumb and made every eye stare. For meanwhile the tightrope walker had begun his performance: he had stepped out of a little door and was walking along the rope, which was stretched between two towers so that it hung over the market place and the people. Just as he was in the middle of his way, the little door opened once more, and a fellow in motley, like a clown, sprang out and went with quick steps after the first. “Forward, lame-foot,” cried his terrible voice, “forward, sluggard, smuggler, pale-face! Or I’ll tickle you with my heel! What are you doing here between the towers? In the tower is where you belong; you should be locked up; you are blocking the way for one better than you!” And with every word he came nearer and nearer; but when he was only one step behind him, there happened the terrible thing that struck every mouth dumb and made every eye stare: he let out a yell like a devil and leapt over the man who stood in his way. But this man, seeing his rival triumph like this, lost his head and the rope; he threw away his pole, and, faster than the pole, like a whirl of arms and legs, he plunged into the depths. The market place and the people were like the sea when a storm drives into it: everyone fled apart and over one another, and most of all where the body was bound to come down.

But Zarathustra stood still, and the body fell right beside him, badly mangled and broken, but not yet dead. After a while consciousness returned to the shattered man, and he saw Zarathustra kneeling beside him. “What are you doing there?” he said at last. “I knew long ago that the devil would trip me up. Now he is dragging me off to hell: will you stop him?”

“On my honour, friend,” answered Zarathustra, “all that you speak of does not exist: there is no devil and no hell. Your soul will be dead even sooner than your body: so fear nothing any more!”

The man looked up mistrustfully. “If you speak the truth,” he said then, “I lose nothing when I lose my life. I am not much more than an animal that has been taught to dance by blows and scraps of food.”

“Not so,” said Zarathustra. “You made danger your calling; there is nothing contemptible in that. Now you perish by your calling: for that I will bury you with my own hands.”

When Zarathustra had said this, the dying man answered no more; but he moved his hand, as if he were seeking Zarathustra’s hand to thank him.

## 7

Meanwhile evening came, and the market place hid itself in darkness: then the people dispersed, for even curiosity and terror grow tired. But Zarathustra sat on the ground beside the dead man, sunk in thought, and so he forgot the time. At last, though, night came, and a cold wind blew over the lonely man. Then Zarathustra rose and said to his heart:

“Truly, Zarathustra has had a fine catch today! He caught no man, but a corpse.

“Human existence is uncanny and still without meaning: a clown can become its fate.

“I want to teach men the meaning of their being: which is the Overman, the lightning from the dark cloud of man.

“But I am still far from them, and my meaning does not speak to their senses. To men I am still something between a fool and a corpse.

“Dark is the night, dark are Zarathustra’s ways. Come, cold and stiff companion! I will carry you to where I can bury you with my own hands.”

## 8

When Zarathustra had said this to his heart, he loaded the corpse on his back and set off. And he had not gone a hundred paces when a man crept up to him and whispered in his ear, and look! The one who spoke was the clown from the tower. “Leave this town, O Zarathustra,” he said; “too many people here hate you. The good and the just hate you, and call you their enemy and despiser; the believers of the true faith hate you, and call you a danger to the crowd. It was your luck that they laughed at you: and truly, you talked like a clown. It was your luck that you joined yourself to the dead dog; by humbling yourself like that, you saved yourself for today. But leave this town, or tomorrow I shall leap over you, a living man over a dead one.” And when he had said this, the man vanished; but Zarathustra went on through the dark streets.

At the town gate he met the gravediggers: they shone their torch in his face, recognised Zarathustra, and mocked him a great deal. “Zarathustra is carrying off the dead dog: fine, that Zarathustra has become a gravedigger! For our hands are too clean for this roast. Does Zarathustra want to steal the devil’s morsel from him? Well then! Good luck with your meal! As long as the devil is not a better thief than Zarathustra! He’ll steal them both, he’ll eat them both!” And they laughed together and put their heads together.

Zarathustra said not a word to this and went on his way. When he had walked for two hours, past forests and swamps, he had heard too much of the hungry howling of wolves, and he grew hungry himself. So he stopped at a lonely house in which a light was burning.

“Hunger falls upon me like a robber,” said Zarathustra. “In forests and swamps my hunger falls upon me, and in the deep of night.

“My hunger has strange moods. Often it comes to me only after a meal, and today it did not come all day: where can it have been?”

And with that Zarathustra knocked at the door of the house. An old man appeared; he carried the light and asked: “Who comes to me and to my bad sleep?”

“A living man and a dead one,” said Zarathustra. “Give me something to eat and drink; I forgot to during the day. He who feeds the hungry refreshes his own soul: so says wisdom.”

The old man went away, but came back at once and offered Zarathustra bread and wine. “This is a bad region for the hungry,” he said; “that is why I live here. Beast and man come to me, the hermit. But tell your companion to eat and drink too; he is more tired than you.” Zarathustra answered: “My companion is dead; I shall hardly persuade him.” “That is no business of mine,” said the old man sulkily; “whoever knocks at my door must take what I offer him. Eat, and fare you well!”

Then Zarathustra walked for another two hours, trusting to the road and the light of the stars: for he was used to walking at night and liked to look into the face of all that sleeps. But when the morning grew grey, Zarathustra found himself in a deep forest, and no path showed itself to him any more. Then he laid the dead man in a hollow tree at his head, for he wanted to protect him from the wolves, and laid himself down on the ground and the moss. And at once he fell asleep, tired in body but with an untroubled soul.

## 9

Zarathustra slept long, and not only the dawn passed over his face, but the morning too. But at last his eyes opened: in wonder Zarathustra looked into the forest and the stillness; in wonder he looked into himself. Then he rose quickly, like a sailor who suddenly sees land, and shouted for joy: for he saw a new truth. And he spoke thus to his heart:

“A light has dawned on me: I need companions, and living ones; not dead companions and corpses that I carry with me wherever I go.

“But living companions I need, who follow me because they want to follow themselves, and to go where I want to go.

“A light has dawned on me: let Zarathustra speak not to the people, but to companions! Zarathustra shall not become the shepherd and the dog of a herd!

“To lure many away from the herd: that is why I came. The people and the herd shall be angry with me: Zarathustra wants to be called a robber by the shepherds.

“Shepherds, I say; but they call themselves the good and the just. Shepherds, I say; but they call themselves the believers of the true faith.

“Look at the good and the just! Whom do they hate most? The one who breaks their tablets of values, the breaker, the lawbreaker: but he is the creator.

“Look at the believers of every faith! Whom do they hate most? The one who breaks their tablets of values, the breaker, the lawbreaker: but he is the creator.

“The creator seeks companions, not corpses, and not herds or believers either. The creator seeks fellow creators, those who write new values on new tablets.

“The creator seeks companions, and fellow harvesters: for everything with him stands ripe for harvest. But he lacks the hundred sickles: so he tears off the ears of corn and is angry.

“The creator seeks companions, and such as know how to whet their sickles. They will be called destroyers and despisers of good and evil. But they are the harvesters and the celebrants.

“Zarathustra seeks fellow creators, fellow harvesters and fellow celebrants: what has he to do with herds and shepherds and corpses!

“And you, my first companion, farewell! I buried you well in your hollow tree; I hid you well from the wolves.

“But I am leaving you; the time is up. Between dawn and dawn a new truth came to me.

“I am not to be a shepherd, nor a gravedigger. Never again will I speak to the people; for the last time I have spoken to a dead man.

“I will join the creators, the harvesters, the celebrants: I will show them the rainbow and all the steps to the Overman.

“I will sing my song to the hermits, alone and in pairs; and whoever still has ears for the unheard-of, I will make his heart heavy with my happiness.

“I want to reach my goal; I go my way; I shall leap over the hesitant and the dawdlers. So let my going be their going-under!”

## 10

Zarathustra had said this to his heart when the sun stood at noon: then he looked up questioningly, for above him he heard the sharp cry of a bird. And look! An eagle was sweeping through the air in wide circles, and on it hung a serpent, not like prey but like a friend: for she was coiled around his neck.

“They are my animals!” said Zarathustra, and rejoiced in his heart.

“The proudest animal under the sun and the wisest animal under the sun: they have gone out to scout.

“They want to find out whether Zarathustra is still alive. Truly, am I still alive?

“I found it more dangerous among men than among animals; dangerous are the ways Zarathustra goes. May my animals lead me!”

When Zarathustra had said this, he remembered the words of the saint in the forest, sighed, and spoke thus to his heart:

“Would that I were wiser! Would that I were wise from the ground up, like my serpent!

“But I am asking for the impossible: so I ask my pride instead always to go with my wisdom!

“And if one day my wisdom leaves me (ah, it loves to fly away!), may my pride then fly with my folly!”

Thus began Zarathustra’s going-under.
