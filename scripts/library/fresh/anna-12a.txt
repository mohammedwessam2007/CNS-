## Part Three · Chapter II

Early in June it happened that the nurse and housekeeper, Agafya Mikhailovna, was carrying a jar of mushrooms she had just salted down to the cellar, slipped, fell and sprained her wrist. The zemstvo doctor came, a talkative young man, a student who had only just finished his course. He examined the hand, said it was not dislocated, put on compresses, and, staying to dinner, evidently enjoyed his conversation with the famous Sergei Ivanovich Koznyshev, and, to show his enlightened view of things, told him all the gossip of the district, complaining of the bad state of the zemstvo’s affairs. Sergei Ivanovich listened attentively, asked questions, and, stimulated by a new listener, began to talk and made several apt and weighty remarks, respectfully appreciated by the young doctor, and came into that animated state of mind, familiar to his brother, into which he usually came after a brilliant and lively conversation. After the doctor had gone, Sergei Ivanovich wanted to go to the river with a fishing-rod. He loved fishing, and seemed proud of being able to love such a stupid occupation.

Konstantin Levin, who had to go to the ploughland and the meadows, offered to drive his brother in the trap.

It was that time of year, the turn of the summer, when this year’s harvest has already been determined, when the cares about sowing for next year begin and the mowing has come round; when the rye has all come into ear and, grey-green, waves its light ears, not yet filled out, in the wind; when the green oats, with clumps of yellow grass scattered through them, shoot up unevenly on the late-sown fields; when the early buckwheat is already spreading its leaves and hiding the ground; when the fallow fields, trodden hard as stone by the cattle, are ploughed half-way, with tracks left that the plough will not take; when the dried heaps of manure carted out to the fields smell at dawn together with the honey-scented grasses, and in the low places, waiting for the scythe, the meadows that have been kept stand like a solid sea, with blackening heaps of the stalks of weeded sorrel.

It was the time when in the work of the country there comes a short breathing-space before the beginning of the harvest, which recurs every year and every year calls on all the strength of the people. The crops were splendid, and there were clear, hot summer days with short, dewy nights.

The brothers had to drive through the wood to get to the meadows. Sergei Ivanovich admired all the time the beauty of the wood, overgrown with leaves, pointing out to his brother now an old lime tree getting ready to flower, dark on its shady side and dappled with yellow stipules, now the young shoots of this year’s trees, shining like emeralds. Konstantin Levin did not like talking or hearing about the beauties of nature. Words took away for him the beauty of what he saw. He agreed with his brother, but without meaning to began thinking of something else. When they had come out of the wood, all his attention was swallowed up by the sight of a fallow field on a rise, yellow with grass in one place, in another trodden and cut up into squares, in another dotted with heaps, and in another ploughed. A line of carts was moving over the field. Levin counted the carts and was pleased that everything needed would be carted out; and at the sight of the meadows his thoughts went on to the question of the mowing. He always felt something that took particular hold of him in the haymaking. When they reached the meadow, Levin stopped the horse.

The morning dew still lay on the thick undergrowth of the grass at the bottom, and Sergei Ivanovich, so as not to get his feet wet, asked to be driven across the meadow in the trap to the willow bush where the perch were biting. Sorry as Konstantin Levin was to trample his grass, he drove into the meadow. The tall grass twined softly round the wheels and the horse’s legs, leaving its seeds on the wet spokes and hubs.

His brother sat down under the bush, sorting out his rods, while Levin led the horse away, tied it up, and walked into the vast grey-green sea of the meadow, unstirred by the wind. The silky grass with its ripening seeds came up to his waist in the flooded part.

Crossing the meadow, Konstantin Levin came out onto the road and met an old man with a swollen eye, carrying a swarm of bees in a basket.

“What? Have you caught one, Fomich?” he asked.

“Caught one, Konstantin Mitrich! If only I could keep my own. This is the second time the second swarm has gone off… Thank heaven, the lads galloped after it. They are ploughing on your land. They unharnessed a horse and galloped after…”

“Well, what do you say, Fomich: should we mow, or wait?”

“Well, now! The way we do it, we wait till St Peter’s day. But you always mow earlier. Well, God willing, the grass is good. There will be plenty of room for the cattle.”

“And the weather, what do you think?”

“That’s God’s business. Perhaps the weather will hold too.”

Levin went up to his brother. Nothing was biting, but Sergei Ivanovich was not bored and seemed in the most cheerful mood. Levin saw that, stirred up by his conversation with the doctor, he wanted to talk. Levin, on the contrary, wanted to get home quickly, to give orders about bringing in the mowers for the next day and to settle a doubt about the mowing which very much occupied him.

“Well, shall we go?” he said.

“Where’s the hurry? Let’s sit a while. How wet you are, though! Even if nothing bites, it’s good. Every kind of sport is good because one has to do with nature. Well, how lovely this steel-coloured water is!” he said. “These meadow banks always remind me of a riddle, you know? The grass says to the water: we shall sway and sway.”

“I don’t know that riddle,” Levin answered dejectedly.

## Chapter III

“And do you know, I have been thinking about you,” said Sergei Ivanovich. “What goes on in your district is beyond anything, from what this doctor has been telling me; he is quite a bright young fellow. And I have told you and I tell you again: it is wrong that you don’t go to the meetings and have altogether withdrawn from zemstvo business. If decent people keep away, of course everything will go God knows how. We pay money, it goes in salaries, and there are no schools, no medical assistants, no midwives, no chemists, nothing.”

“But I tried, you know,” Levin answered quietly and reluctantly. “I can’t! Well, what can I do!”

“But what is it you can’t do? I confess I don’t understand. Indifference, incapacity, I don’t admit; is it really simply laziness?”

“Neither the one, nor the other, nor the third. I tried, and I see that I can do nothing,” said Levin.

He was paying little attention to what his brother was saying. Looking across the river at the ploughland, he made out something black, but could not tell whether it was a horse or the steward on horseback.

“But why can you do nothing? You made one attempt, and it did not turn out as you thought, and you give in. How can you have no self-respect?”

“Self-respect,” said Levin, stung by his brother’s words, “I don’t understand. If at the university I had been told that others understood the integral calculus and I didn’t, that would be a matter of self-respect. But here one must first be convinced that one has to have certain abilities for these things and, above all, that all these things are very important.”

“Well, what! Is it not important?” said Sergei Ivanovich, stung too, both because his brother found unimportant what occupied him and, especially, because he was evidently hardly listening to him.

“It doesn’t seem important to me, it doesn’t take hold of me; what do you want?…” answered Levin, making out that what he saw was the steward, and that the steward had probably let the peasants off from ploughing. They were turning their ploughs over. “Have they really finished ploughing already?” he thought.

“Well, but listen,” said his brother, frowning his handsome, intelligent face, “there are limits to everything. It is very good to be an eccentric and a sincere man and to dislike falseness; I know all that; but really, what you are saying either has no sense or has a very bad sense. How can you find it unimportant that the people you love, as you assure us…”

“I never assured anyone,” thought Konstantin Levin.

“…are dying without help? Ignorant old midwives kill the babies, and the people stagnate in ignorance and remain in the power of every clerk, while you have been given the means to help them; and you don’t help, because it is not important.”

And Sergei Ivanovich put a dilemma to him: either you are so undeveloped that you cannot see all that you can do, or you do not want to give up your peace, your vanity, I don’t know what, in order to do it.

Konstantin Levin felt that nothing was left for him but to give in or to confess to a lack of love for the common good. And this offended and grieved him.

“Both,” he said decidedly. “I don’t see that it is possible…”

“What? It is not possible, by placing the money well, to provide medical help?”

“Not possible, it seems to me… Over the four thousand square versts of our district, with our thaws, snowstorms and working seasons, I don’t see any possibility of providing medical help everywhere. And in general I don’t believe in medicine.”

“Well, excuse me; that is unjust… I could name you thousands of examples… Well, and schools?”

“What are schools for?”

“What are you saying? Can there be any doubt about the benefit of education? If it is good for you, it is good for everyone.”

Konstantin Levin felt himself morally driven into a corner, and so he grew heated and without meaning to spoke out the chief reason for his indifference to the common good.

“Perhaps all that is good; but why should I worry about setting up medical stations which I shall never use, and schools to which I shall not send my children, to which the peasants don’t want to send their children either, and I am not even firmly convinced that they ought to send them?” he said.

Sergei Ivanovich was surprised for a moment by this unexpected view of the matter; but at once he made a new plan of attack.

He was silent for a while, took out one of his rods, cast it again, and, smiling, turned to his brother.

“Well, excuse me… In the first place, the medical station has turned out to be needed. Here we sent for the zemstvo doctor for Agafya Mikhailovna.”

“Well, I think her hand will stay crooked.”

“That’s still a question… Then, a literate peasant is more useful and more valuable to you as a worker.”

“No, ask anyone you like,” answered Konstantin Levin decidedly, “a literate man is much worse as a worker. And the roads can’t be mended; and as soon as they put up bridges, they steal them.”

“However,” said Sergei Ivanovich, frowning, for he disliked contradiction, and especially the kind that kept jumping from one thing to another and brought in new arguments without any connection, so that one could not tell what to answer, “however, that is not the point. Excuse me. Do you admit that education is a blessing for the people?”

“I do,” said Levin inadvertently, and at once thought that he had said not what he thought. He felt that if he admitted this, it would be proved to him that he was talking nonsense that had no sense at all. How it would be proved to him he did not know, but he knew that it would undoubtedly be proved to him logically, and he waited for the proof.

The argument turned out much simpler than Konstantin Levin had expected.

“If you admit it is a blessing,” said Sergei Ivanovich, “then you, as an honest man, cannot help loving and sympathizing with such a cause, and so wishing to work for it.”

“But I don’t yet admit that cause to be a good one,” said Konstantin Levin, blushing.

“What? But you said just now…”

“That is, I don’t admit it to be either good or possible.”

“That you cannot know without making efforts.”

“Well, let us suppose,” said Levin, though he did not suppose anything of the kind, “let us suppose it is so; but still I don’t see why I should worry about it.”

“How do you mean?”

“No, since we have got onto this, explain it to me from the philosophical point of view,” said Levin.

“I don’t see what philosophy has to do with it,” said Sergei Ivanovich, in a tone, as it seemed to Levin, as though he did not admit his brother’s right to reason about philosophy. And this irritated Levin.

“This is what it has to do with it!” he began, getting heated. “I think that the motive of all our actions is, after all, personal happiness. Now in the zemstvo institutions I, as a nobleman, see nothing that would contribute to my well-being. The roads are no better and can’t be better; my horses carry me over bad ones too. I don’t need a doctor or a medical station. I don’t need a justice of the peace; I never go to him and never shall. Schools I not only don’t need, they are even harmful, as I told you. For me the zemstvo institutions are simply the duty of paying eighteen kopecks a desyatina, of going to town, spending the night with bedbugs and listening to all sorts of nonsense and vileness; and personal interest does not move me.”

“Excuse me,” Sergei Ivanovich interrupted with a smile, “personal interest did not move us to work for the emancipation of the peasants, and yet we worked.”

“No!” Konstantin interrupted, growing more and more heated. “The emancipation of the peasants was another matter. There was personal interest in that. We wanted to throw off the yoke that pressed on us, on all decent people. But to be a member of a council and discuss how many night-soil men are needed and how the pipes are to be laid in a town I don’t live in; to be a juror and try a peasant who has stolen a ham, and listen for six hours to all the nonsense the defenders and prosecutors grind out, and how the presiding judge asks my old Alyoshka the simpleton: ‘Do you, the accused, admit the fact of the theft of the ham?’ ‘Eh?’”

Konstantin Levin had already got carried away and begun mimicking the judge and Alyoshka the simpleton; it seemed to him that all this was to the point.

But Sergei Ivanovich shrugged his shoulders.

“Well, what do you mean to say?”

“I only mean to say that the rights that touch me… my interest, I shall always defend with all my strength; that when they searched us students and the gendarmes read our letters, I was ready to defend those rights with all my strength, to defend my rights to education, to freedom. I understand military service, which touches the fate of my children, my brothers and myself; I am ready to discuss what concerns me; but to judge how forty thousand roubles of zemstvo money are to be distributed, or to judge Alyoshka the simpleton, I don’t understand and I can’t.”

Konstantin Levin was talking as though the dam of his words had burst. Sergei Ivanovich smiled.

“And if tomorrow you are put on trial yourself, would you prefer to be tried in the old criminal court?”

“I shan’t be put on trial. I shan’t cut anyone’s throat, and I don’t need it. Well, really!” he went on, again jumping to something quite beside the point, “our institutions and all that are like the little birch trees we stick into the ground, as on Trinity day, to make it look like a forest that grew by itself in Europe; and I cannot water those little birches and believe in them with my whole heart!”

Sergei Ivanovich only shrugged his shoulders, expressing by this gesture his surprise at where these little birches had come from in their argument, though he at once understood what his brother meant by them.

“Excuse me, one can’t reason like that, you know,” he remarked.

But Konstantin Levin wanted to justify himself for the failing he knew he had, indifference to the common good, and he went on.

“I think,” said Konstantin, “that no activity can be lasting if it has no foundation in personal interest. That is a general truth, a philosophical one,” he said, repeating the word philosophical with decision, as if wishing to show that he too had the right, like anyone else, to speak of philosophy.

Sergei Ivanovich smiled once more. “He too has some philosophy of his own there, in the service of his inclinations,” he thought.

“Well, you had better leave philosophy alone,” he said. “The chief task of the philosophy of all ages consists precisely in finding the necessary connection that exists between personal interest and the common interest. But that is beside the point; the point is that I only need to correct your comparison. The little birches are not stuck in; some have been planted and some sown, and they must be treated more carefully. Only those peoples have a future, only those peoples can be called historic, which have a feeling for what is important and significant in their institutions, and value them.”

And Sergei Ivanovich carried the question into the region of the philosophy of history, out of Konstantin Levin’s reach, and showed him all the injustice of his view.

“As for your not liking it, excuse me, that is our Russian laziness and lordliness, and I am sure that with you it is a temporary error and will pass.”

Konstantin was silent. He felt that he was beaten on all sides, but at the same time he felt that what he had wanted to say had not been understood by his brother. Only he did not know why it had not been understood: whether because he had not been able to say clearly what he meant, or because his brother did not want to understand him, or because he could not. But he did not go deeper into these thoughts, and, without arguing with his brother, began thinking about something quite different, a personal matter of his own.

“Well, let us go, though.”

Sergei Ivanovich wound in his last line, Konstantin untied the horse, and they drove off.

## Chapter IV

The personal matter that had occupied Levin during his conversation with his brother was this: the year before, coming once to the mowing and getting angry with the steward, Levin had used his own means of calming himself: he had taken a scythe from a peasant and begun to mow.

He had liked the work so much that he had taken it up several times; he had mowed the whole meadow in front of the house, and this year, from the very beginning of spring, he had made himself a plan: to mow with the peasants for whole days. Since his brother’s arrival he had been in two minds: to mow or not? He felt awkward about leaving his brother alone for whole days, and he was afraid his brother would laugh at him for it. But, walking across the meadow and remembering the impressions of mowing, he had almost decided that he would mow. And after the irritating conversation with his brother he remembered this intention again.

“I need physical exercise, or my character will be decidedly spoiled,” he thought, and he decided to mow, however awkward it might be for him before his brother and the people.

In the evening Konstantin Levin went to the office, gave orders about the work, and sent round to the villages to call in mowers for the next day, to mow Kalinov meadow, the biggest and best.

“And please send my scythe to Tit, so that he can whet it and bring it out tomorrow; I may do some mowing myself too,” he said, trying not to be embarrassed.

The steward smiled and said:

“Very good, sir.”

In the evening at tea Levin told his brother too.

“The weather seems to have settled,” he said. “Tomorrow I start mowing.”

“I am very fond of that work,” said Sergei Ivanovich.

“I am terribly fond of it. I have sometimes mowed with the peasants myself, and tomorrow I want to mow the whole day.”

Sergei Ivanovich raised his head and looked at his brother with curiosity.

“How do you mean? On a level with the peasants, the whole day?”

“Yes, it is very pleasant,” said Levin.

“It is excellent as physical exercise, only will you be able to stand it?” said Sergei Ivanovich, without any mockery.

“I have tried it. It is hard at first, then you get into it. I don’t think I shall lag behind…”

“Really! But tell me, how do the peasants look at it? They must laugh up their sleeves at the master’s whims.”

“No, I don’t think so; but it is such cheerful and at the same time such hard work that there is no time to think.”

“Well, and how will you dine with them? It would be awkward to send you claret and a roast turkey out there.”

“No, I shall just come home at the same time as their rest.”

The next morning Konstantin Levin got up earlier than usual, but orders about the estate held him up, and when he reached the mowing the mowers were already on their second row.

From the top of the hill there opened up before him, below, the shady part of the meadow, already mown, with its greyish rows and the black heaps of coats the mowers had taken off at the place where they had started their first row.

As he rode closer, there came into view the peasants, moving one behind another in a long, drawn-out line and swinging their scythes in different ways, some in coats, some in their shirts only. He counted forty-two of them.

They were moving slowly over the uneven bottom of the meadow, where there had been an old dam. Levin recognized some of his own people. There was old Yermil in a very long white shirt, swinging his scythe, bent over; there was the young fellow Vaska, who had been Levin’s coachman, taking every row with a full sweep. There too was Tit, Levin’s tutor in mowing, a small, skinny little peasant. He was walking at the front without bending, as if playing with his scythe, cutting down his broad row.

Levin got off his horse and, tying it up by the road, went up to Tit, who took a second scythe out of a bush and handed it to him.

“It’s ready, master; it shaves, it mows by itself,” said Tit, taking off his cap with a smile and handing him the scythe.

Levin took the scythe and began to try it. The mowers who had finished their rows, sweating and cheerful, came out one after another onto the road and greeted the master, chuckling. They were all looking at him, but no one said anything until a tall old man with a wrinkled, beardless face, in a sheepskin jacket, who had come out onto the road, spoke to him.

“Mind, master, you’ve put your hand to the plough, don’t fall behind!” he said, and Levin heard suppressed laughter among the mowers.

“I’ll try not to fall behind,” he said, taking his place behind Tit and waiting for the time to begin.

“Mind now,” the old man repeated.

Tit made room, and Levin went after him. The grass was short, by the road, and Levin, who had not mowed for a long time and was embarrassed by the eyes turned on him, mowed badly in the first minutes, though he swung hard. Behind him voices were heard:

“It’s badly set; the handle’s too high; see how he has to stoop,” said one.

“Press more on the heel,” said another.

“Never mind, it’s all right, he’ll get the knack,” the old man went on. “See, he’s off… You take too wide a row, you’ll wear yourself out… He’s the master, he can’t help it, he’s working for himself! But look at the bits he’s leaving! For that the likes of us used to get it across the back, in the old days.”

The grass grew softer, and Levin, listening but not answering, and trying to mow as well as possible, walked behind Tit. They had gone a hundred paces or so; Tit kept going without stopping, not showing the slightest fatigue; but Levin was already beginning to be terribly afraid that he would not hold out: he was so tired.

He felt that he was swinging with the last of his strength, and he decided to ask Tit to stop. But at that very moment Tit stopped of his own accord, and, bending down, took up some grass, wiped the scythe, and began to whet it. Levin straightened up and, sighing, looked round. Behind him came a peasant, and he too was evidently tired, because he stopped at once, before reaching Levin, and began whetting his scythe. Tit whetted his own scythe and Levin’s, and they went on.

The second time it was the same. Tit went on stroke after stroke without stopping and without getting tired. Levin went after him, trying not to fall behind, and it became harder and harder for him: the moment would come when he felt that he had no strength left, but at that very moment Tit would stop and whet.

So they finished the first row. And that long row seemed especially hard to Levin; but then, when the row was finished and Tit, swinging his scythe onto his shoulder, walked back with slow steps over the tracks left by his heels in the mown strip, and Levin walked back the same way over his own strip, in spite of the sweat that ran down his face in streams and dripped off his nose, and his whole back being as wet as if it had been soaked in water, he felt very good. What gladdened him especially was that he knew now that he would hold out.

His pleasure was poisoned only by the fact that his row was not good. “I’ll swing my arm less and my whole body more,” he thought, comparing Tit’s row, cut as straight as by a thread, with his own, which lay scattered and uneven.

The first row, as Levin noticed, Tit had gone especially fast, probably wanting to test the master, and the row happened to be long. The next rows were easier, but still Levin had to strain all his strength not to fall behind the peasants.

He thought of nothing, wished for nothing, except not to fall behind the peasants and to do the work as well as possible. He heard only the clang of the scythes and saw before him Tit’s upright figure moving away, the curved half-circle of the mown strip, the grass and the heads of flowers bending slowly and in waves round the blade of his scythe, and ahead of him the end of the row, where rest would come.

Not understanding what it was or where it came from, in the middle of the work he suddenly felt a pleasant sensation of cold on his hot, sweating shoulders. He glanced at the sky while whetting his scythe. A low, heavy cloud had come up, and big drops of rain were falling. Some of the peasants went to their coats and put them on; others, like Levin, only shrugged their shoulders joyfully under the pleasant freshening.

They went another row, and another. They went long rows and short rows, rows with good grass and with bad. Levin lost all sense of time and decidedly did not know whether it was late or early. A change was now beginning to take place in his work that gave him enormous enjoyment. In the midst of his work there came over him moments during which he forgot what he was doing; it became easy for him, and in those very moments his row came out almost as even and good as Tit’s. But as soon as he remembered what he was doing and began trying to do it better, at once he felt the whole weight of the labour, and his row came out badly.

After going one more row, he wanted to start again, but Tit stopped and, going up to the old man, said something to him quietly. They both looked at the sun. “What are they talking about, and why doesn’t he start the row?” thought Levin, not guessing that the peasants had been mowing without stopping for no less than four hours and that it was time for their breakfast.

“Breakfast, master,” said the old man.

“Is it time? Well, breakfast then.”

Levin gave his scythe to Tit and, with the peasants who were going to their coats for bread, walked to his horse across the rows of the long mown space, lightly sprinkled with rain. Only now did he understand that he had not guessed the weather right and that the rain was wetting his hay.

“It will spoil the hay,” he said.

“Never mind, master: mow in the rain, rake in the sun!” said the old man.

Levin untied his horse and rode home for coffee.

Sergei Ivanovich had only just got up. Having drunk his coffee, Levin rode off again to the mowing before Sergei Ivanovich had had time to dress and come into the dining-room.

## Chapter V

After breakfast Levin did not get his old place in the line, but found himself between the joking old man, who invited him to be his neighbour, and a young peasant who had only been married since the autumn and was mowing his first summer.

The old man, holding himself straight, went in front, moving his turned-out feet evenly and widely, and with a precise and even movement, which seemed to cost him no more effort than swinging his arms as he walked, laid down, as if in play, an even, high row. It was as though not he, but the sharp scythe by itself, was swishing through the juicy grass.

Behind Levin came young Mishka. His pleasant young face, with a twist of fresh grass tied round his hair, was working all over with effort; but whenever anyone looked at him he smiled. He was evidently ready to die rather than admit that it was hard for him.

Levin went between them. In the very heat the mowing seemed to him not so hard. The sweat that drenched him cooled him, and the sun that burned his back, his head and his arm, bare to the elbow, gave him strength and persistence in the work; and more and more often there came those moments of unconsciousness when it was possible not to think of what one was doing. The scythe cut by itself. These were happy moments. Still more joyful were the moments when, coming up to the river, where the rows ended, the old man wiped his scythe with the wet, thick grass, rinsed its steel in the fresh river water, scooped some up in his whetstone-box and offered it to Levin.

“Have some of my kvass! Good, eh?” he would say, winking.

And indeed Levin had never drunk any drink like that warm water with green stuff floating in it and a rusty taste from the tin box. And right after that came the blissful slow walk, with a hand on the scythe, during which one could wipe off the streaming sweat, breathe deeply, and look over the whole long line of mowers and what was going on round about, in the wood and in the field.

The longer Levin mowed, the more often he felt those moments of oblivion when it was no longer his arms that swung the scythe, but the scythe itself that moved after it his whole body, full of life and aware of itself; and, as if by magic, without a thought about it, the work did itself, right and precise. These were the most blissful moments.

It was hard only when he had to stop this movement, which had become unconscious, and think: when he had to mow round a hummock or a clump of sorrel that had not been weeded. The old man did this easily. A hummock would come, he would change his movement and, now with the heel, now with the tip of the scythe, cut round the hummock on both sides with short little strokes. And as he did this he kept looking at and noticing what opened up before him: now he would pick a sorrel stalk and eat it or offer it to Levin; now toss aside a twig with the tip of his scythe; now look at a quail’s nest, from which the hen flew up from right under the scythe; now catch a snake that came in his way and, lifting it on the scythe as on a fork, show it to Levin and throw it away.

For Levin and for the young fellow behind him these changes of movement were hard. Both of them, having settled into one strained movement, were in the fever of the work and had not the power to change the movement and at the same time to watch what lay before them.

Levin did not notice how the time passed. If he had been asked how long he had been mowing, he would have said half an hour; and it was already nearly dinner time. As they were going back to start a row, the old man drew Levin’s attention to the little girls and boys who were coming towards the mowers from different directions, barely visible, through the tall grass and along the road, carrying bundles of bread that pulled down their little arms, and little jugs of kvass stoppered with rags.

“Look, the little beetles are crawling!” he said, pointing at them, and he looked at the sun from under his hand.

They went two more rows, and the old man stopped.

“Well, master, dinner!” he said decidedly. And, reaching the river, the mowers went across the rows to their coats, where the children who had brought the dinners were sitting waiting for them. The peasants gathered, those from far off under the carts, those nearer under a willow bush, on which they had heaped grass.

Levin sat down with them; he did not want to go away.

All constraint before the master had long since vanished. The peasants were getting ready to dine. Some were washing; the young lads were bathing in the river; others were arranging a place to rest, untying their little bags of bread and unstopping their jugs of kvass. The old man crumbled bread into a bowl, mashed it with the handle of a spoon, poured water on it from his whetstone-box, cut more bread, sprinkled it with salt, and turned to the east to pray.

“Here, master, have some of my bread-soup,” he said, kneeling down in front of the bowl.

The bread-soup was so good that Levin gave up the idea of going home to dinner. He dined with the old man and got talking with him about his household affairs, taking the liveliest interest in them, and told him all about his own affairs and all the circumstances that might interest the old man. He felt himself closer to him than to his brother, and could not help smiling from the tenderness he felt for this man. When the old man got up again, prayed, and lay down right there under the bush, putting some grass under his head, Levin did the same and, in spite of the flies and beetles, sticky and persistent in the sun, tickling his sweaty face and body, he fell asleep at once, and woke only when the sun had moved round to the other side of the bush and began to reach him. The old man had long been awake and was sitting whetting the young lads’ scythes.

Levin looked round him and did not recognize the place: everything had changed so much. A huge expanse of meadow had been mown and shone with a special, new brightness, with its already fragrant rows, in the slanting rays of the evening sun. And the mown bushes by the river, and the river itself, not seen before and now shining like steel in its windings, and the people moving and getting up, and the steep wall of grass in the unmown part of the meadow, and the hawks circling over the bared meadow: all this was completely new. Coming to himself, Levin began reckoning how much had been mown and how much more could be done that day.

An extraordinary amount had been done for forty-two men. The whole big meadow, which used to take thirty scythes two days in the days of serfdom, was already mown. Only the corners with short rows were left unmown. But Levin wanted to mow as much as possible that day, and he was vexed with the sun for going down so fast. He felt no fatigue at all; he only wanted to do more and more work, and faster.

“What do you think, shall we mow Mashkin Upland too?” he said to the old man.

“As God wills; the sun’s not high. What about a drop of vodka for the lads?”

At the afternoon break, when they sat down again and those who smoked lit up, the old man announced to the lads: “Mow Mashkin Upland and there’ll be vodka.”

“What, not mow it! Start off, Tit! We’ll knock it off in no time! You can eat at night. Start off!” voices were heard, and, finishing their bread, the mowers went to start.

“Well, lads, keep up!” said Tit, and went off in front almost at a trot.

“Go on, go on!” said the old man, hurrying after him and easily catching up. “I’ll cut you down! Look out!”

And young and old mowed as if racing each other. But however they hurried, they did not spoil the grass, and the rows were laid down just as cleanly and precisely. The little corner that was left was swept off in five minutes. The last mowers were still finishing their rows when the front ones took their coats on their shoulders and went across the road to Mashkin Upland.

The sun was already going down towards the trees when they entered the wooded little ravine of Mashkin Upland, their whetstone-boxes clinking. The grass came up to the waist in the middle of the hollow, tender and soft and broad-leaved, dappled here and there through the wood with cow-wheat.

After a short discussion, whether to go lengthwise or across, Prokhor Yermilin, also a well-known mower, a huge, darkish peasant, went in front. He went a row forward, turned back, and started off, and everyone began falling into line behind him, going downhill along the hollow and uphill right up to the edge of the wood. The sun went down behind the wood. The dew had already fallen, and the mowers were in the sun only on the hilltop, while below, where a mist was rising, and on the far side they moved in the fresh, dewy shade. The work was in full swing.

The grass, cut with a juicy sound and smelling spicy, fell in high rows. The mowers, crowding in the short rows on all sides, their whetstone-boxes clinking and sounding now with the clash of scythes, now with the whistle of a whetstone on a scythe being sharpened, now with cheerful shouts, urged one another on.

Levin was still going between the young lad and the old man. The old man, who had put on his sheepskin jacket, was just as cheerful, joking and free in his movements. In the wood they kept coming upon birch mushrooms, swollen in the juicy grass, which the mowers cut through. But the old man, each time he came on a mushroom, bent down, picked it up and put it inside his shirt. “Another treat for my old woman,” he would say.

Easy as it was to mow the wet, weak grass, it was hard to go down and up the steep slopes of the ravine. But this did not trouble the old man. Swinging his scythe just as before, with a small, firm step of his feet in their big bast shoes, he climbed slowly up the steep, and though he shook all over, and his breeches, hanging below his shirt, shook too, he did not miss a single blade of grass or a single mushroom on his way, and went on joking with the peasants and Levin just the same. Levin went behind him, and often thought that he would certainly fall, climbing with a scythe up such a steep knoll that it was hard to climb even without one; but he climbed it and did what was needed. He felt that some outside force was moving him.

## Chapter VI

Mashkin Upland was mown; they finished the last rows, put on their coats, and set off cheerfully for home. Levin got on his horse and, taking leave of the peasants with regret, rode home. From the hill he looked back; they could not be seen in the mist rising from the bottom; only the cheerful, rough voices could be heard, the laughter, and the sound of scythes clashing.

Sergei Ivanovich had long since dined and was drinking water with lemon and ice in his room, looking through the newspapers and journals that had just come by post, when Levin, his tangled hair stuck to his forehead with sweat and his back and chest darkened and wet, burst into his room, talking cheerfully.

“We’ve done the whole meadow! Oh, how good it is, wonderful! And how have you been getting on?” said Levin, having completely forgotten the unpleasant conversation of the day before.

“Good heavens! What do you look like!” said Sergei Ivanovich, looking round at his brother with displeasure for the first moment. “But the door, shut the door!” he cried. “You’re sure to have let in a whole dozen.”

Sergei Ivanovich could not bear flies, and in his room he opened the windows only at night and carefully kept the doors shut.

“Honestly, not one. And if I have, I’ll catch it. You wouldn’t believe what a joy it is! How did you spend the day?”

“Very well. But did you really mow the whole day? You must be as hungry as a wolf, I expect. Kuzma has got everything ready for you.”

“No, I don’t even want to eat. I had something there. But I’ll go and wash.”

“Well, go on, go on, and I’ll come to you in a minute,” said Sergei Ivanovich, shaking his head as he looked at his brother. “Go on, go quickly,” he added, smiling, and, gathering up his books, he got ready to go. He suddenly felt cheerful himself and did not want to part from his brother. “Well, and where were you during the rain?”

“What rain? It hardly spat. Well, I’ll come directly. So you spent the day well? Well, that’s splendid.” And Levin went off to change.

Five minutes later the brothers met in the dining-room. Although it seemed to Levin that he did not want to eat, and he sat down to dinner only so as not to hurt Kuzma’s feelings, when he began eating the dinner seemed extraordinarily good to him. Sergei Ivanovich watched him, smiling.

“Ah yes, there’s a letter for you,” he said. “Kuzma, please bring it from downstairs. And mind you shut the door.”

The letter was from Oblonsky. Levin read it aloud. Oblonsky wrote from Petersburg: “I have had a letter from Dolly; she is at Yergushovo, and everything seems to be going wrong for her. Do please go and see her, help her with advice, you know all about everything. She will be so glad to see you. She is quite alone, poor thing. My mother-in-law and all the rest are still abroad.”

“That’s splendid! I shall certainly go and see them,” said Levin. “Or let’s go together. She is such a dear. Isn’t she?”

“And are they not far from here?”

“About thirty versts. Perhaps forty. But it’s an excellent road. We’ll have a splendid drive.”

“I shall be very glad,” said Sergei Ivanovich, still smiling.

The sight of his younger brother put him directly in a cheerful mood.

“Well, what an appetite you have!” he said, looking at his face and neck, burnt brownish-red, bent over his plate.

“Splendid! You wouldn’t believe what a useful regimen it is against every kind of foolishness. I want to enrich medicine with a new term: Arbeitskur.”

“Well, you don’t need it, it seems.”

“No, but various nervous patients do.”

“Yes, it ought to be tried. And, you know, I meant to come to the mowing to look at you, but the heat was so unbearable that I went no further than the wood. I sat a while and went through the wood to the village, met your old nurse, and sounded her out about the peasants’ view of you. As I understood it, they don’t approve of it. She said: ‘It’s not gentlemen’s work.’ In general it seems to me that in the people’s notions the requirements for what they call ‘gentlemen’s’ activity are very firmly fixed. And they don’t allow gentlemen to step outside the frame fixed in their notions.”

“Perhaps; but, you know, it is a pleasure such as I have never felt in all my life. And there’s nothing wrong in it, after all. Is there?” answered Levin. “What can I do if they don’t like it? But, anyway, I think it’s nothing. Eh?”

“On the whole,” Sergei Ivanovich went on, “you are pleased with your day, I see.”

“Very pleased. We mowed the whole meadow. And what an old man I made friends with there! You can’t imagine what a delight he is!”

“Well, so you are pleased with your day. And so am I. In the first place, I solved two chess problems, and one is very nice: it opens with a pawn. I’ll show you. And then I thought about our conversation yesterday.”

“What? About our conversation yesterday?” said Levin, blissfully screwing up his eyes and puffing after finishing his dinner, and decidedly unable to remember what conversation there had been yesterday.

“I find that you are partly right. Our disagreement consists in this: that you take personal interest as the motive, while I suppose that interest in the common good must be present in every man who stands at a certain level of education. Perhaps you are right that activity with a material interest would be more desirable. In general your nature is too prime-sautière, as the French say; you want passionate, energetic activity, or nothing.”

Levin listened to his brother and decidedly understood nothing and did not want to understand. He was only afraid that his brother might ask him some question from which it would be seen that he had heard nothing.

“So that’s how it is, my friend,” said Sergei Ivanovich, touching him on the shoulder.

“Yes, of course. But what of it! I don’t insist on my own view,” answered Levin with a childlike, guilty smile. “What was it I was arguing about?” he thought. “Of course I’m right, and he’s right, and everything is splendid. I only need to go to the office and give some orders.” He got up, stretching and smiling.

Sergei Ivanovich smiled too.

“If you want a walk, let’s go together,” he said, not wanting to part from his brother, who simply breathed freshness and vigour. “Let’s go; we’ll look in at the office too, if you need to.”

“Oh, good heavens!” cried Levin, so loudly that Sergei Ivanovich was startled.

“What, what is it?”

“How is Agafya Mikhailovna’s hand?” said Levin, striking himself on the head. “I had forgotten about her.”

“Much better.”

“Well, I’ll run down to her all the same. I’ll be back before you have time to put on your hat.”

And he clattered down the stairs, his heels rattling like a watchman’s rattle.

## Chapter VII

Stepan Arkadyich had gone to Petersburg to carry out the most natural and necessary duty, familiar to everyone in the service, though incomprehensible to those outside it, without which it is impossible to serve: to remind the ministry of his existence; and, in carrying out this duty, having taken almost all the money from home, he was spending his time gaily and pleasantly at the races and at summer villas. Meanwhile Dolly and the children had moved to the country, to reduce expenses as much as possible. She had moved to Yergushovo, the village that had been her dowry, the very one where the forest had been sold in the spring, and which was fifty versts from Levin’s Pokrovskoe.

At Yergushovo the big old house had long since been pulled down, and the wing had been done up and enlarged by the old prince. Twenty years before, when Dolly was a child, the wing had been roomy and comfortable, though, like all wings, it stood sideways to the drive and faced south. But now this wing was old and rotten. When Stepan Arkadyich had gone there in the spring to sell the forest, Dolly had asked him to look over the house and order whatever repairs were needed. Stepan Arkadyich, like all guilty husbands very anxious about his wife’s comfort, had looked over the house himself and given orders about everything that, by his notion, was needed. By his notion, all the furniture had to be re-covered in cretonne, curtains hung, the garden cleared, a little bridge made by the pond and flowers planted; but he had forgotten many other necessary things, the lack of which later tormented Darya Alexandrovna.

However hard Stepan Arkadyich tried to be a caring father and husband, he could never manage to remember that he had a wife and children. He had bachelor tastes, and it was only with them that he reckoned. On returning to Moscow he had announced to his wife with pride that everything was ready, that the house would be a little toy, and that he strongly advised her to go. His wife’s departure for the country was very pleasant to Stepan Arkadyich in every respect: it was healthy for the children, there were fewer expenses, and he was freer. Darya Alexandrovna, for her part, considered the move to the country for the summer necessary for the children, especially for the little girl, who could not recover after scarlet fever, and, finally, in order to escape the petty humiliations, the petty debts to the firewood dealer, the fishmonger, the shoemaker, which tormented her. Besides that, the move was pleasant to her because she dreamed of getting her sister Kitty to come to her in the country; Kitty was to come back from abroad in the middle of the summer, and bathing had been prescribed for her. Kitty wrote from the spa that nothing appealed to her so much as spending the summer with Dolly at Yergushovo, which was full of childhood memories for both of them.

The first period of country life was very hard for Dolly. She had lived in the country as a child, and the impression had stayed with her that the country was a refuge from all the unpleasantnesses of town, that life there, though not elegant (Dolly easily put up with that), was cheap and comfortable: everything was there, everything was cheap, everything could be got, and it was good for the children. But now, coming to the country as mistress of the house, she saw that it was all quite different from what she had thought.

The day after their arrival there was a downpour of rain, and in the night water came through in the corridor and the nursery, so that the little beds were carried into the drawing-room. There was no cook for the servants; of the nine cows, according to the dairymaid, some were in calf, others had just had their first calf, others were old, others again were hard-uddered; there was not enough butter or even milk for the children. There were no eggs. A hen could not be had; they roasted and boiled old, purple, stringy cocks. It was impossible to get women to scrub the floors: they were all at the potatoes. Driving out was impossible, because one horse was restive and pulled at the shaft. There was nowhere to bathe: the whole bank of the river was trampled by cattle and open to the road; it was even impossible to go for walks, because the cattle came into the garden through the broken fence, and there was one terrible bull who bellowed and therefore, no doubt, butted. There were no wardrobes for clothes. Those there were would not shut, and opened of themselves when anyone walked past them. There were no iron pots or earthenware crocks; there was no copper for the laundry, and not even an ironing-board for the maids’ room.

At first, finding herself, instead of peace and rest, in the midst of these calamities, terrible from her point of view, Darya Alexandrovna was in despair: she bustled with all her might, felt the hopelessness of her position, and every minute held back the tears that came into her eyes. The steward, a former sergeant-major whom Stepan Arkadyich had taken a liking to and promoted from hall porter for his handsome and respectful appearance, took no part in Darya Alexandrovna’s calamities, said respectfully, “It can’t be helped at all, the people are so wretched,” and helped her in nothing.

The position seemed hopeless. But in the Oblonskys’ house, as in all family houses, there was one inconspicuous but most important and useful person: Matryona Filimonovna. She calmed her mistress, assured her that everything would shape itself (this was her word, and Matvei had picked it up from her), and acted herself, without hurrying or fussing.

She at once made friends with the steward’s wife, and on the very first day drank tea with her and the steward under the acacias and discussed all their affairs. Soon Matryona Filimonovna’s club was established under the acacias, and here, through this club, which consisted of the steward’s wife, the village elder and the clerk, the difficulties of life began little by little to be smoothed out, and in a week everything really had shaped itself. The roof was mended; a cook was found, the village elder’s gossip; hens were bought; there began to be milk; the garden was fenced off with poles; the carpenter made a mangle; hooks were put on the wardrobes, and they stopped opening at will; and the ironing-board, wrapped in army cloth, lay from the arm of a chair to the chest of drawers, and the maids’ room smelt of the flat-iron.

“Well, there you are! And you were in despair,” said Matryona Filimonovna, pointing to the board.

They even put up a bathing-place out of straw screens. Lili began bathing, and for Darya Alexandrovna her hopes of a life in the country, if not peaceful at least comfortable, were at least partly fulfilled. Peaceful, with six children, Darya Alexandrovna could not be. One fell ill, another might fall ill, a third lacked something, a fourth showed signs of a bad character, and so on and so on. Rarely, very rarely, did short peaceful periods come. But these cares and anxieties were for Darya Alexandrovna the only happiness possible. If it had not been for them, she would have been left alone with her thoughts about her husband, who did not love her. But besides that, hard as the fear of illnesses, the illnesses themselves, and the grief at the signs of bad tendencies in the children were for the mother, the children themselves were already paying her back for her sorrows with small joys. These joys were so small that they were invisible, like gold in sand, and in bad moments she saw only the sorrows, only the sand; but there were good moments too, when she saw only the joys, only the gold.

Now, in the solitude of the country, she became aware of these joys more and more often. Often, looking at them, she made every possible effort to persuade herself that she was mistaken, that she, as a mother, was partial to her children; still she could not help telling herself that she had lovely children, all six of them, all in different ways, but such as one rarely sees; and she was happy in them and proud of them.
