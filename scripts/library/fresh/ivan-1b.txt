## II

The past history of Ivan Ilyich’s life was most simple and ordinary, and most terrible.

Ivan Ilyich died at forty-five, a Member of the Court of Justice. He was the son of an official who had made, in various ministries and departments in Petersburg, the kind of career that brings people to a position in which, although it becomes clear that they are unfit to perform any real duties, they still cannot be dismissed because of their long past service and their rank; and so they receive invented, fictitious posts and non-fictitious thousands, from six to ten thousand a year, on which they live on into deep old age.

Such was the Privy Councillor Ilya Yefimovich Golovin, a useless member of various useless institutions.

He had three sons, and Ivan Ilyich was the second. The eldest was making the same kind of career as his father, only in another ministry, and was already nearing the stage of service at which that inertia of salary is reached. The third son was a failure. He had spoiled things for himself everywhere and was now serving on the railways; his father, his brothers, and especially their wives, not only disliked meeting him but, unless it was absolutely necessary, did not even remember that he existed. Their sister was married to Baron Greff, the same kind of Petersburg official as his father-in-law. Ivan Ilyich was *le phénix de la famille*, the pride of the family, as people said. He was not as cold and correct as the eldest, nor as reckless as the youngest. He was halfway between them: an intelligent, lively, pleasant and decent man. He was educated at the School of Law together with his younger brother. The younger brother did not finish and was expelled in the fifth year, but Ivan Ilyich completed the course with credit. Already at the School of Law he was what he remained for the rest of his life: a capable man, cheerfully good-natured and sociable, but strict in carrying out what he considered his duty; and he considered his duty to be everything that was considered so by those in the highest positions. He was not a toady, either as a boy or later as a grown man, but from his earliest years he was drawn, like a fly to the light, to the people in the highest positions in society; he took on their manners and their views of life, and established friendly relations with them. All the enthusiasms of his childhood and youth passed without leaving much trace; he gave himself up to sensuality and to vanity and, towards the end, in the senior classes, to liberalism, but always within certain limits, which his instinct pointed out to him correctly.

At the School of Law he had done things which had earlier seemed to him great vileness and which had made him disgusted with himself while he was doing them; but later, seeing that such things were done by people of high standing too and were not considered bad by them, he did not exactly come to think them good, but forgot them entirely, and was not in the least troubled by the memory of them.

Having left the School of Law with the tenth rank, and having received money from his father for his outfit, Ivan Ilyich ordered his clothes from Scharmer, the fashionable tailor, hung on his watch chain a medallion inscribed *respice finem*, “consider the end”, said goodbye to the prince who was patron of the school and to his tutor, dined with his friends at Donon’s, and, with a new fashionable trunk, linen, clothes, shaving and toilet things and a travelling rug, all ordered and bought in the very best shops, he left for the provinces to take up the post of secretary for special commissions to the governor, which his father had found for him.

In the provinces Ivan Ilyich at once made for himself the same easy and pleasant position he had had at the School of Law. He worked, made his career, and at the same time amused himself pleasantly and decently; now and then he travelled to the districts on his superiors’ instructions, carried himself with dignity towards both those above and those below him, and carried out the tasks given him, mainly concerning the religious dissenters, with an exactness and incorruptible honesty of which he could not help being proud.

In official matters, in spite of his youth and his taste for light amusement, he was extremely reserved, formal and even stern; but in society he was often playful and witty, and always good-natured, decent and *bon enfant*, a good fellow, as his chief and his chief’s wife said of him; he was like one of the family in their house.

In the provinces there was also an affair with one of the ladies who threw herself at the smart young lawyer; there was a milliner; there were drinking parties with visiting aides-de-camp and trips after supper to a certain street on the edge of town; there was also some currying favour with his chief and even with his chief’s wife; but all this bore such a high tone of decency that it could not be called by bad names: it all came only under the heading of the French saying, *il faut que jeunesse se passe*, youth must have its fling. Everything was done with clean hands, in clean shirts, with French words, and above all in the very highest society, and therefore with the approval of people of high standing.

Ivan Ilyich served like this for five years, and then came a change in his career. The new judicial institutions appeared; new men were needed.

And Ivan Ilyich became that new man.

Ivan Ilyich was offered the post of examining magistrate, and he accepted it, even though the post was in another province and he would have to abandon the relations he had established and establish new ones. His friends saw him off, had a group photograph taken, presented him with a silver cigarette case, and he left for his new post.

As an examining magistrate Ivan Ilyich was just as *comme il faut*, decent, able to separate his official duties from his private life, and commanding general respect, as he had been as secretary for special commissions. The work of an examining magistrate itself was far more interesting and attractive to Ivan Ilyich than his earlier work. In his earlier post it had been pleasant to walk with a free stride, in his Scharmer uniform, past the trembling petitioners waiting to be received and the officials who envied him, straight into his chief’s office, and sit down with him to tea and a cigarette; but there had been few people directly dependent on his will. Such people were only the district police chiefs and the dissenters, when he was sent on commissions; and he liked to treat such people, who depended on him, courteously, almost as comrades; he liked to let them feel that here he was, who could crush them, treating them simply and in a friendly way. There were few such people then. But now, as an examining magistrate, Ivan Ilyich felt that everyone, everyone without exception, the most important and self-satisfied people, were all in his hands, and that he had only to write certain words on a sheet of headed paper, and this important, self-satisfied person would be brought to him as an accused man or a witness, and would, if he did not choose to have him seated, stand before him and answer his questions. Ivan Ilyich never abused this power of his; on the contrary, he tried to soften its expression; but the awareness of this power, and the possibility of softening it, were for him the chief interest and attraction of his new work. In the work itself, in the investigations, Ivan Ilyich very quickly mastered the technique of setting aside all circumstances that did not concern the case, and of putting even the most complicated case into a form in which it appeared on paper only in its external aspect, in which his personal view was completely excluded, and, above all, in which all the required formalities were observed. This work was new. And he was one of the first men to work out in practice how to apply the legal statutes of 1864.

On moving to the new town as examining magistrate, Ivan Ilyich made new acquaintances and connections, set himself up in a new way and adopted a somewhat different tone. He placed himself at a certain dignified distance from the provincial authorities, chose the best circle among the lawyers and the rich gentry living in the town, and adopted a tone of mild dissatisfaction with the government, moderate liberalism and civilised public spirit. At the same time, without changing the elegance of his dress in the least, Ivan Ilyich in his new post stopped shaving his chin and let his beard grow where it pleased.

Ivan Ilyich’s life in the new town also turned out very pleasant: the society that took a stand against the governor was friendly and good; his salary was larger; and no small pleasure was added to life by whist, which Ivan Ilyich now began to play, having a gift for playing cards cheerfully, thinking quickly and very subtly, so that on the whole he always came out ahead.

After two years of service in the new town, Ivan Ilyich met his future wife. Praskovya Fyodorovna Mikhel was the most attractive, intelligent and brilliant girl in the circle in which Ivan Ilyich moved. Among the other amusements and relaxations from his work as a magistrate, Ivan Ilyich established a playful, light relationship with Praskovya Fyodorovna.

When he was secretary for special commissions, Ivan Ilyich had danced as a rule; as an examining magistrate he danced only as an exception. He danced now in the spirit of: “Although I serve in the new institutions and in the fifth class, when it comes to dancing I can prove that in this field too I can do better than others.” So now and then, at the end of an evening, he danced with Praskovya Fyodorovna, and it was mainly during these dances that he won her. She fell in love with him. Ivan Ilyich had no clear, definite intention of marrying, but when the girl fell in love with him, he put the question to himself: “Really, why not marry?” he said to himself.

Miss Praskovya Fyodorovna came of a good noble family and was not bad-looking; there was a little money. Ivan Ilyich could have counted on a more brilliant match, but this too was a good match. Ivan Ilyich had his salary; she, he hoped, would bring as much again. Good connections; and she was a sweet, pretty and thoroughly decent woman. To say that Ivan Ilyich married because he fell in love with his bride and found in her sympathy for his views of life would be as unjust as to say that he married because the people in his circle approved of the match. Ivan Ilyich married for both reasons: he was doing something pleasant for himself in acquiring such a wife, and at the same time he was doing what people in the highest positions considered right.

And Ivan Ilyich married.

The process of marrying itself, and the first period of married life, with conjugal caresses, new furniture, new crockery, new linen, went very well until his wife became pregnant, so that Ivan Ilyich was already beginning to think that marriage would not only not disturb the character of life that was easy, pleasant, cheerful, always decent and approved by society, which Ivan Ilyich considered natural to life in general, but would even enhance it. But then, from the first months of his wife’s pregnancy, something new appeared, something unexpected, unpleasant, heavy and indecent, which could not have been expected and which there was no way of getting rid of.

His wife, without any reason, as it seemed to Ivan Ilyich, *de gaieté de cœur*, out of sheer caprice, as he said to himself, began to disturb the pleasantness and decency of life: she was jealous of him for no reason, demanded that he pay court to her, found fault with everything and made unpleasant and coarse scenes.

At first Ivan Ilyich hoped to free himself from the unpleasantness of this situation by the same easy and decent attitude to life that had saved him before. He tried to ignore his wife’s moods and went on living as before, easily and pleasantly: he invited friends to his house for a game of cards, and tried going off himself to the club or to friends. But once his wife began to abuse him in coarse words with such energy, and continued to abuse him so persistently every time he did not do as she demanded, evidently firmly resolved not to stop until he gave in, that is, until he stayed at home and moped as she did, that Ivan Ilyich was horrified. He understood that married life, at least with his wife, did not always further the pleasures and decencies of life, but on the contrary often disturbed them, and that it was therefore necessary to protect himself from these disturbances. And Ivan Ilyich began to look for the means to do so. His work was the one thing that impressed Praskovya Fyodorovna, and by means of his work and the duties arising from it, Ivan Ilyich began to struggle with his wife, fencing off his own independent world.

With the birth of a child, the attempts at nursing and the various failures in it, and the real and imaginary illnesses of the child and the mother, in which Ivan Ilyich was required to take part but of which he could understand nothing, his need to fence off a world for himself outside the family became still more pressing.

As his wife became more irritable and demanding, Ivan Ilyich moved the centre of gravity of his life more and more into his work. He began to love his work more and became more ambitious than he had been before.

Very soon, no more than a year after his marriage, Ivan Ilyich understood that married life, while offering certain comforts, was in essence a very complicated and difficult business, towards which, in order to do one’s duty, that is, to lead a decent life approved by society, one had to work out a definite attitude, just as one had towards one’s work.

And Ivan Ilyich worked out such an attitude to married life for himself. He required of family life only those comforts it could give him, of dinner at home, a housekeeper, a bed, and above all that decency of external forms which was determined by public opinion. For the rest, he looked for cheerful pleasantness, and if he found it he was very grateful; but if he met with resistance and grumbling, he at once withdrew into his separate, fenced-off world of work and found his pleasures there.

Ivan Ilyich was valued as a good official, and after three years he was made assistant public prosecutor. His new duties, their importance, the possibility of bringing anyone to trial and putting him in prison, the publicity of his speeches, the success Ivan Ilyich had in this work: all this drew him still more to his work.

Children came. His wife grew more and more querulous and bad-tempered, but the attitude to home life that Ivan Ilyich had worked out made him almost impervious to her grumbling.

After seven years of service in one town, Ivan Ilyich was transferred to the post of public prosecutor in another province. They moved; there was little money, and his wife did not like the place they moved to. Although the salary was larger than before, life was more expensive; besides, two of the children died, and so family life became still more unpleasant for Ivan Ilyich.

Praskovya Fyodorovna blamed her husband for every misfortune that happened in this new place. Most subjects of conversation between husband and wife, especially the children’s upbringing, led to questions on which there were memories of quarrels, and quarrels were ready to flare up at any minute. There remained only those rare periods of being in love that came over the couple, but they did not last long. They were little islands on which they landed for a time, but then they set out again on a sea of hidden hostility, which showed itself in their estrangement from each other. This estrangement might have grieved Ivan Ilyich if he had thought that it ought not to be so, but by now he considered this situation not only normal but the very aim of all his activity in the family. His aim consisted in freeing himself more and more from these unpleasantnesses and giving them a character of harmlessness and decency; and he achieved this by spending less and less time with his family, and when he was obliged to, he tried to safeguard his position by the presence of outsiders. But the main thing was that Ivan Ilyich had his work. The whole interest of life was concentrated for him in the world of work. And this interest absorbed him. The awareness of his power, of the possibility of ruining any man he wished to ruin; his importance, even external, when he entered the court and met his subordinates; his success with those above and below him; and, above all, his mastery in conducting cases, which he felt: all this gladdened him, and, together with conversations with his colleagues, dinners and whist, filled his life. So that on the whole Ivan Ilyich’s life went on as he considered it ought to go: pleasantly and decently.

So he lived another seven years. His elder daughter was already sixteen, another child had died, and there remained a boy at the grammar school, a subject of dispute. Ivan Ilyich wanted to send him to the School of Law, but Praskovya Fyodorovna, to spite him, sent him to the grammar school. The daughter was taught at home and was growing up well; the boy too was doing fairly well at his lessons.
