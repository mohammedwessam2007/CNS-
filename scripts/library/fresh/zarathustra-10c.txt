## The Drunken Song

## 1

Meanwhile, however, one after another had stepped out into the open and into the cool, thoughtful night; but Zarathustra himself led the ugliest man by the hand, to show him his night-world and the great round moon and the silver waterfalls near his cave. There at last they stood still together, old people all of them, but with comforted, brave hearts, and wondering within themselves that they felt so well on earth; and the secrecy of the night came closer and closer to their hearts. And again Zarathustra thought to himself: “Oh, how well they please me now, these higher men!” But he did not say it aloud, for he respected their happiness and their silence.

But then that happened which was the most astonishing thing of that long astonishing day: the ugliest man began once more and for the last time to gurgle and snort, and when he had brought it to words, look, a question sprang round and clean out of his mouth, a good, deep, clear question, which moved the heart in the body of everyone who heard him.

“My friends, all of you,” said the ugliest man, “what do you think? For the sake of this day, I am content for the first time to have lived my whole life.

And it is not yet enough for me to bear witness to so much. It is worth while to live on earth: one day, one festival with Zarathustra has taught me to love the earth.

‘Was that life?’ I will say to death. ‘Well then! Once more!’

My friends, what do you think? Will you not, like me, say to death: Was that life? For Zarathustra’s sake, well then! Once more!”

So spoke the ugliest man; and it was not long before midnight. And what do you suppose happened then? As soon as the higher men heard his question, they all at once became aware of their transformation and recovery, and of who had given it to them: then they sprang towards Zarathustra, thanking, honouring, caressing him, kissing his hands, each in his own way: so that some laughed and some wept. But the old soothsayer danced with pleasure; and if, as some storytellers think, he was full of sweet wine then, he was certainly still fuller of sweet life, and had renounced all weariness. There are even those who tell that the ass danced then: for it was not for nothing that the ugliest man had given it wine to drink before. This may be so or otherwise; and if in truth the ass did not dance that evening, greater and stranger wonders happened then than the dancing of an ass would be. In short, as Zarathustra’s saying goes: “What does it matter!”

## 2

But Zarathustra, when this happened with the ugliest man, stood there like a drunken man: his look went dim, his tongue stammered, his feet swayed. And who could guess what thoughts ran over Zarathustra’s soul then? But clearly his spirit drew back and fled ahead and was in far distances, and as it were “on a high ridge, as it is written, between two seas,

wandering between past and future as a heavy cloud.” Gradually, however, while the higher men held him in their arms, he came back a little to himself and fended off with his hands the crowd of those who honoured him and were anxious about him; but he did not speak. All at once, though, he turned his head quickly, for he seemed to hear something: then he put his finger to his lips and said: “Come!”

And at once it became still and secret all around; but from the depths the sound of a bell came slowly up. Zarathustra listened to it, as did the higher men; but then he put his finger to his lips a second time and said again: “Come! Come! It is getting near midnight!” and his voice had changed. But still he did not move from the spot: then it became still more quiet and secret, and everything listened, even the ass, and Zarathustra’s animals of honour, the eagle and the snake, and likewise Zarathustra’s cave and the great cool moon and the night itself. But Zarathustra put his hand to his lips for the third time and said:

Come! Come! Come! Let us walk now! It is the hour: let us walk into the night!

## 3

You higher men, it is getting near midnight: so I will say something into your ears, as that old bell says it into my ear,

as secretly, as terribly, as heartily as that midnight bell says it to me, which has experienced more than one man:

which counted the heartbeats of pain of your fathers already: ah! ah! how it sighs! how it laughs in its dream! the old, deep, deep midnight!

Quiet! Quiet! Things are heard now that may not be spoken aloud by day; but now, in the cool air, when all the noise of your hearts too has grown still,

now it speaks, now it is heard, now it creeps into night-time souls that are over-awake: ah! ah! how it sighs! how it laughs in its dream!

do you not hear how secretly, terribly, heartily it speaks to you, the old, deep, deep midnight? O man, take care!

## 4

Woe is me! Where has time gone? Did I not sink into deep wells? The world is asleep.

Ah! Ah! The dog howls, the moon shines. I would rather die, die, than tell you what my midnight heart is thinking now.

Now I have died already. It is gone. Spider, why do you spin around me? Do you want blood? Ah! Ah! The dew is falling, the hour is coming:

the hour when I shiver and freeze, which asks and asks and asks: “Who has heart enough for it?

who shall be master of the earth? Who will say: thus shall you run, you great and small rivers!”

the hour is drawing near: O man, you higher man, take care! This speech is for fine ears, for your ears: what does the deep midnight declare?

## 5

It carries me away, my soul is dancing. Day’s work! Day’s work! Who shall be master of the earth?

The moon is cool, the wind is silent. Ah! Ah! Did you fly high enough yet? You danced: but a leg is still not a wing.

You good dancers, now all joy is over, wine has become dregs, every cup has grown brittle, the graves stammer.

You did not fly high enough: now the graves stammer, “Redeem the dead! Why is the night so long? Does the moon not make us drunk?”

You higher men, redeem the graves, wake the corpses! Ah, what is the worm still digging? The hour is drawing near, drawing near,

the bell is booming, the heart still rattles, the woodworm is still digging, the heart’s worm. Ah! Ah! The world is deep!

## 6

Sweet lyre! Sweet lyre! I love your sound, your drunken croaking sound! From how long ago, from how far away your sound comes to me, from far away, from the ponds of love!

You old bell, you sweet lyre! Every pain has torn at your heart, father-pain, fathers’ pain, forefathers’ pain; your speech has grown ripe,

ripe like golden autumn and afternoon, like my hermit’s heart: now you speak: the world itself has grown ripe, the grape is turning brown,

now it wants to die, to die of happiness. You higher men, do you not smell it? A smell is welling up secretly,

a fragrance and smell of eternity, a rosy-blissful, brown, golden-wine smell of old happiness,

of drunken midnight-dying-happiness, which sings: the world is deep, and deeper than the day was aware!

## 7

Leave me! Leave me! I am too pure for you. Do not touch me! Has not my world just become perfect?

My skin is too pure for your hands. Leave me, you stupid, clumsy, dull day! Is not midnight brighter?

The purest shall be masters of the earth, the most unknown, the strongest, the midnight souls, who are brighter and deeper than any day.

O day, you grope for me? You feel for my happiness? Am I rich to you, lonely, a treasure pit, a chamber of gold?

O world, you want me? Am I worldly to you? Am I spiritual to you? Am I divine to you? But day and world, you are too clumsy:

have cleverer hands, reach for deeper happiness, for deeper unhappiness, reach for some god, do not reach for me:

my unhappiness, my happiness is deep, you strange day, but still I am no god, no god’s hell: deep is its woe.

## 8

God’s woe is deeper, you strange world! Reach for God’s woe, not for me! What am I! A drunken, sweet lyre,

a midnight lyre, a bell-toad that no one understands, but which must speak, before the deaf, you higher men! For you do not understand me!

Gone! Gone! O youth! O noon! O afternoon! Now evening has come, and night and midnight; the dog howls, the wind:

is the wind not a dog? It whines, it yaps, it howls. Ah! Ah! how it sighs! how it laughs, how it rattles and gasps, the midnight!

How soberly it speaks just now, this drunken poetess! Has it gone beyond its drunkenness? Has it become over-awake? Is it chewing the cud?

it is chewing over its woe, in its dream, the old deep midnight, and still more its joy. For joy, even if woe is deep: joy is deeper still than heart’s despair.

## 9

You vine! Why do you praise me? Did I not cut you? I am cruel, you bleed: what does your praise of my drunken cruelty want?

“What has become perfect, everything ripe, wants to die!” so you speak. Blessed, blessed be the vintner’s knife! But everything unripe wants to live: woe!

Woe says: “Go! Away, woe!” But everything that suffers wants to live, so that it may become ripe and joyful and longing,

longing for what is further, higher, brighter. “I want heirs,” so says everything that suffers, “I want children, I do not want myself.”

But joy does not want heirs, nor children; joy wants itself, wants eternity, wants recurrence, wants everything eternally the same.

Woe says: “Break, bleed, heart! Walk, leg! Wing, fly! Onward! Upward! Pain!” Well then! Up! O my old heart: woe says: “Go!”

## 10

You higher men, what do you think? Am I a soothsayer? A dreamer? A drunkard? An interpreter of dreams? A midnight bell?

A drop of dew? A vapour and fragrance of eternity? Do you not hear it? Do you not smell it? My world has just become perfect; midnight is also noon,

pain is also a joy, a curse is also a blessing, night is also a sun: go away, or you will learn: a wise man is also a fool.

Did you ever say Yes to a single joy? Oh, my friends, then you said Yes to all woe as well. All things are chained together, threaded together, in love,

if you ever wanted one time twice, if you ever said, “You please me, happiness! Quick! Moment!” then you wanted everything back!

everything anew, everything eternally, everything chained, threaded, in love: oh, then you loved the world,

you eternal ones, love it eternally and always: and to woe too you say: go, but come back! For all joy wants eternity!

## 11

All joy wants the eternity of all things, wants honey, wants dregs, wants drunken midnight, wants graves, wants the comfort of tears at graves, wants gilded sunset;

what does joy not want! It is thirstier, heartier, hungrier, more terrible, more secret than all woe; it wants itself, it bites into itself, the ring’s will wrestles in it;

it wants love, it wants hatred, it is over-rich, it gives, throws away, begs for someone to take it, thanks the one who takes, it would like to be hated;

so rich is joy that it thirsts for woe, for hell, for hatred, for disgrace, for the cripple, for the world: for this world, oh, you know it well!

You higher men, it is for you that it longs, joy, the unbridled, blissful one, for your woe, you failures! All eternal joy longs for failures.

For all joy wants itself, and so it wants heart’s grief too! O happiness, O pain! Oh, break, heart! You higher men, learn it: joy wants eternity,

joy wants the eternity of all things, wants deep, deep eternity!

## 12

Have you learned my song now? Have you guessed what it wants? Well then! Up! You higher men, now sing me my round!

Sing me now yourselves the song whose name is “Once More”, whose meaning is “for all eternity!” Sing, you higher men, Zarathustra’s round!

> O man! Take care!
> What does the deep midnight declare?
> “I slept, I slept,
> From deep dream I awoke to air:
> The world is deep,
> And deeper than the day was aware.
> Deep is its woe,
> Joy, deeper still than heart’s despair:
> Woe says: Go!
> But all joy wants eternity,
> Wants deep, deep eternity!”
— Zarathustra’s round

## The Sign

But in the morning after this night Zarathustra sprang up from his bed, girded his loins, and came out of his cave, glowing and strong, like a morning sun that comes out of dark mountains.

“You great star,” he said, as he had once said before, “you deep eye of happiness, what would all your happiness be, if you did not have those for whom you shine!

And if they stayed in their rooms while you were already awake and coming and giving and handing out: how your proud shame would be angry at that!

Well then! They are still asleep, these higher men, while I am awake: these are not my right companions! It is not for them that I am waiting here in my mountains.

I want to go to my work, to my day: but they do not understand what the signs of my morning are; my step is no call to wake for them.

They are still asleep in my cave; their dream is still chewing on my midnights. The ear that listens for me, the obeying ear, is lacking in their limbs.”

Zarathustra had said this to his heart when the sun rose: then he looked up questioningly, for he heard above him the sharp cry of his eagle. “Well then!” he called up. “So it pleases me and so it is fitting for me. My animals are awake, for I am awake.

My eagle is awake and honours the sun as I do. With eagle’s claws he reaches for the new light. You are my right animals; I love you.

But I still lack my right men!”

So spoke Zarathustra; but then it happened that he suddenly heard himself swarmed and fluttered around as if by countless birds; and the whirring of so many wings and the crowding round his head was so great that he closed his eyes. And truly, it fell on him like a cloud, like a cloud of arrows pouring itself out over a new enemy. But look, here it was a cloud of love, and over a new friend.

“What is happening to me?” thought Zarathustra in his astonished heart, and slowly sat down on the big stone that lay beside the entrance to his cave. But as he reached around him and above him and below him with his hands, and fended off the tender birds, look, something stranger still happened to him: for as he did so he reached, without noticing, into a thick, warm, shaggy mane; and at the same time a roar sounded in front of him, a soft, long lion’s roar.

“The sign is coming,” said Zarathustra, and his heart was changed. And in truth, when it grew light before him, a mighty yellow animal lay at his feet and pressed its head against his knees and would not leave him for love, and behaved like a dog that finds its old master again. But the doves were no less eager with their love than the lion; and each time a dove flitted across the lion’s nose, the lion shook its head and wondered and laughed at it.

To all this Zarathustra said only one thing: “My children are near, my children”; then he became quite silent. But his heart was loosened, and tears dropped from his eyes and fell on his hands. And he paid no more attention to anything, and sat there motionless, and no longer fended off the animals. Then the doves flew to and fro and settled on his shoulder and caressed his white hair and did not tire of tenderness and rejoicing. But the strong lion kept licking the tears that fell on Zarathustra’s hands, and roared and growled shyly as it did so. So these animals behaved.

All this lasted a long time, or a short time: for, properly speaking, there is no time on earth for such things. But meanwhile the higher men in Zarathustra’s cave had woken up, and were arranging themselves into a procession to go out to meet Zarathustra and offer him their morning greeting: for when they woke they had found that he was no longer among them. But when they reached the door of the cave, and the sound of their steps ran ahead of them, the lion started violently, turned away from Zarathustra all at once, and sprang, roaring wildly, at the cave; but the higher men, when they heard it roar, all cried out as if with one mouth, and fled back, and were gone in an instant.

But Zarathustra himself, dazed and strange, rose from his seat, looked around him, stood there in amazement, questioned his heart, collected himself, and was alone. “What was it I heard?” he said at last, slowly. “What has just happened to me?”

And already memory came to him, and he understood at a glance everything that had happened between yesterday and today. “Here is the stone,” he said, and stroked his beard, “on which I sat yesterday morning; and here the soothsayer came to me, and here I first heard the cry I heard just now, the great cry of distress.

O you higher men, it was your distress that that old soothsayer prophesied to me yesterday morning,

to your distress he wanted to seduce and tempt me: ‘O Zarathustra,’ he said to me, ‘I come to seduce you to your last sin.’

To my last sin?” cried Zarathustra, and laughed angrily at his own words: “What was it that was saved up for me as my last sin?”

And once more Zarathustra sank into himself, and sat down again on the big stone and thought. Suddenly he sprang up:

“Pity! Pity for the higher man!” he cried, and his face turned to bronze. “Well then! That has had its time!

My suffering and my pity: what does it matter! Am I striving for happiness? I am striving for my work!

Well then! The lion has come, my children are near, Zarathustra has grown ripe, my hour has come:

this is my morning, my day is beginning: rise now, rise, you great noon!”

Thus spoke Zarathustra, and left his cave, glowing and strong, like a morning sun that comes out of dark mountains.
