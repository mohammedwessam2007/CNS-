## Book Six

1. The substance of the whole is obedient and easily shaped; and the reason that governs it has no cause in itself for doing harm. For it has no badness, nor does it do anything badly, nor is anything harmed by it. Everything comes into being and is completed according to it.

2. Let it make no difference to you whether you are cold or warm when you do what is right, whether sleepy or well rested, whether spoken badly of or praised, whether dying or doing something else. For dying too is one of the acts of life; so here too it is enough to do the present thing well.

3. Look within. Do not let the special quality or the value of anything slip past you.

4. All the things that exist will very soon change: they will either be turned to vapour, if all substance is one, or be scattered.

5. The reason that governs knows its own state, and what it is doing, and on what material.

6. The best way to take revenge is not to become like the wrongdoer.

7. Take delight and rest in one thing: passing from one act for the common good to another act for the common good, with God in mind.

8. The ruling mind is what wakes itself and turns itself, and makes itself whatever it wants to be, and makes everything that happens appear to itself as it wants it to.

9. Everything is brought to completion according to the nature of the whole; and not according to any other nature, either surrounding it from outside, or contained within it, or hanging apart from it outside.

10. Either a jumble, a tangle and a scattering; or unity, order and providence. If the first, why do I even want to stay in such a random mixture and mess? Why should I care about anything except how I shall at last “become earth”? And why am I troubled? The scattering will come to me whatever I do. But if the second, I revere, I stand firm, and I trust in the one who governs.

11. When you are forced by circumstances to be, as it were, thrown into confusion, come back to yourself quickly, and do not stay out of rhythm longer than you must. For you will master the harmony better by constantly coming back to it.

12. If you had a stepmother and a mother at the same time, you would look after the one, but you would still keep going back to your mother. This is what the palace and philosophy are to you now. Go back to philosophy often, and rest in her; it is because of her that life at the palace seems bearable to you, and you seem bearable in it.

13. Just as, with meat and other things we eat, we can form the impression: this is the dead body of a fish, this the dead body of a bird or a pig; and again, that this fine Falernian wine is a little grape juice, and this purple robe some sheep’s wool dyed in the blood of a shellfish; and of sex, that it is the rubbing together of bits of flesh and a spurt of fluid with a spasm: these are the kind of impressions that get down to the things themselves and go right through them, so that we see what they really are. So we should do the same all through life: where things seem most worthy of our trust, lay them bare, see how cheap they are, and strip away the story that makes them seem grand. For vanity is a terrible cheat, and when you think you are most busy with serious things, that is when it most bewitches you. See, anyway, what Crates says about Xenocrates himself.

14. Most of the things the crowd admires belong to the most general class: things held together by mere cohesion or by growth, like stones, wood, fig trees, vines, olives. Those admired by people a little more refined belong to things held together by a living soul, such as flocks and herds. Those admired by people more refined still belong to things with a rational soul, though not a universal one, but rational in being skilled in some craft or clever in some other way, or simply in owning a great many slaves. But the man who values a soul that is rational, universal and made for the city no longer turns towards any of the others, but above all keeps his own soul rational and social in its state and its movements, and works together with those of his kind towards this end.

15. Some things are hurrying to come into being, others hurrying to be gone, and of what is coming into being, part has already gone out. Flows and changes are constantly renewing the world, just as the unbroken passage of time always makes the endless age new. In this river, then, what is there among the things rushing past, on which one cannot stand, that one should value highly? It is as if someone began to love one of the little sparrows flying past, and it had already gone out of sight. Each person’s life itself is something like this, like the vapour rising from blood, or the breath drawn from the air. For what it is to draw in the air once and give it back, as we do every moment, so it is to give back the whole power of breathing, which you got yesterday or the day before at your birth, to the place from which you first drew it.

16. It is not to be valued that we breathe out moisture like plants, or breathe like cattle and wild beasts, or receive impressions from things, or are jerked about like puppets by our impulses, or herd together, or take food; for that is just like getting rid of the waste of our food.

What, then, is to be valued? To be applauded? No. Nor, then, to be applauded by tongues; for the praise of the many is a clapping of tongues. So you have let go of your bit of fame too. What is left that is worth valuing? This, I think: to move and to hold back in keeping with our own make-up, which is what all training and all crafts lead to.

For every craft aims at this: that what is made should be fit for the work it was made for. The vine-grower, the horse-breaker, the dog-trainer all look for this. And what do education and teaching aim at?

Here, then, is what is valuable. And if this goes well, you will not try to get any of the other things for yourself. Will you not stop valuing many other things as well? Then you will be neither free, nor self-sufficient, nor free of passion. For you are bound to be envious and jealous, to be suspicious of those who can take those things away, and to plot against those who have what you value. In a word, a man who needs any of those things is bound to be in turmoil, and to blame the gods often as well.

But respect and honour for your own mind will make you pleasing to yourself, in harmony with your fellows, and in tune with the gods: that is, praising whatever they give out and have ordained.

17. Up, down, round in circles go the movements of the elements. But the movement of virtue is in none of these; it is something more divine, and goes on its way well, along a road hard to understand.

18. What a thing people do! They will not speak well of those who live in their own time and with them; but they set great store on being spoken well of by those who will come after them, whom they have never seen and never will see. That is almost like being upset that people born before you did not speak well of you.

19. If something is hard for you to do, do not think it impossible for a human being. But if something is possible for a human being and in keeping with his nature, think that it is within your reach too.

20. In the gymnasium someone scratches us with his nails, or butts us with his head and gives us a bruise; but we do not make a show of it, or take offence, or suspect him afterwards of plotting against us. We do keep an eye on him, but not as an enemy, nor with suspicion: we just keep out of his way, good-naturedly. Let it be like this in the rest of life too. Let us overlook many things in those who are, so to speak, training with us. For it is possible, as I said, to keep out of their way without suspecting or hating them.

21. If anyone can prove to me and show me that I am wrong in what I think or do, I will gladly change. For I am seeking the truth, by which no one was ever harmed; but the one who stays in his own self-deception and ignorance is harmed.

22. I do my own duty. Other things do not distract me; for they are either lifeless, or without reason, or have lost their way and do not know the road.

23. Towards animals without reason, and things and objects in general, behave generously and freely, since you have reason and they do not. Towards human beings, since they have reason, behave as a fellow. And in everything call on the gods. And do not worry about how long you will be doing this; three hours spent like this are enough.

24. Alexander of Macedon and his mule-driver, when they died, came to the same state. For either they were taken back into the same seed-reasons of the world, or they were scattered alike into atoms.

25. Think how many things happen in each of us at the same moment, in body and soul alike. Then you will not be surprised that many more things, or rather all the things that come to be, exist at the same time in the one great whole, which we call the world.

26. If someone asks you how to spell the name Antoninus, will you shout out each letter in a rage? And if they get angry, will you get angry back? Will you not rather go through the letters one by one, calmly? So too here: remember that every duty is made up of certain steps. You must keep to these, without getting upset, and without being difficult with those who are difficult with you, and finish what is in front of you methodically.

27. How cruel it is not to let people go after what seems to them to be in their own interest and good for them! And yet, in a way, you do not allow them to do this when you are angry that they do wrong; for they are certainly moving towards what they think is their own interest and good. “But it is not so.” Then teach them, and show them, without being angry.

28. Death is a rest from the jolts of the senses, from being jerked about by impulses, from the wanderings of thought, and from serving the flesh.

29. It is a disgrace if, in this life where your body does not give up, your soul gives up first.

30. Take care that you are not turned into a Caesar, not dyed purple; for it happens. Keep yourself, then, simple, good, pure, serious, unaffected, a friend of justice, reverent towards the gods, kind, affectionate, strong in doing what you should. Struggle to stay the man that philosophy wanted to make you. Revere the gods, look after people. Life is short. The one harvest of life on earth is a holy disposition and actions for the common good.

Do everything as a pupil of Antoninus. Remember his energy in acting according to reason, his evenness in everything, his holiness, the calm of his face, his gentleness, his freedom from vanity, his eagerness to get a firm grasp of things.

And how he would never let anything go without first examining it thoroughly and understanding it clearly; and how he put up with those who blamed him unjustly, without blaming them in return; how he was never in a hurry; how he would not listen to slander; how carefully he looked into people’s characters and actions, and how he was no fault-finder, not easily frightened, not suspicious, not a sophist; how little he needed, in house, bed, clothes, food and servants.

How hard-working he was, and how patient; how, because of his simple diet, he could stay at the same task till evening, without even needing to relieve himself except at the usual hour; how steady and consistent he was in his friendships; how he put up with those who openly opposed his opinions, and was glad if anyone showed him something better; how reverent he was towards the gods, without superstition. So that your last hour may find you with as good a conscience as his.

31. Sober up, call yourself back; and when you have woken from sleep and realised that it was dreams that troubled you, now that you are awake again, look at these things as you looked at those.

32. I am made of a little body and a soul. To the little body all things are indifferent, for it cannot tell the difference. To the mind, everything is indifferent that is not its own activity; and all its own activities are up to it. And of these, it is concerned only with the present; for its future and past activities are themselves already indifferent.

33. Pain in the hand or the foot is not against nature, as long as the foot does the work of a foot and the hand the work of a hand. So neither is pain against nature for a human being as a human being, as long as he does the work of a human being. And if it is not against his nature, it is not bad for him either.

34. What pleasures robbers have enjoyed, and perverts, and parricides, and tyrants!

35. Do you not see how ordinary craftsmen go some way to meet the wishes of people who know nothing of their craft, but still keep hold of the principles of their craft, and will not bear to depart from them? Is it not strange that the architect and the doctor should respect the principles of their own craft more than a human being respects his own, which he shares with the gods?

36. Asia and Europe are corners of the world; every sea a drop in the world; Mount Athos a little clod of the world; all of present time a point in eternity. Everything is small, changeable, vanishing.

Everything comes from there, either springing directly from that common ruling mind, or following from it as a consequence. So even the lion’s gaping jaws, and poison, and every harmful thing, like thorns and mud, are by-products of those grand and beautiful things. So do not imagine that they are foreign to what you revere, but think of the source of all things.

37. Whoever has seen what is now has seen everything that has happened from all eternity and everything that will happen to endless time. For all things are of one kind and one form.

38. Think often of how all things in the world are bound together and related to one another. For in a way all things are woven together, and so all are friendly to one another; for one thing follows another in order, because of the tension that runs through them, their breathing together, and the unity of their substance.

39. Fit yourself to the things that have fallen to your lot; and love the people among whom your lot has been cast, but truly.

40. Every instrument, tool or vessel is in good order if it does the work it was made for; and yet there the maker is not present. But in things held together by nature, the power that made them is within them and stays there. So you should respect it all the more, and believe that, if you live and act according to its will, everything is as you would wish. And so too, for the whole, its own affairs are as it would wish.

41. Whatever thing outside your choice you take to be good or bad for you, then when that bad thing befalls you, or you miss that good thing, you are bound to blame the gods, and to hate the people who caused the misfortune or the loss, or whom you suspect will. And indeed we do much wrong because we care about these things. But if we judge only the things that are up to us to be good and bad, there is no reason left either to accuse God or to take up a stand against a man as an enemy.

42. We are all working together towards one end, some knowingly and with understanding, others without realising it; just as Heraclitus, I think, says that even people asleep are workers and fellow-workers in what happens in the world. Each works together in a different way; and even the one who complains and tries to oppose and undo what happens contributes abundantly, for the world needed such a man too. So in the end, understand which of these you are placing yourself among. For the one who governs the whole will certainly use you well, and will take you in as a part of the fellow-workers and helpers. But do not you become a part like the cheap and ridiculous line in the play that Chrysippus mentions.

43. Does the sun expect to do the work of the rain? Or Asclepius that of the Fruit-bearing goddess? And what of each of the stars? Are they not different, and yet working together towards the same end?

44. If the gods have made decisions about me and what must happen to me, they have decided well; for it is not easy even to imagine a god without wisdom. And why should they want to harm me? What would they gain by it, or the whole, which is what they care for most? And if they have not made decisions about me individually, they have certainly decided about the whole, and I ought to welcome and be content with what follows from that.

And if they decide about nothing (which it is impious to believe; or else let us stop sacrificing, praying, swearing, and doing all the other things we do on the assumption that the gods are present and living with us), if, then, they decide about none of our affairs, I can still decide about myself, and it is for me to consider what is useful. And what is useful for each thing is what is in keeping with its make-up and nature; and my nature is rational and made for the city.

My city and my country, as Antoninus, is Rome; as a human being, the world. So only what benefits these cities is good for me.

45. Whatever happens to each person is for the good of the whole. That would be enough. But if you watch carefully you will see this too, as a general rule: whatever is good for one person is good for other people too. But here let “good” be taken in the common sense, of the indifferent things.

46. Just as the shows in the amphitheatre and places like that disgust you, because the same things are always seen, and the sameness makes the spectacle tedious, so it is with the whole of life. Everything, up and down, is the same, and comes from the same things. How long, then?

47. Think constantly of all kinds of people, of all sorts of occupations and of every nation, who have died; come right down to Philistion and Phoebus and Origanion. Now pass on to other kinds. We must go where so many great orators have gone, so many noble philosophers, Heraclitus, Pythagoras, Socrates; so many heroes of old, so many generals and tyrants since; and besides these, Eudoxus, Hipparchus, Archimedes, and other sharp minds, great-souled, hard-working, cunning, self-willed; mockers of this perishable and short-lived human life itself, like Menippus and all his kind. Think of all of them, that they have long been in the ground. What harm is that to them? And what to those whose names are not even mentioned? One thing here is worth a great deal: to live out your life with truth and justice, kind even to liars and the unjust.

48. When you want to cheer yourself up, think of the good qualities of the people you live with: one’s energy, another’s modesty, another’s generosity, and some other quality in another. For nothing is as cheering as the images of the virtues showing in the characters of the people we live with, coming together as much as possible. So keep them ready to hand.

49. Are you upset that you weigh only so many pounds, and not three hundred? Then do not be upset either that you must live only so many years, and no longer. For just as you are content with the amount of substance given to you, so be content with the time.

50. Try to persuade them; but act even against their will, when the principle of justice leads you to. But if someone uses force to stand in your way, change course to accepting it without distress, and use the obstacle to practise another virtue. And remember that your impulse was with a reservation, and that you were not aiming at the impossible. At what, then? At an impulse of this kind. And that you have achieved. The things we set out to do are happening.

51. The man who loves fame thinks his good lies in other people’s activity; the man who loves pleasure, in his own feelings; the man with sense, in his own actions.

52. It is possible to form no opinion about this, and not to be troubled in your soul. For things themselves have no power by nature to create our judgements.

53. Get used to listening carefully to what another person says, and as far as possible get inside the mind of the speaker.

54. What is not good for the hive is not good for the bee either.

55. If the sailors spoke badly of the helmsman, or the sick of the doctor, would they be paying attention to anything else than how he might bring about the safety of the passengers, or the health of those he is treating?

56. How many of those with whom I came into the world have already left it!

57. To people with jaundice honey seems bitter; to people bitten by a mad dog, water is terrifying; to children, a ball is beautiful. Why then am I angry? Do you think a false belief has less power than bile in the jaundiced, or poison in the man who has been bitten?

58. No one will prevent you from living according to the reason of your own nature; nothing will happen to you against the reason of the common nature.

59. What kind of people they are whom men want to please, and for what gains, and by what actions! How quickly time will cover everything, and how much it has covered already!
