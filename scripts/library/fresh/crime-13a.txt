## CHAPTER IV

And Raskolnikov went straight to the house on the canal where Sonya lived. It was a three-storey house, old and painted green. He sought out the caretaker and got vague directions from him as to where Kapernaumov the tailor lived. Having found, in a corner of the courtyard, the entrance to a narrow, dark staircase, he climbed at last to the second floor and came out onto the gallery that ran round it on the courtyard side. While he was wandering in the dark, puzzling where the entrance to Kapernaumov’s could be, a door suddenly opened three steps away from him; he caught hold of it mechanically.

“Who is there?” asked a woman’s voice anxiously.

“It’s me... to see you,” answered Raskolnikov, and went into a tiny entrance. There, on a chair with a sunken seat, in a bent brass candlestick, stood a candle.

“It’s you! Lord!” Sonya cried out faintly, and stood rooted to the spot.

“Which way to you? In here?”

And Raskolnikov, trying not to look at her, went quickly into the room.

A minute later Sonya came in too with the candle, set it down and stood before him, completely at a loss, in inexpressible agitation and evidently frightened by his unexpected visit. Suddenly colour rushed into her pale face, and tears even came into her eyes... She felt sick, and ashamed, and sweet... Raskolnikov quickly turned away and sat down on a chair by the table. In passing he managed to take in the room at a glance.

It was a large room, but extremely low, the only one let by the Kapernaumovs, whose locked door was in the wall on the left. On the opposite side, in the wall on the right, there was another door, always locked tight. Beyond it was another flat, a neighbouring one, under another number. Sonya’s room looked rather like a shed; it had the shape of a very irregular quadrilateral, and this gave it something misshapen. The wall with three windows, looking out on the canal, cut across the room at a slant, so that one corner, terribly sharp, ran off somewhere into the depths, so that in the weak light one could not even make it out properly; the other corner was far too hideously obtuse. In all this large room there was hardly any furniture. In the corner on the right was a bed; beside it, nearer the door, a chair. Along the same wall as the bed, right by the door into the other flat, stood a plain deal table covered with a little blue cloth; by the table, two wicker chairs. Then, against the opposite wall, near the sharp corner, stood a small chest of drawers of plain wood, as if lost in the emptiness. That was all there was in the room. The yellowish, scuffed and worn wallpaper had gone black in all the corners; it must have been damp and full of fumes here in winter. The poverty was plain to see; there were not even curtains at the bed.

Sonya silently watched her visitor, who was examining her room so attentively and so unceremoniously, and at last even began to tremble with fear, as if she were standing before a judge who would decide her fate.

“I am late... Is it eleven yet?” he asked, still not raising his eyes to her.

“Yes,” muttered Sonya. “Oh yes, it is!” she hurried suddenly, as if this were the whole way out for her. “The landlord’s clock has just struck... and I heard it myself... Yes, it is.”

“I have come to you for the last time,” Raskolnikov went on gloomily, though even now this was only the first time. “I may not see you again...”

“You are... going away?”

“I don’t know... everything is tomorrow...”

“So you won’t be at Katerina Ivanovna’s tomorrow?” Sonya’s voice trembled.

“I don’t know. Everything is tomorrow morning... That is not the point: I have come to say one word...”

He raised his thoughtful look to her and suddenly noticed that he was sitting, while she was still standing before him.

“Why are you standing? Sit down,” he said, in a suddenly changed, quiet and gentle voice.

She sat down. He looked at her kindly and almost with compassion for a minute.

“How thin you are! What a hand you have! Quite transparent. Fingers like a dead girl’s.”

He took her hand. Sonya smiled faintly.

“I have always been like this,” she said.

“Even when you lived at home?”

“Yes.”

“Well, of course!” he said abruptly, and the expression of his face and the sound of his voice suddenly changed again. He looked round once more.

“You rent this from Kapernaumov?”

“Yes, sir...”

“They are there, behind the door?”

“Yes... They have the same kind of room too.”

“All in one?”

“In one, sir.”

“I would be afraid in your room at night,” he remarked gloomily.

“The landlord and his wife are very good, very kind,” answered Sonya, still as if she had not come to herself or collected her wits. “And all the furniture, and everything... everything is theirs. And they are very kind, and the children often come to see me too...”

“The ones with the speech defect?”

“Yes, sir... He stammers, and he is lame too. And his wife too... Not that she stammers, but it’s as if she can’t say everything out. She is kind, very. And he used to be a house serf. And there are seven children... and only the eldest stammers, and the others are just sickly... but they don’t stammer... But how do you know about them?” she added with some surprise.

“Your father told me everything then. He told me everything about you... And about how you went out at six o’clock and came back after eight, and about how Katerina Ivanovna knelt by your bed.”

Sonya was confused.

“I thought I saw him today,” she whispered hesitantly.

“Whom?”

“Father. I was walking along the street, near there, at the corner, after nine, and it was as if he were walking ahead of me. And it was just like him. I was going to go in to Katerina Ivanovna’s...”

“You were walking out?”

“Yes,” Sonya whispered abruptly, confused again and looking down.

“Katerina Ivanovna nearly beat you, didn’t she, when you lived with your father?”

“Oh no, what are you saying, what are you saying, no!” Sonya looked at him even with a kind of fright.

“So you love her?”

“Her? But how could I no-o-ot!” Sonya drew out plaintively, suddenly clasping her hands in suffering. “Ah! you... her... If only you knew. Why, she is just like a child... Why, her mind is quite unhinged... from grief. And how clever she used to be... how generous... how kind! You don’t know anything, anything... ah!”

Sonya said this as if in despair, agitated and suffering and wringing her hands. Her pale cheeks flushed again, and torment showed in her eyes. It was evident that a terrible lot had been stirred in her, that she terribly wanted to express something, to say something, to stand up for someone. A kind of insatiable compassion, if one may put it so, suddenly appeared in every feature of her face.

“Beat me! What are you saying! Lord, beat me! And even if she did beat me, what of it! Well, what of it? You don’t know anything, anything... She is so unhappy, ah, how unhappy! And ill... She is looking for justice... She is pure. She believes so much that there must be justice in everything, and she demands it... And you could torture her, but she would not do anything unjust. She doesn’t notice herself how impossible it all is for there to be justice among people, and she gets irritated... Like a child, like a child! She is just, just!”

“And what will become of you?”

Sonya looked at him questioningly.

“They are left on your hands, you know. It’s true that everything was on you before too, and your late father came to you to beg when he had a hangover. Well, and now what will happen?”

“I don’t know,” Sonya said sadly.

“Will they stay there?”

“I don’t know; they owe money for that lodging; only the landlady, I hear, said today that she wants to give them notice, and Katerina Ivanovna says that she won’t stay a minute herself.”

“How is it she is so bold? Is she relying on you?”

“Ah no, don’t say that!.. We are one, we live as one,” Sonya suddenly grew agitated again and even irritated, exactly as if a canary or some other little bird had got angry. “And what is she to do? Well, what, what is she to do?” she asked, growing heated and agitated. “And how, how she cried today! Her mind is getting confused, didn’t you notice? It is getting confused; now she worries like a little girl about everything being done properly tomorrow, about there being food and everything... now she wrings her hands, coughs blood, cries, suddenly starts beating her head against the wall, as if in despair. And then she is comforted again; she keeps relying on you: she says that you will be her helper now, and that she will borrow a little money somewhere and go to her home town, with me, and open a boarding school for girls of noble birth, and take me as a supervisor, and a whole new, beautiful life will begin for us, and she kisses me, hugs me, comforts me, and she believes it so! she believes her fancies so! Well, how can anyone contradict her? And she herself spent the whole day today washing, cleaning, mending; she dragged the washtub into the room herself, with her poor little strength, she got out of breath, and just fell down on the bed; and this morning we went to the market stalls too, to buy shoes for Polechka and Lena, because theirs have all fallen to pieces, only we didn’t have enough money for what we had worked out, a great deal short, and she chose such dear little boots, because she has taste, you don’t know... And right there in the shop she burst out crying, in front of the shopmen, because there wasn’t enough... Ah, how pitiful it was to see.”

“Well, after that it’s understandable that you... live like this,” said Raskolnikov with a bitter smile.

“But aren’t you sorry? Aren’t you sorry?” Sonya flared up again. “Why, you, I know, you gave away your last money yourself, without having seen anything yet. And if you had seen everything, oh Lord! And how many, many times I have brought her to tears! Why, only last week! Oh, me! Only a week before his death. I acted cruelly! And how many, many times I have done that. Ah, how painful it was to remember all day today!”

Sonya was even wringing her hands as she spoke, from the pain of the memory.

“You, cruel?”

“Yes, me, me! I came to see them then,” she went on, crying, “and my late father says: ‘Read to me, Sonya,’ he says, ‘my head is aching somehow, read to me... here is a book’; he had some book, he had got it from Andrei Semyonych, from Lebezyatnikov, who lives here; he was always getting such funny books. And I say: ‘I must go’, and so I wouldn’t read to him; and I had come in to them mainly to show Katerina Ivanovna some collars; Lizaveta, the dealer, had brought me some collars and cuffs cheap, pretty ones, new, with a pattern. And Katerina Ivanovna liked them very much; she put them on and looked at herself in the mirror, and she liked them very, very much: ‘Give them to me, Sonya,’ she says, ‘please.’ She said please, and she wanted them so much. And where was she to wear them! It was just that the old, happy time came back to her! She looks at herself in the mirror, admires herself, and she has no dresses at all, none, no things at all, for so many years now! And she never asks anyone for anything; she is proud, she would sooner give away her last thing herself; and here she asked, she liked them so much! And I grudged giving them: ‘What do you want them for, Katerina Ivanovna?’ I said. I said just that, ‘what for’. That I should never have said to her! She looked at me like that, and she felt so heavy, so heavy, that I had refused, and it was so pitiful to see... And it wasn’t the collars that made it heavy for her, but that I had refused, I could see. Ah, I think I would take it all back now, do it all over again, all those words of before... Oh, me... but what of it!.. it’s all the same to you!”

“You knew this Lizaveta, the dealer?”

“Yes... But did you know her?” Sonya asked in her turn, with some surprise.

“Katerina Ivanovna has consumption, a bad case; she will die soon,” said Raskolnikov after a silence, not answering the question.

“Oh, no, no, no!” And with an unconscious gesture Sonya seized him by both hands, as if begging him that it should not be.

“But it will be better if she dies.”

“No, not better, not better, not better at all!” she repeated, frightened and without thinking.

“And the children? Where will you take them then, if not to your place?”

“Oh, I don’t know!” Sonya cried almost in despair, and clutched her head. It was evident that this thought had flashed through her many, many times already, and he had only startled it up again.

“Well, and if you, while Katerina Ivanovna is still alive, now, fall ill and are taken to the hospital, what will happen then?” he insisted mercilessly.

“Ah, what are you saying, what are you saying! That can’t possibly happen!” and Sonya’s face was distorted with terrible fright.

“Why can’t it?” Raskolnikov went on with a harsh smile. “You’re not insured, are you? Then what will become of them? They will go out into the street, the whole troop of them; she will cough and beg and beat her head against a wall somewhere, as she did today, and the children will cry... And then she will collapse, be taken to the police station, to the hospital, she will die, and the children...”

“Oh, no!.. God will not let that happen!” broke at last from Sonya’s tight breast. She listened, looking at him in supplication and folding her hands in mute entreaty, as if everything depended on him.

Raskolnikov stood up and began walking about the room. A minute passed. Sonya stood with her arms and head lowered, in terrible anguish.

“And can’t you save? Put something by for a rainy day?” he asked, stopping suddenly in front of her.

“No,” whispered Sonya.

“Of course not! And have you tried?” he added, almost mockingly.

“I have.”

“And it came to nothing! Well, of course! Why even ask!”

And again he walked about the room. Another minute passed.

“You don’t earn something every day?”

Sonya was more embarrassed than before, and the colour rushed into her face again.

“No,” she whispered with a tormented effort.

“It will probably be the same with Polechka,” he said suddenly.

“No! no! It can’t be, no!” Sonya cried aloud, like someone desperate, as if she had suddenly been stabbed with a knife. “God, God will not allow such a horror!..”

“He allows it for others.”

“No, no! God will protect her, God!..” she repeated, beside herself.

“But perhaps there is no God at all,” Raskolnikov answered with a kind of malicious glee, laughed, and looked at her.

Sonya’s face suddenly changed terribly: spasms ran across it. With inexpressible reproach she looked at him, meant to say something, but could not get out a word, and only suddenly began to sob bitterly, bitterly, covering her face with her hands.

“You say Katerina Ivanovna’s mind is getting confused; your own mind is getting confused,” he said after some silence.

About five minutes passed. He kept walking up and down, silently, without looking at her. At last he came up to her; his eyes were flashing. He took her by the shoulders with both hands and looked straight into her weeping face. His look was dry, inflamed, piercing; his lips were trembling violently... Suddenly he bent down quickly with his whole body and, sinking to the floor, kissed her foot. Sonya recoiled from him in horror, as from a madman. And indeed he looked quite like a madman.

“What are you doing, what are you doing? Before me!” she muttered, turning pale, and suddenly her heart was wrung painfully, painfully.

He stood up at once.

“I did not bow down to you; I bowed down to all human suffering,” he said somehow wildly, and went off to the window. “Listen,” he added, coming back to her a minute later, “I told a certain insulting fellow today that he wasn’t worth your little finger... and that I did my sister an honour today by seating her beside you.”

“Ah, why did you tell them that! And in front of her?” Sonya cried in fright. “To sit with me! An honour! Why, I am... dishonoured... I am a great, great sinner! Ah, why did you say that!”

“It was not for your dishonour and your sin that I said it of you, but for your great suffering. And that you are a great sinner, that is so,” he added almost rapturously, “and above all you are a sinner because you have destroyed and betrayed yourself for nothing. Isn’t that a horror! Isn’t it a horror that you live in this filth which you hate so much, and at the same time you know yourself (you have only to open your eyes) that you are not helping anyone by it, and are not saving anyone from anything! But tell me at last,” he said, almost in a frenzy, “how can such shame and such baseness live in you side by side with other feelings, opposite and holy? Why, it would be more just, a thousand times more just and more reasonable, to go head first into the water and end it all at once!”

“And what will become of them?” Sonya asked weakly, looking at him in suffering, but at the same time as if not at all surprised by his suggestion. Raskolnikov looked at her strangely.

He read everything in that one look of hers. So she herself really had already had this thought. Perhaps many times and seriously she had considered in despair how to end it all at once, and so seriously that now she was hardly surprised at his suggestion. She did not even notice the cruelty of his words (the meaning of his reproaches and his particular view of her shame she of course did not notice either, and that was visible to him). But he understood fully to what monstrous pain the thought of her dishonourable and shameful position had tormented her, and for a long time. What, what could have held back until now, he thought, her resolve to end it all at once? And only then did he understand fully what these poor little orphaned children meant to her, and this pitiful, half-mad Katerina Ivanovna, with her consumption and her beating her head against the wall.

But nonetheless it was again clear to him that Sonya, with her character and with the education, such as it was, that she had received, could not in any case go on like this. Still it was a question for him: how could she have stayed in such a position so very long without going out of her mind, if she did not have the strength to throw herself into the water? Of course he understood that Sonya’s position was an accidental phenomenon in society, though unfortunately far from solitary or exceptional. But that very accidentalness, that certain degree of education and her whole previous life might, it seems, have killed her at once, at the first step on that loathsome road. What, then, sustained her? Not depravity, surely? This shame had obviously touched her only mechanically; real depravity had not yet penetrated a single drop into her heart: he saw that; she stood before him in reality...

“There are three roads for her,” he thought: “to throw herself into the canal, to end up in a madhouse, or... or, at last, to throw herself into depravity, which stupefies the mind and turns the heart to stone.” The last thought was the most repulsive of all to him; but he was already a sceptic, he was young, abstract and therefore cruel, and so he could not help believing that the last way out, that is, depravity, was the most likely of all.

“But can this really be true,” he exclaimed to himself, “can this creature too, who has still kept her purity of spirit, be drawn consciously at last into that vile, stinking pit? Has this drawing-in already begun, and is it only because of that that she has been able to endure until now, because vice no longer seems so repulsive to her? No, no, it cannot be!” he exclaimed, as Sonya had earlier. “No, what has kept her from the canal until now is the thought of sin, and they, those... And if she has not yet gone out of her mind... But who said that she has not already gone out of her mind? Is she in her right mind? Can a person talk as she does? Can anyone in her right mind reason as she does? Can one sit over one’s own ruin like this, right over the stinking pit into which one is already being drawn, and wave one’s hands and stop one’s ears when one is told of the danger? What is she waiting for, a miracle? Surely that is it. Aren’t all these signs of madness?”

He fixed stubbornly on this thought. He even liked this way out better than any other. He began to look at her more intently.

“So you pray to God a great deal, Sonya?” he asked her.

Sonya was silent; he stood beside her and waited for an answer.

“What would I be without God?” she whispered quickly and energetically, glancing up at him for a moment with suddenly flashing eyes, and she pressed his hand tightly with hers.

“Well, so it is!” he thought.

“And what does God do for you in return?” he asked, probing further.

Sonya was silent for a long time, as if she could not answer. Her weak little breast heaved all over with agitation.

“Be quiet! Don’t ask! You are not worthy!..” she cried suddenly, looking at him sternly and angrily.

“So it is! so it is!” he repeated insistently to himself.

“He does everything!” she whispered quickly, looking down again.

“There is the way out! There is the explanation of the way out!” he decided to himself, examining her with greedy curiosity.

With a new, strange, almost painful feeling he gazed at that pale, thin, irregular, angular little face, at those gentle blue eyes that could flash with such fire, with such stern, energetic feeling, at that small body still trembling with indignation and anger, and all this seemed to him more and more strange, almost impossible. “A holy fool! a holy fool!” he kept saying to himself.

On the chest of drawers lay some book. Each time he passed up and down he had noticed it; now he picked it up and looked. It was the New Testament in Russian translation. The book was old, well used, in a leather binding.

“Where is this from?” he called to her across the room. She was still standing in the same place, three steps from the table.

“It was brought to me,” she answered, as if reluctantly and without looking at him.

“Who brought it?”

“Lizaveta brought it; I asked her to.”

“Lizaveta! Strange!” he thought. Everything about Sonya was becoming somehow stranger and more wonderful to him with every minute. He carried the book over to the candle and began turning the pages.

“Where is the part about Lazarus?” he asked suddenly.

Sonya looked stubbornly at the floor and did not answer. She was standing a little sideways to the table.

“Where is the raising of Lazarus? Find it for me, Sonya.”

She glanced at him sideways.

“You are not looking in the right place... in the fourth gospel...” she whispered sternly, without moving towards him.

“Find it and read it to me,” he said, sat down, leaned his elbows on the table, propped his head on his hand and stared gloomily to one side, ready to listen.

“In three weeks’ time, out at the seventh verst, you’re welcome! I think I shall be there myself, if nothing worse happens,” he muttered to himself.

Sonya stepped hesitantly to the table, having listened mistrustfully to Raskolnikov’s strange wish. She took the book, however.

“Haven’t you read it?” she asked, glancing at him across the table, from under her brows. Her voice was growing sterner and sterner.

“Long ago... When I was at school. Read!”

“And haven’t you heard it in church?”

“I... didn’t go. Do you go often?”

“N-no,” whispered Sonya.

Raskolnikov smiled.

“I understand... And so you won’t go to bury your father tomorrow?”

“I will. I was there last week too... I had a requiem sung.”

“For whom?”

“For Lizaveta. She was killed with an axe.”

His nerves were growing more and more irritated. His head began to spin.

“You were friends with Lizaveta?”

“Yes... She was just... she used to come... rarely... it wasn’t possible. We used to read together and... talk. She will see God.”

These bookish words sounded strange to him, and here was something new again: some mysterious meetings with Lizaveta, and both of them holy fools.

“One could become a holy fool oneself here! It’s catching!” he thought. “Read!” he exclaimed suddenly, insistently and irritably.

Sonya still hesitated. Her heart was pounding. Somehow she did not dare to read to him. Almost in torment he looked at the “unhappy madwoman”.

“What do you want it for? You don’t believe, do you?..” she whispered quietly and somehow breathlessly.

“Read! I want you to!” he insisted. “You read to Lizaveta!”

Sonya opened the book and found the place. Her hands were trembling; her voice failed her. Twice she began, and still the first syllable would not come out.

“Now a certain man was sick, named Lazarus, of Bethany...” she uttered at last, with an effort, but suddenly, at the third word, her voice rang and broke like a string stretched too tight. Her breath caught and her chest tightened.

Raskolnikov understood in part why Sonya could not bring herself to read to him, and the more he understood it, the more roughly and irritably, as it were, he insisted on the reading. He understood only too well how hard it was for her now to give away and expose everything that was her own. He understood that these feelings really did form, as it were, her real and long-standing secret, perhaps since her girlhood, back in the family, beside her unhappy father and her stepmother driven mad by grief, among hungry children, hideous shouts and reproaches. But at the same time he knew now, and knew for certain, that though she was full of anguish and terribly afraid of something as she began to read, yet at the same time she herself tormentingly wanted to read, in spite of all her anguish and all her fears, and to him precisely, so that he would hear it, and certainly now, “whatever might come of it afterwards!”... He read this in her eyes, understood it from her rapturous agitation... She mastered herself, suppressed the spasm in her throat that had cut off her voice at the beginning of the verse, and went on reading the eleventh chapter of the Gospel of John. So she read on to the nineteenth verse:

“And many of the Jews came to Martha and Mary, to comfort them concerning their brother. Then Martha, as soon as she heard that Jesus was coming, went and met him: but Mary sat still in the house. Then said Martha unto Jesus, Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died. But I know, that even now, whatsoever thou wilt ask of God, God will give it thee.”

Here she stopped again, foreseeing with shame that her voice would tremble and break again...

“Jesus saith unto her, Thy brother shall rise again. Martha saith unto him, I know that he shall rise again in the resurrection at the last day. Jesus said unto her, I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live: and whosoever liveth and believeth in me shall never die. Believest thou this? She saith unto him,”

(and drawing breath as if in pain, Sonya read distinctly and forcefully, as if she herself were confessing before all the world):

“Yea, Lord: I believe that thou art the Christ, the Son of God, which should come into the world.”

She was about to stop, quickly raised her eyes to him, but hastily mastered herself and went on reading. Raskolnikov sat and listened motionless, not turning round, his elbows on the table, looking to one side. They read on to the thirty-second verse.

“Then when Mary was come where Jesus was, and saw him, she fell down at his feet, saying unto him, Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died. When Jesus therefore saw her weeping, and the Jews also weeping which came with her, he groaned in the spirit, and was troubled, and said, Where have ye laid him? They said unto him, Lord, come and see. Jesus wept. Then said the Jews, Behold how he loved him! And some of them said, Could not this man, which opened the eyes of the blind, have caused that even this man should not have died?”

Raskolnikov turned to her and looked at her with agitation: yes, so it was! She was already trembling all over in a real, genuine fever. He had expected it. She was approaching the word about the greatest and unheard-of miracle, and a feeling of great triumph took hold of her. Her voice grew ringing, like metal; triumph and joy rang in it and strengthened it. The lines swam before her, because her eyes were darkening, but she knew by heart what she was reading. At the last verse, “Could not this man, which opened the eyes of the blind...”, she lowered her voice and conveyed hotly and passionately the doubt, the reproach and the blasphemy of the unbelieving, blind Jews, who in a moment, in a minute, would fall as if struck by thunder, sob and believe... “And he, he too is blinded and unbelieving; he too will hear in a moment, he too will believe, yes, yes! at once, right now,” she dreamed, and she trembled with joyful expectation.

“Jesus therefore again groaning in himself cometh to the grave. It was a cave, and a stone lay upon it. Jesus said, Take ye away the stone. Martha, the sister of him that was dead, saith unto him, Lord, by this time he stinketh: for he hath been dead four days.”

She struck the word four with energy.

“Jesus saith unto her, Said I not unto thee, that, if thou wouldest believe, thou shouldest see the glory of God? Then they took away the stone from the place where the dead was laid. And Jesus lifted up his eyes, and said, Father, I thank thee that thou hast heard me. And I knew that thou hearest me always: but because of the people which stand by I said it, that they may believe that thou hast sent me. And when he thus had spoken, he cried with a loud voice, Lazarus, come forth. And he that was dead came forth,”

(she read loudly and rapturously, trembling and turning cold, as if she were seeing it with her own eyes):

“bound hand and foot with graveclothes: and his face was bound about with a napkin. Jesus saith unto them, Loose him, and let him go. Then many of the Jews which came to Mary, and had seen the things which Jesus did, believed on him.”

Further she did not read, and could not read; she closed the book and quickly got up from her chair.

“That is all about the raising of Lazarus,” she whispered abruptly and sternly, and stood motionless, turned aside, not daring, and as if ashamed, to raise her eyes to him. Her feverish trembling still went on. The candle end had long been guttering in the bent candlestick, dimly lighting up in this beggarly room the murderer and the harlot, strangely come together over the reading of the eternal book. Five minutes or more passed.

“I came to talk about something,” Raskolnikov said suddenly, loudly and frowning; he stood up and went over to Sonya. She raised her eyes to him in silence. His look was especially stern, and a kind of wild resolve was expressed in it.

“Today I abandoned my family,” he said, “my mother and my sister. I will not go to them now. I have broken off everything there.”

“Why?” Sonya asked, as if stunned. The meeting with his mother and sister earlier had left an extraordinary impression on her, though one unclear even to herself. She heard the news of the break almost with horror.

“Now I have only you,” he added. “Let us go together... I have come to you. We are both cursed; let us go together!”

His eyes were flashing. “Like a half-wit!” Sonya thought in her turn.

“Go where?” she asked in fear, and involuntarily stepped back.

“How should I know? I only know that it is the same road, I know that for certain, and nothing more. One goal!”

She looked at him and understood nothing. She understood only that he was terribly, infinitely unhappy.

“None of them will understand anything, if you talk to them,” he went on, “but I have understood. I need you; that is why I have come to you.”

“I don’t understand...” whispered Sonya.

“You will understand later. Haven’t you done the same thing? You too have stepped over... you were able to step over. You have laid hands on yourself, you have destroyed a life... your own (it is all the same!). You could have lived by spirit and reason, and you will end up on the Haymarket... But you can’t bear it, and if you are left alone you will go out of your mind, like me. You are like a madwoman even now; so we must go together, by the same road! Let us go!”

“Why? Why are you saying this!” said Sonya, strangely and rebelliously stirred by his words.

“Why? Because one cannot stay like this, that is why! One must reason seriously and directly at last, and not cry like a child and shout that God won’t allow it! Well, what will happen if you really are taken to the hospital tomorrow? She is out of her mind and consumptive, she will die soon, and the children? Won’t Polechka be ruined? Haven’t you seen children here, at the street corners, whose mothers send them out to beg? I have found out where these mothers live and in what surroundings. There children cannot remain children. There a seven-year-old is depraved and a thief. And yet children are the image of Christ: ‘For of such is the kingdom of God.’ He commanded us to honour and love them; they are the humanity of the future...”

“What, then, what is to be done?” Sonya repeated, weeping hysterically and wringing her hands.

“What is to be done? Break what must be broken, once and for all, and that’s all: and take the suffering on yourself! What? You don’t understand? You will understand later... Freedom and power, and above all power! Over all trembling creatures and over the whole anthill!.. That is the goal! Remember it! That is my parting word to you! Perhaps I am speaking to you for the last time. If I don’t come tomorrow, you will hear about everything yourself, and then remember these words of now. And some day, later, years later, with life, perhaps you will understand what they meant. But if I come tomorrow, I will tell you who killed Lizaveta. Goodbye!”

Sonya shuddered all over with fright.

“But do you know who killed her?” she asked, going cold with horror and looking at him wildly.

“I know and I will tell... You, only you! I have chosen you. I am not coming to you to ask forgiveness; I will simply tell you. I chose you long ago to tell you this; already then, when your father talked about you and Lizaveta was alive, I thought of it. Goodbye. Don’t give me your hand. Tomorrow!”

He went out. Sonya looked at him as at a madman; but she herself was as if out of her mind and felt it. Her head was spinning. “Lord! how does he know who killed Lizaveta? What did those words mean? It is frightening!” But at the same time the thought did not come into her head. Not at all! Not at all!.. “Oh, he must be terribly unhappy!.. He has abandoned his mother and sister. Why? What happened? And what does he intend? What was he saying to her? He kissed her foot and said... said (yes, he said it clearly) that he cannot live without her any more... Oh Lord!”

Sonya spent the whole night in fever and delirium. At times she jumped up, wept, wrung her hands, then sank again into a feverish sleep, and she dreamed of Polechka, Katerina Ivanovna, Lizaveta, the reading of the Gospel, and him... him, with his pale face, with his burning eyes... He kisses her feet, he weeps... Oh Lord!

Behind the door on the right, behind that very door which separated Sonya’s lodging from the lodging of Gertrude Karlovna Resslich, there was an intermediate room, long empty, belonging to Madame Resslich’s flat and offered by her for rent, for which notices had been put up on the gate and little papers stuck on the window panes looking out onto the canal. Sonya had long been used to regarding this room as uninhabited. And yet, all this time, Mr Svidrigailov had been standing at the door in the empty room, hidden, eavesdropping. When Raskolnikov went out, he stood for a while, thought, went on tiptoe to his own room, next to the empty one, fetched a chair and noiselessly carried it right up to the door leading into Sonya’s room. He had found the conversation entertaining and significant, and he liked it very, very much: so much that he even carried the chair over, so that in future, tomorrow for instance, he would not again be subjected to the unpleasantness of standing for a whole hour on his feet, but could settle himself more comfortably, so as to get complete satisfaction in every respect.
