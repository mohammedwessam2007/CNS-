## V

“So this is it, so this is it at last, the clash with reality,” I muttered, rushing headlong down the stairs. “This is no Pope leaving Rome for Brazil, it seems; this is no ball on Lake Como!”

“You are a scoundrel,” flashed through my head, “if you can laugh at this now.”

“So be it!” I shouted, answering myself. “Everything is lost now anyway!”

They had vanished without a trace; but no matter: I knew where they had gone.

At the entrance stood a solitary night cabby, a poor one, in a coarse peasant coat, all powdered with the wet and, as it seemed, warm snow that was still falling. It was steamy and close. His little shaggy, piebald nag was all powdered with snow too, and coughing; I remember that very well. I rushed for the bast-matted sledge; but as soon as I raised my foot to get in, the memory of how Simonov had just given me six roubles knocked my legs from under me, and I collapsed into the sledge like a sack.

“No! Much must be done to redeem all this!” I shouted. “But I will redeem it, or perish on the spot this very night. Drive on!”

We set off. A whole whirlwind was spinning in my head.

“They won’t go down on their knees and beg for my friendship. That is a mirage, a vulgar mirage, disgusting, romantic and fantastic: the same ball on Lake Como. And so I must slap Zverkov’s face! I am obliged to. So it is decided; I am flying now to slap his face.”

“Faster!”

The cabby tugged at the reins.

“As soon as I go in, I’ll slap him. Should I say a few words before the slap, by way of preface? No! I’ll simply go in and slap him. They will all be sitting in the drawing room, and he on the sofa with Olympia. Damned Olympia! She once laughed at my face and refused me. I’ll drag Olympia by the hair, and Zverkov by the ears! No, better by one ear, and I’ll lead him by the ear all round the room. Perhaps they will all start beating me and throw me out. That is even certain. So be it! All the same, I will have given the slap first: the initiative is mine; and by the laws of honour that is everything; he is branded already, and no beating can wash the slap off him, only a duel. He will have to fight. And let them beat me now. Let them, the ungrateful wretches! Trudolyubov will beat me hardest: he is so strong. Ferfichkin will hang on from the side, and certainly by my hair. But let them, let them! That is what I came for. Their sheep’s heads will be forced at last to grasp the tragedy in all this! When they drag me to the door, I will shout to them that in reality they are not worth my little finger.”

“Faster, driver, faster!” I shouted at the cabby.

He even started and cracked his whip. I had shouted very wildly.

“We fight at dawn; that is decided. The office is finished. Ferfichkin said ‘de-part-ment’ just now, mocking me. But where do I get pistols? Nonsense! I’ll get an advance on my salary and buy them. And powder, and bullets? That’s the second’s business. And how can it all be done by dawn? And where will I find a second? I have no acquaintances...”

“Nonsense!” I shouted, whirling myself up still more. “Nonsense!

“The first person I meet in the street whom I ask is obliged to be my second, just as he would be obliged to pull a drowning man out of the water. The most eccentric cases must be allowed. Why, if I were to ask the director himself to be my second tomorrow, even he would have to agree, from a sense of chivalry alone, and keep it secret! Anton Antonych...”

The fact is that at that very moment the whole vile absurdity of my plans, and the other side of the coin, was clearer and more vivid to me than to anyone else in the whole world, but...

“Faster, driver, faster, you rascal, faster!”

“Ech, master!” said the voice of the people.

A sudden chill came over me.

“Wouldn’t it be better... wouldn’t it be better... to go straight home now? Oh my God! Why, why did I offer myself for this dinner yesterday! But no, it’s impossible! And the three hours’ walk from the table to the stove? No, they, and no one else, must pay me for that walk! They must wash away this dishonour!”

“Faster!”

“And what if they hand me over to the police? They won’t dare! They’ll be afraid of a scandal. And what if Zverkov refuses the duel out of contempt? That is even certain; but then I’ll show them... I’ll rush to the posting station tomorrow when he is leaving; I’ll seize him by the leg; I’ll tear his coat off him as he climbs into the carriage. I’ll sink my teeth into his hand; I’ll bite him. ‘Look, all of you, what a desperate man can be driven to!’ Let him beat me on the head, and all of them from behind. I’ll shout to the whole public: ‘Look, here is a young puppy going off to captivate the Circassian girls with my spit on his face!’

“Of course, after that everything is finished! The office has vanished from the face of the earth. They’ll seize me, they’ll try me, they’ll dismiss me from the service, put me in prison, send me to Siberia, to a settlement. No matter! Fifteen years later, when they let me out of prison, I’ll drag myself after him in rags, a beggar. I’ll find him somewhere in a provincial town. He’ll be married and happy. He’ll have a grown-up daughter... I’ll say: ‘Look, monster, look at my sunken cheeks and my rags! I have lost everything, my career, my happiness, art, science, the woman I loved, and all because of you. Here are pistols. I have come to fire my pistol and... and I forgive you.’ And then I’ll fire into the air, and nothing more will ever be heard of me...”

I even began to cry, though I knew perfectly well at that very moment that all this came from Pushkin’s *Silvio* and Lermontov’s *Masquerade*. And suddenly I felt terribly ashamed, so ashamed that I stopped the horse, got out of the sledge and stood in the snow in the middle of the street. The cabby looked at me in amazement, sighing.

What was I to do? I could not go there: it would be nonsense; and I could not leave it at that, because then it would come out... Lord! How could I leave it at that! And after such insults!

“No!” I cried, throwing myself into the sledge again. “It is predestined, it is fate! Faster, faster, there!”

And in my impatience I hit the cabby on the neck with my fist.

“Hey, what’s that for, why are you hitting me?” cried the little peasant, but he whipped up his nag all the same, so that it began kicking with its hind legs.

The wet snow was falling in flakes; I unbuttoned my coat; I did not care about it. I forgot everything else, because I had finally decided on the slap and felt with horror that it was certain to happen now, at once, and that no power on earth could stop it. The deserted street lamps flickered gloomily in the snowy haze, like torches at a funeral. The snow had got under my overcoat, under my jacket, under my tie, and was melting there; I did not cover myself up: everything was lost anyway! At last we arrived. I jumped out almost beside myself, ran up the steps and began knocking on the door with my hands and feet. My legs, especially at the knees, had grown terribly weak. They opened quickly somehow, as though they knew I was coming. (Simonov had indeed warned them that one more might come, and here one had to give warning and take precautions in general. It was one of those “fashion shops” of those days, which have long since been wiped out by the police. By day it really was a shop; but in the evenings those who had an introduction could come as guests.) I walked quickly through the dark shop into the drawing room I knew, where only one candle was burning, and stopped in bewilderment: there was no one there.

“Where are they?” I asked someone.

But of course they had already dispersed...

Before me stood a person with a stupid smile, the madam herself, who knew me slightly. A minute later a door opened, and another person came in.

Paying no attention to anything, I paced the room and, I think, talked to myself. It was as if I had been saved from death, and I felt it joyfully with my whole being: for I would have slapped him; I would certainly, certainly have slapped him! But now they were not here, and... everything had vanished, everything had changed!.. I looked around. I could not yet take it in. Mechanically I glanced at the girl who had come in: before me flashed a fresh, young, rather pale face, with straight dark eyebrows and a serious, somewhat surprised look. I liked that at once; I would have hated her if she had smiled. I began to look at her more closely, as if with an effort: my thoughts had not yet all come together. There was something simple and kind in that face, but somehow strangely serious. I am sure that this told against her here, and none of those fools had noticed her. She could not be called a beauty, though; but she was tall, strong and well built. She was dressed extremely simply. Something nasty stung me; I went straight up to her...

I happened to look at myself in the mirror. My agitated face seemed to me utterly repulsive: pale, spiteful, mean, with dishevelled hair. “So be it; I am glad of it,” I thought. “I am glad precisely that I shall seem repulsive to her; it pleases me...”
