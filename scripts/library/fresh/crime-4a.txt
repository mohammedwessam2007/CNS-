## CHAPTER VII

The door, as before, opened a tiny crack, and again two sharp, distrustful eyes stared at him out of the darkness. Here Raskolnikov lost his head and almost made a serious mistake.

Afraid that the old woman would be frightened at their being alone, and not hoping that his appearance would reassure her, he took hold of the door and pulled it towards him, so that the old woman should not somehow take it into her head to lock herself in again. Seeing this, she did not pull the door back towards her, but she did not let go of the handle either, so that he almost pulled her out onto the landing together with the door. Seeing that she was standing across the doorway and would not let him pass, he walked straight at her. She sprang back in fright, was about to say something, but seemed unable to, and stared at him wide-eyed.

“Good evening, Alyona Ivanovna,” he began, as casually as he could, but his voice would not obey him; it broke off and trembled. “I have... brought you a thing... but let us come over here... to the light...” And leaving her, he walked straight into the room without being asked. The old woman ran after him; her tongue was loosened.

“Lord! What do you want?.. Who are you? What do you want?”

“Excuse me, Alyona Ivanovna... you know me... Raskolnikov... here, I have brought the pledge I promised the other day...” And he held the pledge out to her.

The old woman glanced at the pledge, but at once fixed her eyes straight on the eyes of her uninvited visitor. She looked attentively, spitefully and distrustfully. A minute passed; he even thought he saw something like mockery in her eyes, as if she had already guessed everything. He felt that he was losing his head, that he was almost afraid, so afraid that it seemed to him that if she went on looking like that and did not say a word for another half minute, he would have run away from her.

“Why do you look at me like that, as if you did not recognise me?” he said suddenly, also with spite. “Take it if you want it, and if not I will go to someone else; I have no time.”

He had not even meant to say this; it simply came out of itself.

The old woman came to herself, and her visitor’s resolute tone evidently reassured her.

“But why so sudden, my dear... what is it?” she asked, looking at the pledge.

“A silver cigarette case: I told you last time.”

She held out her hand.

“But why are you so pale? Your hands are trembling too! Have you been bathing, my dear?”

“Fever,” he answered abruptly. “One can’t help being pale... when one has nothing to eat,” he added, hardly able to get the words out. His strength was leaving him again. But the answer seemed plausible; the old woman took the pledge.

“What is it?” she asked, looking Raskolnikov over intently once more and weighing the pledge in her hand.

“A thing... a cigarette case... silver... look.”

“But it doesn’t seem to be silver... My, how he has wrapped it up.”

Trying to untie the string and turning to the window, to the light (all her windows were shut, in spite of the stuffiness), she left him completely for a few seconds and stood with her back to him. He unbuttoned his coat and freed the axe from the loop, but did not take it out altogether yet; he only held it with his right hand under his clothes. His hands were terribly weak; he could feel them himself growing more and more numb and wooden with every moment. He was afraid he would let go of the axe and drop it... suddenly his head seemed to spin.

“What has he wrapped up here!” the old woman cried in annoyance, and moved towards him.

Not another moment could be lost. He took the axe right out, swung it up with both hands, hardly aware of himself, and almost without effort, almost mechanically, brought the butt of it down on her head. It was as if he had no strength. But as soon as he had once brought the axe down, the strength was born in him.

The old woman, as always, had nothing on her head. Her thin, fair hair, streaked with grey and thickly greased as usual, was plaited into a rat’s tail and pinned up under a broken horn comb that stuck out at the back of her head. The blow fell right on the crown of her head, which her small height made easier. She cried out, but very faintly, and suddenly sank all the way to the floor, though she still managed to raise both hands to her head. In one hand she still went on holding the “pledge”. Then he struck her with all his strength once and again, still with the butt and still on the crown. The blood gushed out as if from an overturned glass, and the body fell over on its back. He stepped back, let it fall, and at once bent down to her face; she was already dead. Her eyes were wide open, as if they wanted to jump out, and her forehead and her whole face were wrinkled and distorted by a spasm.

He put the axe on the floor beside the dead woman and at once reached into her pocket, trying not to stain himself with the flowing blood: the same right-hand pocket from which she had taken her keys last time. He was in full possession of his mind; there were no more blackouts or dizziness, but his hands were still trembling. He remembered afterwards that he had even been very attentive and careful, and kept trying not to get stained... He took out the keys at once; they were all, as before, in one bunch, on one steel ring. He ran with them at once into the bedroom. It was a very small room, with an enormous case of icons. Against another wall stood a big bed, very clean, with a silk patchwork quilt filled with wadding. Against the third wall was the chest of drawers. A strange thing: as soon as he began fitting the keys to the chest of drawers, as soon as he heard them jingle, a kind of convulsion seemed to pass through him. He suddenly wanted again to drop everything and go away. But that was only for a moment; it was too late to go away. He even smiled at himself, when suddenly another anxious thought struck him. It suddenly seemed to him that the old woman might still be alive and might still come to. Dropping the keys and the chest of drawers, he ran back to the body, seized the axe and swung it up once more over the old woman, but did not bring it down. There was no doubt that she was dead. Bending down and examining her more closely again, he saw clearly that the skull was smashed and even twisted a little to one side. He was about to feel it with his finger, but he snatched his hand back; it could be seen without that. Meanwhile a whole pool of blood had already flowed out. Suddenly he noticed a cord round her neck; he tugged at it, but the cord was strong and would not break; besides, it was soaked in blood. He tried to pull it out like that, from her bosom, but something was in the way, caught. In his impatience he raised the axe again to cut the cord right there, on the body, from above, but he did not dare, and with difficulty, smearing his hands and the axe, after two minutes’ fumbling, he cut the cord without touching the body with the axe, and took it off; he had not been mistaken: a purse. On the cord were two crosses, one of cypress wood and one of copper, and besides them an enamelled little icon; and hanging there with them was a small, greasy chamois-leather purse with a steel rim and ring. The purse was stuffed very full; Raskolnikov thrust it into his pocket without looking at it, threw the crosses on the old woman’s breast, and, taking the axe with him this time, rushed back into the bedroom.

He was in a terrible hurry; he seized the keys and began fumbling with them again. But somehow nothing went right: they would not go into the locks. It was not that his hands were trembling so much, but he kept making mistakes: he would see, for instance, that a key was the wrong one, that it did not fit, and still he kept pushing it in. Suddenly he remembered and realised that the big key with the notched bit, which was dangling there with the other little ones, could certainly not be for the chest of drawers at all (as had occurred to him last time too), but for some trunk, and that perhaps everything was hidden in that trunk. He left the chest of drawers and at once crawled under the bed, knowing that old women usually keep their trunks under their beds. Just so: there stood a sizeable trunk, more than a yard long, with a rounded lid, covered in red morocco and studded with steel nails. The notched key fitted exactly and opened it. On top, under a white sheet, lay a hareskin coat covered with red brocade; under it was a silk dress, then a shawl, and further down, it seemed, there was nothing but rags. First of all he began to wipe his bloodstained hands on the red brocade. “It’s red, and on red blood is less noticeable,” he reasoned, and suddenly he came to himself: “Lord! Am I going out of my mind?” he thought in fright.

But as soon as he stirred these rags, a gold watch suddenly slipped out from under the coat. He rushed to turn everything over. Indeed, gold things were mixed in among the rags, probably all pledges, redeemed and unredeemed: bracelets, chains, earrings, pins and so on. Some were in cases, others simply wrapped in newspaper, but neatly and carefully, in double sheets, and tied round with tape. Without the least delay he began stuffing his trouser pockets and his coat pockets with them, without sorting them or opening the packets and cases; but he did not have time to take many...

Suddenly he heard someone walking in the room where the old woman was. He stopped and kept still as a dead man. But all was quiet, so he must have imagined it. Suddenly he distinctly heard a faint cry, or as if someone had moaned softly and abruptly and fallen silent. Then again dead silence, for a minute or two. He sat crouching by the trunk and waited, hardly drawing breath, but suddenly he jumped up, seized the axe and ran out of the bedroom.

In the middle of the room stood Lizaveta, with a big bundle in her hands, gazing in a stupor at her murdered sister, as white as a sheet and as if unable to cry out. Seeing him run out, she trembled like a leaf, with a fine trembling, and spasms ran all over her face; she half raised her hand, opened her mouth, but still did not cry out, and slowly, backwards, began to move away from him into the corner, staring intently, point-blank, at him, but still not crying out, as if she had not enough air to cry out. He rushed at her with the axe; her lips were twisted so pitifully, like those of very small children when they begin to be frightened of something, stare at the thing that frightens them, and are about to scream. And this unhappy Lizaveta was so simple, so downtrodden and so frightened once and for all, that she did not even raise a hand to protect her face, though that was the most necessary and natural gesture at that moment, because the axe was raised right over her face. She only lifted her free left hand a very little, far short of her face, and slowly stretched it out towards him, as if to push him away. The blow fell right on her skull, with the blade, and at once split the whole upper part of her forehead, almost to the crown. She simply crashed down. Raskolnikov completely lost his head; he seized her bundle, threw it down again, and ran into the hall.

Fear was taking hold of him more and more, especially after this second, completely unexpected murder. He wanted to run away from here as fast as possible. And if at that moment he had been able to see and reason more clearly; if only he could have grasped all the difficulties of his position, all its despair, all its hideousness and absurdity, and understood at the same time how many obstacles, and perhaps even crimes, still remained for him to overcome and commit in order to get out of here and reach home, then it is very possible that he would have thrown up everything and at once gone to give himself up, and not even from fear for himself, but simply from horror and disgust at what he had done. The disgust especially rose and grew in him with every minute. Not for anything in the world would he have gone back to the trunk now, or even into the rooms.

But a kind of absent-mindedness, as if even a kind of reverie, began little by little to take hold of him: at moments he seemed to forget himself, or rather to forget the main thing, and to cling to trifles. However, glancing into the kitchen and seeing a bucket half full of water on a bench, he thought of washing his hands and the axe. His hands were covered in blood and sticky. He put the axe blade down straight into the water, seized a bit of soap that lay on the window sill on a cracked saucer, and began to wash his hands right there in the bucket. When he had washed them, he took out the axe too, washed the iron, and for a long time, about three minutes, washed the wood where it was bloodstained, even trying the blood with soap. Then he wiped it all with some linen that was drying there on a line stretched across the kitchen, and then for a long time, attentively, examined the axe at the window. There were no traces left; only the handle was still damp. He carefully put the axe into the loop, under his coat. Then, as far as the light in the dim kitchen allowed, he examined his coat, his trousers, his boots. On the outside, at first glance, there seemed to be nothing; only there were stains on his boots. He wetted a rag and wiped his boots. He knew, however, that he was not looking properly, that perhaps there was something that would catch the eye which he was not noticing. He stood thinking in the middle of the room. A tormenting, dark thought rose in him: the thought that he was going mad, and that at this moment he was unable either to reason or to protect himself, that perhaps he ought not to be doing at all what he was doing now... “My God! I must run, run!” he muttered, and rushed into the hall. But there such a horror awaited him as he had certainly never experienced before.

He stood, looked, and did not believe his eyes: the door, the outer door, from the hall to the stairs, the very door at which he had rung and come in a little while ago, stood unlocked, even open a whole hand’s breadth: neither locked nor bolted, all the time, all this time! The old woman had not locked it after him, perhaps out of caution. But God! He had seen Lizaveta afterwards! And how could he, how could he not have guessed that she must have come in from somewhere! Not through the wall.

He rushed to the door and put the bolt on.

“But no, that’s wrong again! I must go, go...”

He took off the bolt, opened the door and began listening on the stairs.

He listened for a long time. Somewhere far away, below, probably under the gateway, two voices were shouting loudly and shrilly, arguing and swearing. “What are they doing?..” He waited patiently. At last everything fell silent at once, as if cut off; they had gone their ways. He was about to go out, when suddenly on the floor below a door onto the stairs was flung open noisily, and someone began going down, humming a tune. “How they all make such a noise!” flashed through his head. He shut the door behind him again and waited. At last everything was silent, not a soul. He had already taken a step onto the stairs when suddenly again someone’s new footsteps were heard.

These steps sounded very far off, still at the very bottom of the stairs, but he remembered very well and distinctly that from the very first sound he had begun for some reason to suspect that they were certainly coming here, to the fourth floor, to the old woman’s. Why? Were the sounds so special, so significant? The steps were heavy, even, unhurried. Now he had passed the first floor, now he had climbed further; louder and louder! The heavy panting of the man coming up could be heard. Now the third floor had begun... Here! And suddenly it seemed to him that he had turned to stone, that it was just like a dream when one dreams that someone is chasing one, is near, wants to kill one, and one is rooted to the spot and cannot even move one’s arms.

And at last, when the visitor had already begun to climb to the fourth floor, only then did he suddenly start all over and managed after all to slip back quickly and deftly from the landing into the flat and shut the door behind him. Then he seized the bolt and quietly, soundlessly, slipped it into the catch. Instinct helped him. When he had done it all, he hid without breathing, right by the door. The uninvited visitor was at the door too. They stood now facing each other, as he and the old woman had a little while before, when the door separated them and he was listening.

The visitor panted heavily several times. “Fat and big, he must be,” thought Raskolnikov, gripping the axe in his hand. Indeed, it was exactly as if he were dreaming all this. The visitor seized the bell and rang hard.

As soon as the tinny sound of the bell jangled, it suddenly seemed to him that something stirred in the room. For a few seconds he even listened seriously. The stranger rang once more, waited again, and suddenly, impatiently, began tugging at the door handle with all his might. Raskolnikov watched in horror the hook of the bolt jumping in its catch, and with dull fear waited for the bolt to jump out any moment. Indeed, it seemed possible: they were tugging so hard. He thought of holding the bolt with his hand, but the man might guess. His head seemed to be beginning to spin again. “I shall fall!” flashed through him, but the stranger began to speak, and he came to himself at once.

“What are they doing in there, snoring, or has someone strangled them? Thrrrice-cursed women!” he bellowed as if from a barrel. “Hey, Alyona Ivanovna, you old witch! Lizaveta Ivanovna, you indescribable beauty! Open up! Ugh, curse them, are they asleep, or what?”

And again, in a fury, he pulled the bell ten times running with all his might. He was certainly a man of authority and on familiar terms in the house.

At that very moment light, hurried steps were suddenly heard not far off on the stairs. Someone else was coming up. Raskolnikov did not hear him at first.

“Is there really no one there?” the newcomer called out in a ringing, cheerful voice, addressing the first visitor directly, who was still tugging at the bell. “Good evening, Koch!”

“From his voice, he must be very young,” Raskolnikov suddenly thought.

“The devil knows what’s going on; I’ve almost broken the lock,” answered Koch. “But how do you know me?”

“Well, really! Why, the day before yesterday at the Gambrinus I took three games running off you at billiards!”

“A-a-ah...”

“So they’re not in? Strange. Terribly stupid, though. Where could the old woman have gone? I have business with her.”

“And so have I, my dear fellow, business!”

“Well, what can we do? We’ll have to go back. Eh-eh! And I was hoping to get some money!” cried the young man.

“Of course, go back; but why make an appointment? She fixed the time herself, the witch. It’s out of my way, too. And where the devil should she go traipsing about, I don’t understand? The witch sits there all year round going sour, her legs ache, and then suddenly she’s off on an outing!”

“Shouldn’t we ask the caretaker?”

“Ask what?”

“Where she has gone and when she’ll be back?”

“Hm... devil... ask... But she never goes anywhere...” And he tugged at the door handle once more. “Devil, nothing to be done, we’ll have to go!”

“Wait!” the young man suddenly cried. “Look: do you see how the door gives when you pull it?”

“Well?”

“That means it isn’t locked, it’s bolted, on the hook, that is! Do you hear the bolt rattle?”

“Well?”

“Why, don’t you understand? That means one of them is at home. If they had all gone out, they would have locked it with a key from outside, not bolted it from inside. But here... do you hear the bolt rattle? And to bolt the door from inside, you have to be at home, do you understand? So they are at home, but they won’t open!”

“Bah! Why, so it is!” cried the astonished Koch. “So what are they doing in there!” And he began tugging at the door furiously.

“Wait!” the young man cried again. “Don’t pull at it! There’s something wrong here... you rang, you pulled, and they don’t open; that means either they have both fainted, or...”

“What?”

“I’ll tell you what: let’s go for the caretaker; let him wake them himself.”

“Good idea!” Both started down.

“Wait! You stay here, and I’ll run down for the caretaker.”

“Why should I stay?”

“Well, you never know?..”

“All right...”

“I’m training to be an examining magistrate, you know! There is obviously, ob-vi-ous-ly something wrong here!” the young man cried hotly, and ran off down the stairs.

Koch stayed, moved the bell once more, softly, and it gave one jangle; then quietly, as if reflecting and examining, he began to move the door handle, pulling it towards him and letting it go, to make sure once more that it was only bolted. Then, puffing, he bent down and began looking through the keyhole; but the key was in it on the inside, so nothing could be seen.

Raskolnikov stood gripping the axe. He was as if in delirium. He was even preparing to fight them when they came in. While they were knocking and talking it over, the thought had suddenly come to him several times to finish it all at once and shout to them through the door. At times he wanted to begin swearing at them, teasing them, until they opened the door. “If only it would be soon!” flashed through his head.

“But he, devil take it...”

Time passed, a minute, another; no one came. Koch began to fidget.

“Devil take it, though!..” he suddenly shouted, and, impatient, abandoned his watch and went down too, hurrying and stamping down the stairs in his boots. The steps died away.

“Lord, what am I to do!”

Raskolnikov took off the bolt and opened the door a little: nothing could be heard; and suddenly, no longer thinking at all, he went out, shut the door behind him as tightly as he could, and set off downstairs.

He had already gone down three flights when suddenly there was a loud noise below: where could he go! There was nowhere to hide. He started to run back, into the flat again.

“Hey, you devil, damn you! Stop him!”

With a shout someone burst out of some flat below and not so much ran as fell down the stairs, shouting at the top of his voice:

“Mitka! Mitka! Mitka! Mitka! Mitka! The devil take you-ou-ou!”

The shout ended in a shriek; the last sounds were already heard from the yard; everything fell silent. But at that same moment several men, talking loudly and fast, began to come noisily up the stairs. There were three or four of them. He made out the young man’s ringing voice. “It’s them!”

In utter despair he went straight down to meet them: come what may! If they stop me, all is lost; if they let me pass, all is lost too: they will remember me. They were already drawing near; only one flight was left between them, and suddenly, salvation! A few steps from him, on the right, an empty flat, wide open, the same flat on the second floor where the workmen had been painting, and now, as if on purpose, they had gone. It must have been they who had just run out with such shouting. The floors had just been painted; in the middle of the room stood a little tub and a crock with paint and a brush. In an instant he slipped through the open door and hid behind the wall, and just in time: they were already standing on the landing. Then they turned upward and went past, to the fourth floor, talking loudly. He waited, came out on tiptoe and ran downstairs.

No one on the stairs! Nor under the gateway. He walked quickly through the archway and turned left along the street.

He knew very well, he knew perfectly well that at that moment they were already in the flat, that they had been very surprised to find it unlocked when it had been locked just before, that they were already looking at the bodies, and that not more than a minute would pass before they guessed and fully understood that the murderer had only just been there and had managed to hide somewhere, slip past them, and run away; perhaps they would even guess that he had been sitting in the empty flat while they went up. And yet on no account did he dare quicken his pace much, though there were still about a hundred paces to the first turning. “Shouldn’t I slip into some gateway and wait somewhere on a strange staircase? No, disaster! Shouldn’t I throw the axe away somewhere? Shouldn’t I take a cab? Disaster! Disaster!”

At last here was the lane; he turned into it half dead; here he was already half saved, and he knew it: less suspicion, and besides, there were a lot of people bustling about here, and he was lost among them like a grain of sand. But all these torments had so weakened him that he could hardly move. Sweat was pouring off him in drops; his neck was all wet. “Look at him, he’s had a skinful!” someone shouted at him when he came out onto the canal.

He hardly remembered himself now; the further he went, the worse it was. He remembered, however, how suddenly, coming out onto the canal, he was frightened that there were few people there and that he would be more noticeable, and wanted to turn back into the lane. Although he was almost falling, he still made a detour and came home from quite another side.

He was not fully conscious as he went through the gateway of his house either; at least he was already on the stairs before he remembered the axe. And yet a very important task lay before him: to put it back, and as unnoticeably as possible. Of course, he was no longer able to realise that it might perhaps have been much better for him not to put the axe back in its old place at all, but to throw it, even later, into some strange yard.

But it all went off well. The door of the caretaker’s room was shut, but not locked, so it was most likely that the caretaker was in. But he had so lost the ability to think anything out that he went straight up to the caretaker’s room and opened the door. If the caretaker had asked him, “What do you want?”, he might perhaps simply have handed him the axe. But again the caretaker was not there, and he managed to put the axe back in its old place under the bench; he even covered it with a log as before. He met no one, not a single soul, after that all the way to his room; the landlady’s door was shut. Going into his room, he threw himself on the sofa just as he was. He did not sleep, but he was in a kind of trance. If anyone had come into his room then, he would have jumped up at once and screamed. Scraps and fragments of thoughts swarmed in his head; but he could not catch hold of a single one, could not stop at a single one, in spite of all his efforts...
