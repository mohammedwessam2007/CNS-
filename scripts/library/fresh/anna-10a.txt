## Part Two · Chapter XXV

Seventeen officers in all were riding. The race was to be run on the big elliptical course, nearly three miles round, in front of the pavilion. Nine obstacles had been set up on this course: the river; a big solid barrier, nearly five feet high, right in front of the pavilion; a dry ditch; a ditch with water; a steep slope; an Irish bank, which was one of the hardest obstacles, consisting of a mound stuck with brushwood, behind which, invisible to the horse, there was another ditch, so that the horse had to clear both obstacles or kill itself; then two more ditches with water and one dry one; and the finish was opposite the pavilion. But the race did not start on the course; it started some two hundred yards to one side of it, and on that stretch was the first obstacle, a dammed river seven feet wide, which the riders could jump or ford as they chose.

Three times or so the riders were brought into line, but each time someone’s horse pushed forward, and they had to go back and begin again. The expert starter, Colonel Sestrin, was already beginning to get angry when at last, the fourth time, he shouted “Go!” and the riders set off.

All eyes, all field-glasses, were turned on the bright little cluster of riders while they were being lined up.

“They’re off! They’re racing!” was heard from all sides after the silence of waiting.

And groups and solitary walkers began running from place to place to see better. In the very first minute the bunched-up cluster of riders drew out, and one could see them approaching the river in twos and threes and one behind another. To the spectators it seemed that they had all started together; but for the riders there were seconds of difference that meant a great deal to them.

Frou-Frou, excited and over-nervous, lost the first moment, and several horses got off before her; but before they reached the river Vronsky, holding back with all his strength the horse, which was pulling on the reins, easily passed three of them, and in front of him there remained only Makhotin’s chestnut Gladiator, whose hindquarters rose and fell evenly and lightly just in front of Vronsky, and, ahead of everyone, the lovely Diana, carrying Kuzovlev, more dead than alive.

In the first minutes Vronsky was master neither of himself nor of his horse. Until the first obstacle, the river, he could not direct the horse’s movements.

Gladiator and Diana came up to it together and almost at the same moment: one, two, they rose over the river and flew across to the other side; imperceptibly, as if flying, Frou-Frou soared up after them; but at the very moment when Vronsky felt himself in the air, he suddenly saw, almost under his horse’s feet, Kuzovlev floundering with Diana on the other side of the river (Kuzovlev had let go of the reins after the jump, and the horse had gone head over heels with him). These details Vronsky learned only afterwards; now he saw only that just where Frou-Frou must land, Diana’s leg or head might be in the way. But Frou-Frou, like a falling cat, made an effort with her legs and back in the middle of the jump and, clearing the other horse, raced on.

“Oh, you darling!” thought Vronsky.

After the river Vronsky had complete mastery of his horse, and began to hold her back, meaning to take the big barrier behind Makhotin and then, on the following stretch of some four hundred yards without obstacles, to try to pass him.

The big barrier stood right in front of the Imperial pavilion. The Emperor, and the whole court, and crowds of people, all were looking at them, at him and at Makhotin a length ahead of him, as they came up to “the devil” (as the solid barrier was called). Vronsky felt these eyes turned on him from all sides, but he saw nothing except the ears and neck of his horse, the ground running towards him, and the hindquarters and white legs of Gladiator, quickly beating time in front of him and staying always the same distance away. Gladiator rose without touching anything, flicked his short tail, and vanished from Vronsky’s sight.

“Bravo!” said a single voice.

At that same instant the planks of the barrier flashed before Vronsky’s eyes, right in front of him. Without the slightest change in her movement the horse soared up under him; the planks disappeared, and only something knocked behind. Excited by Gladiator going ahead, the horse had risen too early before the barrier and struck it with a hind hoof. But her pace did not change, and Vronsky, getting a lump of mud in his face, understood that he was again the same distance behind Gladiator. Again he saw ahead of him his hindquarters, his short tail, and again those same quickly moving white legs that came no nearer.

At the very instant when Vronsky thought that now he must pass Makhotin, Frou-Frou herself, understanding what he had thought, without any urging, put on speed considerably and began closing on Makhotin on the most favourable side, the side of the rope. Makhotin would not give up the rope. Vronsky had only thought that he could also pass on the outside, when Frou-Frou changed her leg and began passing in just that way. Frou-Frou’s shoulder, already beginning to darken with sweat, came level with Gladiator’s hindquarters. For a few strides they went side by side. But before the obstacle they were coming to, Vronsky, so as not to take the wide circle, began working the reins, and quickly, on the very slope, passed Makhotin. He caught a glimpse of his face, spattered with mud. It even seemed to him that he smiled. Vronsky had passed Makhotin, but he felt him right behind him, and never stopped hearing at his very back the even stride and the abrupt, still quite fresh breathing of Gladiator’s nostrils.

The next two obstacles, a ditch and a barrier, were taken easily, but Vronsky began to hear Gladiator’s snorting and stride closer. He urged on his horse, and felt with joy that she easily increased her pace, and the sound of Gladiator’s hoofs was heard again at the same distance as before.

Vronsky was leading the race, which was just what he had wanted to do and what Cord had advised him, and now he was sure of success. His excitement, his joy and his tenderness for Frou-Frou grew stronger and stronger. He longed to look round, but he did not dare, and tried to calm himself and not to urge his horse, so as to keep in her a reserve equal to the one he felt was left in Gladiator. There remained one obstacle, and the hardest; if he cleared it ahead of the others, he would come in first. He was galloping up to the Irish bank. Together with Frou-Frou he saw this bank from far off, and to both of them, to him and the horse, came a momentary doubt. He noticed hesitation in the horse’s ears and raised his whip, but at once felt that the doubt was groundless: the horse knew what was needed. She put on speed and, evenly, just as he had expected, soared up, and, pushing off from the ground, gave herself up to the force of her momentum, which carried her far over the ditch; and in the same rhythm, without effort, on the same leg, Frou-Frou went on with the race.

“Bravo, Vronsky!” he heard the voices of a group of people, men of his regiment and friends, he knew, who were standing at this obstacle; he could not fail to recognize Yashvin’s voice, but he did not see him.

“Oh, my beauty!” he thought of Frou-Frou, listening to what was happening behind. “He’s over!” he thought, hearing Gladiator’s stride behind him. There remained one last little ditch with water, five feet wide; Vronsky did not even look at it, but, wanting to come in a long way first, began working the reins in a circular motion, raising and lowering the horse’s head in time with her stride. He felt that the horse was going on her last reserve; not only her neck and shoulders were wet, but on her withers, her head, her sharp ears the sweat stood out in drops, and she was breathing sharply and shortly. But he knew that this reserve was more than enough for the remaining four hundred yards. Only because he felt himself closer to the ground, and from the special smoothness of her movement, did Vronsky know how much his horse had increased her speed. She flew over the little ditch as if not noticing it. She flew over it like a bird; but at that very moment Vronsky, to his horror, felt that, failing to keep up with the horse’s movement, he had, not understanding how himself, made a bad, unforgivable movement, letting himself down into the saddle. Suddenly his position changed, and he understood that something terrible had happened. He could not yet give himself an account of what had happened, when the white legs of the chestnut stallion flashed right beside him, and Makhotin went past at a fast gallop. Vronsky was touching the ground with one foot, and his horse was falling over on that foot. He just managed to free his leg as she fell on one side, wheezing heavily, and, making vain efforts with her slender, sweating neck to get up, she fluttered on the ground at his feet like a shot bird. The clumsy movement Vronsky had made had broken her back. But that he understood much later. Now he saw only that Makhotin was quickly moving away, while he stood alone, staggering, on the muddy, motionless ground, and before him, breathing heavily, lay Frou-Frou, who, bending her head towards him, looked at him with her lovely eye. Still not understanding what had happened, Vronsky pulled at the horse’s rein. Again she struggled all over, like a fish, creaking the flaps of the saddle, freed her front legs, but, unable to lift her hindquarters, at once began to tremble and fell on her side again. His face disfigured with passion, pale, his lower jaw shaking, Vronsky struck her in the belly with his heel and again began pulling at the reins. But she did not move, and, burying her muzzle in the ground, only looked at her master with her speaking eyes.

“Aaah!” groaned Vronsky, clutching his head. “Aaah! What have I done!” he cried. “And the race lost! And my own fault, shameful, unforgivable! And this poor, dear, ruined horse! Aaah! What have I done!”

People, a doctor and his assistant, officers of his regiment, were running towards him. To his misfortune, he felt that he was whole and unhurt. The horse had broken her back, and it was decided to shoot her. Vronsky could not answer questions, could not speak to anyone. He turned and, without picking up the cap that had fallen off his head, walked away from the racecourse, not knowing himself where he was going. He felt wretched. For the first time in his life he had experienced the heaviest kind of misfortune, a misfortune that could not be put right, and one for which he himself was to blame.

Yashvin caught up with him with his cap and took him home, and in half an hour Vronsky came to himself. But the memory of this race stayed in his soul for a long time as the heaviest and most tormenting memory of his life.

## Chapter XXVI

Outwardly the relations of Alexei Alexandrovich with his wife were the same as before. The only difference was that he was even busier than before. As in former years, at the opening of spring he went abroad to a spa to restore his health, which was upset every year by his intensive winter work, and, as usual, he came back in July and at once took up his usual work with increased energy. As usual, his wife had moved to their summer villa, while he stayed in Petersburg.

Since that conversation after the evening at Princess Tverskaya’s he had never spoken to Anna of his suspicions and jealousy, and that habitual tone of his, as of someone mimicking somebody, could not have been more convenient for his present relations with his wife. He was somewhat colder to his wife. He seemed only to have a slight grudge against her for that first night-time conversation, which she had put aside. In his attitude to her there was a shade of vexation, but no more. “You did not want to have it out with me,” he seemed to say, addressing her in his mind; “so much the worse for you. Now you will have to ask me, and I shall not have it out. So much the worse for you,” he said in his mind, like a man who has vainly tried to put out a fire, become angry at his vain efforts, and said: “Well then, take that! You shall burn for this!”

He, this man so clever and subtle in the affairs of his service, did not understand the whole madness of such an attitude to his wife. He did not understand it because it was too frightening for him to understand his real position, and in his soul he had closed, locked and sealed that drawer in which he kept his feelings for his family, that is, for his wife and son. He, an attentive father, had become particularly cold to his son since the end of that winter, and had the same bantering manner with him as with his wife. “Ah! young man!” he would address him.

Alexei Alexandrovich thought and said that in no year had he had so much official work as this one; but he was not aware that he himself was inventing work for himself this year, that it was one of the means of not opening that drawer where his feelings for his wife and family and his thoughts about them lay, and which became more frightening the longer they lay there. If anyone had had the right to ask Alexei Alexandrovich what he thought of his wife’s behaviour, the mild, meek Alexei Alexandrovich would have answered nothing, but would have been very angry with the man who asked him about it. That is why there was something proud and stern in the expression of Alexei Alexandrovich’s face when he was asked about his wife’s health. Alexei Alexandrovich did not want to think anything about his wife’s behaviour and feelings, and indeed he did not think anything about them.

Alexei Alexandrovich’s regular summer villa was in Peterhof, and usually Countess Lidia Ivanovna lived there in the summer too, as Anna’s neighbour and in constant contact with her. This year Countess Lidia Ivanovna had refused to live in Peterhof, had not once been to see Anna Arkadyevna, and had hinted to Alexei Alexandrovich that Anna’s intimacy with Betsy and Vronsky was unsuitable. Alexei Alexandrovich had stopped her sternly, expressing the thought that his wife was above suspicion, and since then he had begun to avoid Countess Lidia Ivanovna. He did not want to see, and did not see, that many people in society were already looking askance at his wife; he did not want to understand, and did not understand, why his wife had particularly insisted on moving to Tsarskoe, where Betsy lived, and from which it was not far to the camp of Vronsky’s regiment. He did not allow himself to think about it, and did not think; yet at the same time, in the depths of his soul, never putting it into words to himself and having not only no proofs of it but no suspicions either, he knew beyond doubt that he was a deceived husband, and was deeply unhappy because of it.

How many times in the course of his eight years of happy life with his wife, looking at other men’s unfaithful wives and deceived husbands, had Alexei Alexandrovich said to himself: “How can anyone let it come to that? How can anyone not untie such an ugly situation?” But now, when the trouble had fallen on his own head, not only did he not think of how to untie the situation, he did not want to know of it at all, did not want to know precisely because it was too terrible, too unnatural.

Since his return from abroad Alexei Alexandrovich had been to the villa twice. Once he dined, and another time he spent the evening with guests, but not once did he stay the night, as he had been in the habit of doing in former years.

The day of the races was a very busy day for Alexei Alexandrovich; but, having drawn up a timetable for the day early in the morning, he decided that immediately after an early dinner he would drive to his wife at the villa and from there to the races, at which the whole court would be present and at which he had to be. He would call on his wife because he had decided to visit her once a week for the sake of appearances. Besides, on that day he had to hand his wife money for her expenses, as was the established custom, by the fifteenth of the month.

With his usual control over his thoughts, having thought all this out about his wife, he did not allow his thoughts to range further over what concerned her.

That morning was a very busy one for Alexei Alexandrovich. The evening before, Countess Lidia Ivanovna had sent him a pamphlet by a famous traveller in China who was in Petersburg, with a letter asking him to receive the traveller himself, a man who for various reasons was very interesting and useful. Alexei Alexandrovich had not had time to read the pamphlet in the evening and finished it in the morning. Then petitioners came, and there began reports, receptions, appointments, dismissals, distributions of awards, pensions, salaries, correspondence: that workaday business, as Alexei Alexandrovich called it, which took up so much time. Then there was private business, a visit from the doctor and from his steward. The steward did not take much time. He only handed over the money Alexei Alexandrovich needed and gave a brief report on the state of his affairs, which were not altogether good, since it so happened that this year, because of frequent outings, more had been spent and there was a deficit. But the doctor, a famous Petersburg doctor who was on friendly terms with Alexei Alexandrovich, took up a great deal of time. Alexei Alexandrovich had not been expecting him that day and was surprised by his visit, and still more by the doctor’s questioning Alexei Alexandrovich very attentively about his condition, listening to his chest, tapping and feeling his liver. Alexei Alexandrovich did not know that his friend Lidia Ivanovna, noticing that Alexei Alexandrovich’s health was not good this year, had asked the doctor to come and look at the patient. “Do it for my sake,” Countess Lidia Ivanovna had said to him.

“I shall do it for Russia’s sake, Countess,” the doctor had answered.

“A priceless man!” Countess Lidia Ivanovna had said.

The doctor was very dissatisfied with Alexei Alexandrovich. He found the liver considerably enlarged, the nourishment reduced, and no effect at all from the waters. He prescribed as much physical exercise as possible and as little mental strain as possible, and above all no distress of any kind: that is, the very thing that was as impossible for Alexei Alexandrovich as not breathing; and he left, leaving in Alexei Alexandrovich an unpleasant awareness that something was wrong with him and that it could not be put right.

Coming out from Alexei Alexandrovich, the doctor ran into Slyudin on the porch, Alexei Alexandrovich’s head of chancery, whom he knew well. They had been fellow students at the university and, though they met rarely, they respected each other and were good friends; and so there was no one to whom the doctor would have spoken his frank opinion of the patient as he would to Slyudin.

“How glad I am that you have seen him,” said Slyudin. “He is not well, it seems to me… Well, what is it?”

“This is what,” said the doctor, waving over Slyudin’s head to his coachman to bring the carriage round. “This is what,” said the doctor, taking a finger of his kid glove in his white hands and pulling it tight. “If you don’t stretch a string and try to break it, it is very hard; but stretch it to the last limit and press on the stretched string with the weight of a finger, and it snaps. And he, with his assiduity, his conscientiousness about his work, he is stretched to the utmost degree; and there is pressure from outside, and a heavy one,” the doctor concluded, raising his eyebrows significantly. “Will you be at the races?” he added, going down to the waiting carriage. “Yes, yes, of course, it takes a great deal of time,” the doctor answered something to what Slyudin had said and he had not caught.

After the doctor, who had taken up so much time, came the famous traveller, and Alexei Alexandrovich, making use of the pamphlet he had just read and of his earlier knowledge of the subject, struck the traveller with the depth of his knowledge of it and the breadth of his enlightened outlook.

Together with the traveller, the arrival of a provincial marshal of nobility was announced, who had come to Petersburg and with whom it was necessary to have a talk. After he had gone it was necessary to finish the workaday business with the head of chancery, and there was still a call to be made on a serious and important matter on a certain important person. Alexei Alexandrovich only just managed to get back by five o’clock, his dinner hour, and, having dined with the head of chancery, he invited him to drive with him to the villa and to the races.

Without admitting it to himself, Alexei Alexandrovich was now looking for opportunities to have a third person present at his meetings with his wife.

## Chapter XXVII

Anna was standing upstairs in front of the mirror, pinning the last bow on her dress with Annushka’s help, when she heard by the porch the sound of wheels crunching the gravel.

“It’s too early for Betsy,” she thought, and, looking out of the window, she saw the carriage and, leaning out of it, a black hat and the so familiar ears of Alexei Alexandrovich. “How inconvenient: can he be going to stay the night?” she thought. And everything that might come of it seemed to her so terrible and frightening that, without a moment’s thought, with a gay and radiant face, she went out to meet them, and, feeling within her the presence of the spirit of lies and deceit, already familiar to her, she at once gave herself up to this spirit and began talking, not knowing herself what she would say.

“Ah, how nice of you!” she said, giving her husband her hand and greeting Slyudin, who was like one of the household, with a smile. “You’ll stay the night, I hope?” was the first word the spirit of deceit prompted her with. “And now we’ll go together. Only it’s a pity I promised Betsy. She is calling for me.”

Alexei Alexandrovich frowned at the name of Betsy.

“Oh, I shall not separate the inseparables,” he said in his usual joking tone. “Mikhail Vasilyevich and I will go. The doctors tell me to walk, too. I shall stroll along the way and imagine that I am at the spa.”

“There is no hurry,” said Anna. “Will you have some tea?” She rang.

“Bring tea, and tell Seryozha that Alexei Alexandrovich has come. Well, and how is your health? Mikhail Vasilyevich, you have never been to see me; look how nice it is on my balcony,” she said, turning now to one, now to the other.

She spoke very simply and naturally, but too much and too fast. She felt this herself, all the more because in the curious glance Mikhail Vasilyevich gave her she noticed that he seemed to be watching her.

Mikhail Vasilyevich at once went out onto the terrace.

She sat down beside her husband.

“You don’t look quite well,” she said.

“Yes,” he said; “the doctor was with me today and took up an hour of my time. I feel that one of my friends sent him: so precious is my health…”

“No, but what did he say?”

She asked him about his health and his work, urged him to rest and to move out to her.

All this she said gaily, quickly, and with a special brightness in her eyes; but Alexei Alexandrovich now attached no significance to her tone. He heard only her words and gave them only their direct meaning. And he answered her simply, though jokingly. There was nothing special in the whole conversation, but never afterwards could Anna remember this whole short scene without a tormenting pain of shame.

Seryozha came in, preceded by his governess. If Alexei Alexandrovich had allowed himself to observe, he would have noticed the timid, bewildered look with which Seryozha glanced at his father and then at his mother. But he did not want to see anything, and he did not see.

“Ah, young man! He has grown. Really, he is becoming quite a man. Good day, young man.”

And he gave his hand to the frightened Seryozha.

Seryozha, who had been shy of his father before, now, since Alexei Alexandrovich had begun calling him young man, and since the riddle of whether Vronsky was friend or enemy had come into his head, kept away from his father. He looked round at his mother, as though asking for protection. Only with his mother was he happy. Meanwhile Alexei Alexandrovich, having started talking to the governess, was holding his son by the shoulder, and Seryozha felt so tormentingly awkward that Anna saw he was about to cry.

Anna, who had blushed the moment her son came in, noticing that Seryozha was ill at ease, jumped up quickly, lifted Alexei Alexandrovich’s hand from her son’s shoulder, and, kissing her son, led him out onto the terrace and came back at once.

“But it’s time to go,” she said, looking at her watch. “Why doesn’t Betsy come!…”

“Yes,” said Alexei Alexandrovich, and, getting up, he clasped his hands and cracked his fingers. “I came also to bring you money, since nightingales are not fed on fables,” he said. “You need it, I expect.”

“No, I don’t… yes, I do,” she said, not looking at him and blushing to the roots of her hair. “But you will call in here after the races, I expect.”

“Oh yes!” answered Alexei Alexandrovich. “And here is the beauty of Peterhof, Princess Tverskaya,” he added, looking out of the window at an English carriage with blinkered horses and a tiny body set extremely high, which was driving up. “What elegance! Charming! Well then, let us be going too.”

Princess Tverskaya did not get out of the carriage; only her footman, in gaiters, a short cape and a black hat, jumped down at the porch.

“I’m going, goodbye!” said Anna, and, kissing her son, she went up to Alexei Alexandrovich and held out her hand to him. “It was very nice of you to come.”

Alexei Alexandrovich kissed her hand.

“Well, goodbye then. You’ll come in for tea, splendid!” she said, and went out, radiant and gay. But as soon as she could no longer see him, she felt the place on her hand that his lips had touched, and shuddered with disgust.

## Chapter XXVIII

When Alexei Alexandrovich appeared at the races, Anna was already sitting in the pavilion beside Betsy, the pavilion where all high society gathered. She saw her husband from far off. Two men, her husband and her lover, were the two centres of her life, and without the help of her outward senses she felt their nearness. She felt her husband’s approach while he was still far off, and could not help following him with her eyes in the waves of the crowd through which he was moving. She saw how he came towards the pavilion, now condescendingly answering ingratiating bows, now greeting his equals in a friendly, absent-minded way, now carefully waiting for a glance from the great of this world and raising his big round hat, which pressed on the tips of his ears. She knew all these ways of his, and all of them were loathsome to her. “Nothing but ambition, nothing but the wish to succeed, that is all there is in his soul,” she thought; “and lofty considerations, love of enlightenment, religion, all that is only a tool for succeeding.”

From his glances at the ladies’ pavilion (he was looking straight at her but did not recognize his wife in the sea of muslin, tulle, ribbons, hair and parasols) she understood that he was looking for her; but she deliberately did not notice him.

“Alexei Alexandrovich!” Princess Betsy called to him. “You can’t see your wife, I expect; here she is!”

He smiled his cold smile.

“There is so much brilliance here that one’s eyes are dazzled,” he said, and went into the pavilion. He smiled at his wife as a husband ought to smile on meeting a wife he has only just seen, and greeted the princess and other acquaintances, giving each his due, that is, joking with the ladies and exchanging greetings with the men. Below, beside the pavilion, stood an adjutant-general whom Alexei Alexandrovich respected, known for his intelligence and education. Alexei Alexandrovich began talking with him.

There was an interval between races, and so nothing hindered conversation. The adjutant-general was condemning races. Alexei Alexandrovich was objecting, defending them. Anna listened to his thin, even voice, not missing a single word, and every word of his seemed false to her and cut her ear with pain.

When the steeplechase of nearly three miles began, she bent forward and, never taking her eyes away, watched Vronsky going up to his horse and mounting, and at the same time heard that loathsome, never-ceasing voice of her husband. She was tormented with fear for Vronsky, but still more tormented by what seemed to her the never-ceasing sound of her husband’s thin voice with its familiar intonations.

“I am a bad woman, I am a ruined woman,” she thought, “but I don’t like lying, I can’t bear lies, and his food is lies. He knows everything, sees everything; what can he feel, if he can talk so calmly? If he killed me, if he killed Vronsky, I would respect him. But no, all he needs is lies and propriety,” Anna said to herself, not thinking of what exactly she wanted from her husband, what she would have liked him to be. Nor did she understand that this particular talkativeness of Alexei Alexandrovich today, which so irritated her, was only the expression of his inner alarm and uneasiness. As a child who has hurt himself jumps about, setting his muscles moving to deaden the pain, so for Alexei Alexandrovich mental movement was necessary to deaden those thoughts about his wife which, in her presence and in Vronsky’s, and with the constant repetition of his name, demanded attention. And as it is natural for a child to jump, so it was natural for him to talk well and cleverly. He was saying:

“Danger in races of military men and cavalrymen is an essential condition of racing. If England can point in her military history to the most brilliant cavalry feats, it is only thanks to her having historically developed this strength in animals and men. Sport, in my opinion, has great significance, and, as always, we see only what is most superficial.”

“Not superficial,” said Princess Tverskaya. “One officer, they say, has broken two ribs.”

Alexei Alexandrovich smiled his smile, which only showed his teeth and said nothing more.

“Let us grant, Princess, that this is not superficial,” he said, “but internal. But that is not the point,” and he turned again to the general, with whom he was talking seriously. “Do not forget that it is military men who are racing, who have chosen this activity, and you will agree that every calling has its other side of the medal. This forms a direct part of a military man’s duties. The ugly sport of prize-fighting or of Spanish toreadors is a sign of barbarism. But specialized sport is a sign of development.”

“No, I shan’t come another time; it excites me too much,” said Princess Betsy. “Isn’t that so, Anna?”

“It excites one, but one can’t tear oneself away,” said another lady. “If I had been a Roman woman, I wouldn’t have missed a single circus.”

Anna said nothing and, not lowering her field-glass, looked at one spot.

At that moment a tall general was passing through the pavilion. Breaking off his speech, Alexei Alexandrovich got up hurriedly but with dignity, and bowed low to the passing officer.

“You are not racing?” the officer joked to him.

“My race is a harder one,” Alexei Alexandrovich answered respectfully.

And although the answer meant nothing, the officer looked as if he had received a witty saying from a witty man and fully understood la pointe de la sauce.

“There are two sides,” Alexei Alexandrovich went on, sitting down, “the performers and the spectators; and love of these spectacles is the surest sign of low development in the spectators, I agree, but…”

“Princess, a bet!” Stepan Arkadyich’s voice was heard from below, addressing Betsy. “Whom are you backing?”

“Anna and I are for Prince Kuzovlev,” answered Betsy.

“I’m for Vronsky. A pair of gloves.”

“Done!”

“How beautiful it is, isn’t it?”

Alexei Alexandrovich was silent while people around him talked, but at once began again.

“I agree, but manly games…” he was going on.

But at that moment the riders were being started, and all conversation stopped. Alexei Alexandrovich fell silent, and everyone got up and turned towards the river. Alexei Alexandrovich was not interested in races and so did not look at the riders, but began absent-mindedly letting his tired eyes wander over the spectators. His gaze stopped on Anna.

Her face was pale and stern. She evidently saw nothing and no one except one man. Her hand clutched her fan convulsively, and she was not breathing. He looked at her and hurriedly turned away, looking at other faces.

“Yes, and that lady and the others too are very excited; it is very natural,” Alexei Alexandrovich said to himself. He wanted not to look at her, but his gaze was drawn to her in spite of himself.
