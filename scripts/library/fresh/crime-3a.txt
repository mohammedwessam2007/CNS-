## CHAPTER V

“It is true, I was meaning not long ago to ask Razumikhin for work, to get me lessons or something...” Raskolnikov thought it through, “but how can he help me now? Suppose he gets me lessons, suppose he even shares his last kopeck with me, if he has a kopeck, so that I could even buy boots and mend my clothes to go to lessons... hm... Well, and then? What shall I do with five-kopeck pieces? Is that what I need now? Really, it is ridiculous that I set out for Razumikhin’s...”

The question of why he had set out for Razumikhin’s now troubled him more than even he himself realised; uneasily he kept searching for some sinister meaning for himself in what seemed the most ordinary action.

“What, did I really want to put the whole thing right with Razumikhin alone, and find the way out of everything in Razumikhin?” he asked himself in surprise.

He thought and rubbed his forehead, and, strange to say, somehow by chance, suddenly and almost of itself, after very long reflection, a very strange thought came into his head.

“Hm... to Razumikhin,” he said suddenly, quite calmly, as if it were a final decision, “to Razumikhin I shall go, of course... but not now... I shall go to him... the day after, after that, when that is already over and everything begins anew...”

And suddenly he came to himself.

“After that,” he cried, jumping up from the bench, “but is that going to happen? Is it really going to happen?”

He left the bench and walked off, almost ran; he was about to turn back towards home, but suddenly he felt a terrible loathing for going home: there, in that corner, in that terrible cupboard, all this had been ripening for more than a month now; and he walked on wherever his feet took him.

His nervous trembling had turned into something like a fever; he even felt a chill; in such heat he was growing cold. As if with an effort, almost unconsciously, from some inner necessity, he began to stare at every object he came across, as if searching hard for distraction; but he did not succeed very well, and kept falling into thought. And when, with a start, he raised his head again and looked around, he immediately forgot what he had just been thinking about and even where he was going. In this way he walked the whole length of Vasilyevsky Island, came out at the Little Neva, crossed the bridge and turned towards the Islands. The greenery and freshness pleased his tired eyes at first, used as they were to the dust of the city, to lime and to huge houses that crowded and oppressed him. Here there was no stuffiness, no stench, no taverns. But soon these new, pleasant sensations too turned sick and irritating. Sometimes he stopped before some summer villa decked in greenery, looked through the fence, and saw in the distance, on balconies and terraces, women in fine dresses and children running in the garden. The flowers especially caught his attention; he looked at them longest of all. He also met splendid carriages, men and women on horseback; he followed them with his eyes curiously and forgot them before they had disappeared from sight. Once he stopped and counted his money: it turned out to be about thirty kopecks. “Twenty to the policeman, three to Nastasya for the letter, so I gave the Marmeladovs forty-seven or fifty kopecks yesterday,” he thought, reckoning for some reason; but soon he even forgot why he had taken the money out of his pocket. He remembered it as he passed an eating house, a sort of cheap tavern, and felt that he was hungry. Going into the eating house, he drank a glass of vodka and ate a pie with some sort of filling. He finished it on the road again. He had not drunk vodka for a very long time, and it took effect at once, though he had drunk only one glass. His legs suddenly grew heavy, and he began to feel a strong urge to sleep. He set off home; but when he had got as far as Petrovsky Island, he stopped in complete exhaustion, left the road, went into the bushes, fell on the grass and at that very moment fell asleep.

In a sick state, dreams are often distinguished by an extraordinary vividness and brightness and an extreme likeness to reality. Sometimes a monstrous picture takes shape, but the setting and the whole course of the scene are at the same time so probable, and with such fine, unexpected details, which yet fit artistically into the fullness of the picture, that the dreamer himself could not invent them awake, even if he were as great an artist as Pushkin or Turgenev. Such dreams, sick dreams, are always remembered for a long time and make a strong impression on a man’s disordered and already overexcited organism.

Raskolnikov had a terrible dream. He dreamed of his childhood, back in their little town. He is about seven and is walking with his father outside the town on a holiday, towards evening. The day is grey and stifling, the place is exactly as it had survived in his memory; indeed in his memory it had faded much more than it appeared now in the dream. The little town lies open as if on the palm of a hand, not a willow anywhere around; somewhere very far away, at the very edge of the sky, a little wood shows black. A few paces from the last market garden of the town stands a tavern, a big tavern, which always made a most unpleasant impression on him, and even frightened him, when he walked past it with his father. There was always such a crowd there, they shouted, guffawed, swore, sang so hideously and hoarsely, and fought so often; such drunken and fearful faces always hung about the tavern... When he met them, he pressed close to his father and trembled all over. By the tavern runs the road, a country track, always dusty, and the dust on it is always so black. It winds on further, and about three hundred paces on it curves to the right round the town cemetery. In the middle of the cemetery is a stone church with a green dome, to which he went with his father and mother two or three times a year to Mass, when requiems were sung for his grandmother, who had died long before and whom he had never seen. They always took with them on these occasions funeral rice on a white dish, wrapped in a napkin, and the rice was sweet, made of rice and raisins pressed into the rice in the shape of a cross. He loved this church and its old icons, mostly without frames, and the old priest with his trembling head. Beside his grandmother’s grave, which had a stone slab on it, was the little grave of his younger brother, who had died at six months old and whom he had not known at all either and could not remember; but he had been told that he had a little brother, and every time he visited the cemetery he crossed himself religiously and reverently over the little grave, bowed to it and kissed it. And now he dreams: he and his father are walking along the road to the cemetery and passing the tavern; he is holding his father’s hand and looking round fearfully at the tavern. A particular circumstance catches his attention: this time there seems to be some festivity here, a crowd of townswomen in their best clothes, peasant women, their husbands and all sorts of riffraff. They are all drunk, they are all singing songs, and by the tavern steps stands a cart, but a strange cart. It is one of those big carts to which big dray-horses are harnessed and in which goods and barrels of wine are carried. He always loved to watch those huge dray-horses, with their long manes and thick legs, walking calmly with measured steps and pulling a whole mountain behind them without the least strain, as if it were even easier for them with loads than without. But now, strange to say, a small, thin, sorrel peasant nag was harnessed to such a big cart, one of those which, as he had often seen, strain themselves sometimes with some high load of wood or hay, especially when the load sticks in the mud or in a rut, and the peasants always beat them so painfully, so painfully with their whips, sometimes even right on the muzzle and on the eyes, and he is so sorry, so sorry to see it that he almost cries, and Mama always used to lead him away from the window. But now suddenly it grows very noisy: out of the tavern, with shouts and songs and balalaikas, come some huge peasants, dead drunk, in red and blue shirts, with their coats thrown over their shoulders. “Get in, everybody get in!” shouts one of them, still young, with a thick neck and a fleshy face as red as a carrot. “I’ll drive you all, get in!” But at once there is laughter and exclamations:

“A nag like that pull it!”

“Have you gone out of your mind, Mikolka? Harnessing a little mare like that to a cart like that!”

“Why, the sorrel must be twenty years old, lads!”

“Get in, I’ll drive you all!” Mikolka shouts again, jumping first into the cart; he takes the reins and stands up at full height in the front. “The bay went off with Matvey just now,” he shouts from the cart, “and this little mare, lads, only breaks my heart: I could kill her, it seems, she eats her bread for nothing. I say get in! I’ll make her gallop! She’ll gallop!” And he takes the whip in his hands, getting ready with relish to flog the sorrel.

“Get in, then, why not!” they guffaw in the crowd. “Do you hear, she’ll gallop!”

“She hasn’t galloped for ten years, I bet.”

“She’ll gallop!”

“Don’t spare her, lads, everybody take a whip, get ready!”

“That’s it! Flog her!”

They all climb into Mikolka’s cart with guffaws and jokes. Six or so have climbed in, and there is room for more. They take one woman with them, fat and rosy. She is in red calico, with a beaded headdress, with felt boots on her feet; she cracks nuts and laughs. Around them in the crowd they are laughing too, and indeed, how could they not laugh: a wretched little mare like that is going to pull such a load at a gallop! Two lads in the cart take a whip each at once, to help Mikolka. There is a cry of “Gee up!”, the little nag pulls with all her strength, but far from galloping, she can hardly even manage a walk; she only shuffles her feet, grunts, and cringes under the blows of the three whips, which rain down on her like peas. The laughter in the cart and in the crowd doubles, but Mikolka is angry and in a rage flogs the little mare with quicker and quicker blows, as if he really thinks she will gallop.

“Let me in too, lads!” shouts one lad in the crowd who has got a taste for it.

“Get in! Everybody get in!” shouts Mikolka. “She’ll pull you all. I’ll flog her to death!” And he lashes and lashes, and in his frenzy no longer knows what to beat her with.

“Papa, Papa,” he cries to his father, “Papa, what are they doing? Papa, they are beating the poor horse!”

“Come along, come along!” says his father. “They are drunk, they are fooling about, the fools: come along, don’t look!” And he tries to lead him away, but he tears himself out of his hands and, beside himself, runs to the horse. But the poor horse is in a bad way already. She is gasping, she stops, pulls again, almost falls.

“Flog her to death!” shouts Mikolka. “That’s what it’s come to. I’ll flog her to death!”

“Have you no cross on you, you devil?” shouts an old man from the crowd.

“Whoever saw a little horse like that pull a load like that?” adds another.

“You’ll kill her!” shouts a third.

“Don’t touch! She’s my property! I do what I like. Get in, more of you! Everybody get in! I want her to gallop, no matter what!..”

Suddenly a volley of laughter breaks out and drowns everything: the little mare could not bear the quickening blows and in her helplessness began to kick. Even the old man could not help grinning. And indeed: such a wretched little mare, and she kicks too!

Two lads from the crowd get hold of a whip each as well and run to the horse to flog her from the sides. Each runs to his own side.

“On the muzzle, lash her on the eyes, on the eyes!” shouts Mikolka.

“A song, lads!” shouts someone from the cart, and everyone in the cart joins in. A rollicking song rings out, a tambourine jingles, there is whistling in the refrains. The woman cracks nuts and laughs.

...He runs beside the horse, he runs in front, he sees them lashing her on the eyes, right on the eyes! He is crying. His heart rises in him, tears flow. One of those flogging catches him across the face; he does not feel it, he wrings his hands, he cries out, he rushes to a grey-haired old man with a grey beard, who is shaking his head and condemning it all. A woman takes him by the hand and tries to lead him away; but he tears himself free and runs to the horse again. She is at her last efforts, but she begins to kick once more.

“The devil take you!” Mikolka cries in a fury. He throws down the whip, bends down and pulls out from the bottom of the cart a long, thick shaft, takes it by the end in both hands and swings it with an effort over the sorrel.

“He’ll smash her!” they shout all around.

“He’ll kill her!”

“She’s my property!” shouts Mikolka, and brings the shaft down with all his might. There is a heavy thud.

“Flog her, flog her! Why have you stopped?” shout voices from the crowd.

And Mikolka swings a second time, and a second blow falls with all his might on the back of the unhappy nag. She sinks down on her haunches, but leaps up and pulls, pulls with all her last strength in different directions, trying to move the cart; but on all sides they meet her with six whips, and the shaft rises again and falls a third time, then a fourth, steadily, with a full swing. Mikolka is furious that he cannot kill her with one blow.

“She’s tough!” they shout all around.

“She’ll fall in a minute, lads, sure enough, and that’ll be the end of her!” shouts an enthusiast from the crowd.

“Take an axe to her, why not! Finish her off at once,” shouts a third.

“Eh, may the mosquitoes eat you! Make way!” Mikolka screams in a frenzy; he throws down the shaft, bends into the cart again and pulls out an iron crowbar. “Look out!” he shouts, and with all his might he deals his poor little horse a stunning blow with a full swing. The blow crashes down; the little mare staggers, sinks down, tries to pull, but the crowbar comes down on her back again with a full swing, and she falls to the ground as if all four legs had been cut from under her at once.

“Finish her off!” shouts Mikolka, and leaps down from the cart as if beside himself. Several lads, also red and drunk, seize whatever comes to hand, whips, sticks, the shaft, and run to the dying mare. Mikolka stands at her side and begins to beat her at random on the back with the crowbar. The nag stretches out her muzzle, sighs heavily, and dies.

“He’s done for her!” they shout in the crowd.

“Why wouldn’t she gallop!”

“She’s my property!” shouts Mikolka, with the crowbar in his hands and his eyes bloodshot. He stands there as if sorry that there is no one left to beat.

“Well, there really is no cross on you, it seems!” many voices now shout from the crowd.

But the poor boy is beside himself. With a cry he forces his way through the crowd to the sorrel, puts his arms around her dead, bleeding muzzle and kisses it, kisses her on the eyes, on the lips... Then suddenly he jumps up and in a frenzy throws himself with his little fists on Mikolka. At that moment his father, who has long been chasing after him, at last seizes him and carries him out of the crowd.

“Come along! Come along!” he says to him. “Let us go home!”

“Papa! Why did they... kill... the poor horse!” he sobs, but his breath catches, and the words burst out in cries from his tight chest.

“They are drunk, they are fooling about, it’s not our business, come along!” says his father. He puts his arms around his father, but his chest is tight, tight. He tries to get his breath, to cry out, and wakes up.

He woke up all in a sweat, his hair wet with sweat, gasping, and raised himself in horror.

“Thank God, it was only a dream!” he said, sitting up under a tree and drawing deep breaths. “But what is this? Is a fever beginning in me: such a hideous dream!”

His whole body felt as if it had been beaten; his soul was troubled and dark. He put his elbows on his knees and propped his head in both hands.

“God!” he exclaimed. “Can it be, can it be that I shall really take an axe, that I shall strike her on the head, smash her skull... that I shall slip in the sticky, warm blood, break open the lock, steal and tremble; hide, all covered in blood... with the axe... Lord, can it be?”

He was trembling like a leaf as he said this.

“But what am I doing!” he went on, raising himself again, as if in deep amazement. “I knew, after all, that I could not bear it, so why have I been tormenting myself until now? Why, only yesterday, yesterday, when I went to make that... rehearsal, I understood perfectly yesterday that I could not stand it... What am I doing now, then? Why have I still been doubting until now? Why, yesterday, coming down the stairs, I said myself that it was base, loathsome, low, low... why, the mere thought of it made me sick in waking life and threw me into horror...

“No, I cannot stand it, I cannot stand it! Let it be, let there even be no doubt at all about all those calculations, let everything that has been decided this month be as clear as day, as just as arithmetic. Lord! I still shall not dare to do it! I cannot stand it, I cannot stand it!.. Why, then, why until now...”

He got to his feet, looked round in surprise, as if wondering even that he had come here, and went towards T. Bridge. He was pale, his eyes were burning, there was exhaustion in all his limbs, but suddenly he seemed to breathe more easily. He felt that he had already thrown off that terrible burden that had weighed on him so long, and suddenly his soul felt light and at peace. “Lord!” he prayed, “show me my way, and I renounce this accursed... dream of mine!”

As he crossed the bridge, he looked quietly and calmly at the Neva, at the bright sunset of the bright red sun. In spite of his weakness he did not even feel tired. It was as if an abscess in his heart, which had been festering all month, had suddenly burst. Freedom, freedom! He was free now from those spells, from sorcery, from enchantment, from obsession!

Later, when he remembered this time and everything that happened to him in those days, minute by minute, point by point, feature by feature, he was always struck almost superstitiously by one circumstance, which in essence was not very extraordinary, but which always seemed to him afterwards to be a kind of predetermination of his fate.

Namely: he could never understand or explain to himself why, tired and worn out as he was, when it would have been best of all for him to go home by the shortest and most direct way, he went home by way of the Haymarket, where he had no need at all to go. The detour was not great, but it was obvious and completely unnecessary. Of course, dozens of times it had happened to him to come home without remembering the streets he had walked through. But why, he always asked, why did such an important meeting, so decisive for him, and at the same time so utterly accidental, in the Haymarket (where he had no reason even to go), come just now, at such an hour, at such a minute in his life, precisely when his mind was in such a mood and in precisely such circumstances, the only ones in which this meeting could have the most decisive and most final effect on his whole fate? As if it had been lying in wait for him there on purpose!

It was about nine o’clock when he crossed the Haymarket. All the traders at their tables, on their trays, in their shops and stalls, were shutting up their establishments, or taking down and putting away their wares, and going home, as were their customers. Round the cook-shops on the ground floors, in the dirty, stinking courtyards of the Haymarket houses, and most of all by the taverns, crowds of all sorts of tradesmen and ragged people were gathered. Raskolnikov liked these places especially, as well as all the alleys nearby, when he went out into the street with no aim. Here his rags attracted no one’s haughty attention, and one could walk about looking however one liked without scandalising anyone. Right by K. Lane, on the corner, a tradesman and a woman, his wife, were selling goods from two tables: thread, tape, cotton kerchiefs and so on. They too were getting up to go home, but they had lingered, talking to an acquaintance who had come up. This acquaintance was Lizaveta Ivanovna, or simply Lizaveta, as everyone called her, the younger sister of that same old woman, Alyona Ivanovna, a collegiate registrar’s widow and moneylender, whom Raskolnikov had visited the day before when he came to pawn his watch and make his rehearsal... He had long known all about this Lizaveta, and she even knew him a little. She was a tall, clumsy, timid and humble woman, almost simple-minded, thirty-five years old, who was in complete slavery to her sister, worked for her day and night, trembled before her and even suffered beatings from her. She stood there thinking, with a bundle, in front of the tradesman and his wife, and listened to them attentively. They were explaining something to her with special warmth. When Raskolnikov suddenly saw her, a strange sensation, like the deepest astonishment, seized him, although there was nothing astonishing in this meeting.

“You should settle it yourself, Lizaveta Ivanovna,” the tradesman was saying loudly. “Come round tomorrow, about seven o’clock. They’ll be there too.”

“Tomorrow?” Lizaveta said slowly and thoughtfully, as if unable to decide.

“My, how Alyona Ivanovna has frightened you!” the trader’s wife rattled on; she was a lively little woman. “I look at you, and you’re just like a little child. And she isn’t even your own sister, only a half-sister, and see what a hold she has taken over you.”

“And don’t say anything to Alyona Ivanovna this time,” the husband interrupted. “That’s my advice. Just come round to us without asking. It’s a profitable business. Your sister can think it over herself afterwards.”

“Shall I come, then?”

“About seven, tomorrow; and someone from them will be there; you can settle it yourself.”

“And we’ll put the samovar on,” added the wife.

“All right, I’ll come,” said Lizaveta, still thinking it over, and slowly began to move off.

Raskolnikov had already passed by and heard no more. He passed quietly, unnoticed, trying not to miss a single word. His first astonishment gave way little by little to horror, as if a frost had passed down his spine. He had learned, he had suddenly, abruptly and quite unexpectedly learned, that tomorrow, at exactly seven o’clock in the evening, Lizaveta, the old woman’s sister and the only person who lived with her, would not be at home, and that, therefore, the old woman, at exactly seven o’clock in the evening, would be at home alone.

There were only a few steps left to his lodging. He went in like a man condemned to death. He did not reason about anything and was completely unable to reason; but with his whole being he suddenly felt that he no longer had any freedom of reason or will, and that everything had suddenly been decided finally.

Of course, even if he had had to wait whole years for a convenient chance, even then, having the plan, he could not have counted for certain on a more obvious step towards the success of that plan than the one that had suddenly presented itself just now. In any case, it would have been hard to find out the day before, and for certain, with greater precision and with the least risk, without any dangerous questions and inquiries, that tomorrow, at such an hour, such and such an old woman, on whom an attempt was being prepared, would be at home quite alone.
