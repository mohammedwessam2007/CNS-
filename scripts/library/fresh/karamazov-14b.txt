## Chapter II · From the Life of the Hieroschemamonk Elder Zosima, Departed in God, Compiled from His Own Words by Alexei Fyodorovich Karamazov · Biographical Notes · (a) Of Elder Zosima’s Young Brother

Beloved fathers and teachers, I was born in a distant northern province, in the town of V., to a father of the gentry, but not of note and not of high rank. He died when I was only two years old, and I do not remember him at all. He left my mother a small wooden house and some capital, not large, but enough to live on with her children without want. There were only two of us children: I, Zinovy, and my elder brother, Markel. He was about eight years older than I, of a hot and irritable temper, but kind, not given to mockery, and strangely silent, especially at home, with me, with our mother and with the servants. He did well at the high school but did not get on with his schoolfellows, though he did not quarrel with them either; so at least our mother remembered him. Half a year before his death, when he had turned seventeen, he took to visiting a solitary man in our town, a political exile of sorts, banished from Moscow to our town for freethinking. This exile was no small scholar and a distinguished philosopher at the university. For some reason he took a liking to Markel and began to receive him. The youth spent whole evenings with him, all through the winter, until the exile was called back to government service in Petersburg at his own request, for he had patrons. Great Lent began, and Markel would not fast; he swore and laughed at it: “It’s all nonsense,” he said, “and there is no God at all,” so that he horrified our mother and the servants, and me too, little as I was; for though I was only nine, hearing those words I was very frightened too. Our servants were all serfs, four of them, all bought in the name of a landowner we knew. I remember too how of these four my mother sold one, the cook Afimya, lame and elderly, for sixty paper roubles, and hired a free woman in her place. And then in the sixth week of Lent my brother suddenly grew worse. He had always been unwell, weak in the chest, of a frail constitution and inclined to consumption; he was tall, but thin and sickly, and very handsome in face. He had caught cold, perhaps; but the doctor came and soon whispered to our mother that it was galloping consumption and that he would not live through the spring. Mother began to weep and to beg my brother, cautiously (more so as not to frighten him), to fast and take the holy sacrament, for he was still on his feet then. Hearing this, he grew angry and abused the church of God; but he began to think: he guessed at once that he was dangerously ill, and that this was why his mother was sending him to fast and take communion while he still had strength. Besides, he knew himself that he had long been unwell, and a year before had said once at table to my mother and me, coolly: “I am not long for this world among you; perhaps I shan’t live a year”; and it was as if he had prophesied it. Three days passed, and Holy Week came. And on the Tuesday morning my brother began to prepare for communion. “I am doing this really for you, mother, to please you and set your mind at rest,” he told her. Mother wept for joy, and for grief too: “His end must be near, if there is such a change in him all of a sudden.” But he did not go to church for long; he took to his bed, so that he made his confession and received communion at home. The days became bright, clear and fragrant; Easter was late that year. All night long, I remember, he would cough and sleep badly, and in the morning he would always dress and try to sit in the soft armchair. That is how I shall remember him: sitting quiet and meek, smiling, ill himself, but his face cheerful and joyful. He was changed altogether in his soul, such a wonderful change had suddenly begun in him! The old nurse would come into his room: “Let me light the lamp before the icon in your room too, my dear.” Before, he would not allow it; he even blew it out. “Light it, dear, light it; I was a monster to forbid you before. You pray to God lighting the lamp, and I pray rejoicing at you. So we pray to the one God.” These words seemed strange to us, and our mother would go to her room and weep and weep, only wiping her eyes and putting on a cheerful look when she went in to him. “Mother, don’t cry, my dear,” he would say, “I have a long time yet to live, a long time to make merry with you, and life, life is merry and joyful!” “Ah, my dear, what merriment is there for you, when you burn in fever all night and cough so that your chest is nearly torn apart?” “Mother,” he answered her, “don’t cry; life is paradise, and we are all in paradise, but we don’t want to know it, and if we wanted to know it, tomorrow there would be paradise in the whole world.” And everyone wondered at his words, he said it so strangely and so decidedly; they were moved and wept. Acquaintances would come to see us: “My dear ones,” he would say, “dear ones, what have I done to deserve your love, why do you love such a one as me, and how is it I didn’t know it before, didn’t value it?” To the servants coming in he would say every minute: “My dear ones, my precious ones, why do you wait on me, and am I worth being waited on? If God had mercy and let me live, I would wait on you myself, for everyone should serve one another.” Our mother, listening, shook her head: “My dear, it is your illness that makes you talk like that.” “Mother, my joy,” he said, “there must be masters and servants, but let me too be the servant of my servants, just as they are to me. And I will tell you something else, mother: every one of us is guilty before everyone in everything, and I more than all.” Our mother even smiled at that; she wept and smiled: “Well, how are you guilty before everyone more than all? There are murderers and robbers, and what sins have you had time to commit, that you accuse yourself more than all?” “Mother, my own heart’s blood,” he said (he had begun to use such tender, unexpected words then), “my dear heart’s blood, my joy, know that truly each of us is guilty before everyone, for everyone and for everything. I don’t know how to explain it to you, but I feel it is so, so that it torments me. And how did we live, and get angry, and know nothing then?” So he would rise from sleep, every day more and more moved and joyful and all trembling with love. The doctor would come (an old German, Eisenschmidt, used to come): “Well, doctor, shall I live another day in the world?” he would joke with him. “Not one day, many days you will live,” the doctor would answer, “and months and years too.” “What years, what months!” he would exclaim. “Why count the days? One day is enough for a man to know all happiness. My dear ones, why do we quarrel, boast to each other, remember each other’s wrongs? Let us go straight into the garden and walk and play, love and praise and kiss each other, and bless our life.” “Your son is not long for this world,” the doctor said to our mother as she saw him off to the porch; “his illness is making him lose his mind.” The windows of his room looked out on the garden, and our garden was shady, with old trees; the spring buds were swelling on the trees, the early birds had come; they chattered and sang at his windows. And suddenly, looking at them and admiring them, he began to beg their forgiveness too: “Birds of God, joyful birds, forgive me too, for I have sinned against you as well.” None of us could understand that then, but he wept for joy: “Yes,” he said, “there was such glory of God all round me: birds, trees, meadows, the heavens; I alone lived in shame, I alone dishonoured everything and never noticed the beauty and the glory at all.” “You take too many sins on yourself,” our mother would weep. “Mother, my joy, I am weeping from gladness, not from grief; I want to be guilty before them myself; only I can’t explain it to you, for I don’t even know how to love them. Let me be guilty before everyone; then everyone will forgive me too, and that is paradise. Am I not in paradise now?”

And there was much more that I cannot remember or write down. I remember that once I went in to him alone, when no one else was with him. It was evening, clear; the sun was setting and lit up the whole room with a slanting ray. He beckoned to me when he saw me; I went up to him; he took me by the shoulders with both hands and looked into my face tenderly, lovingly; he said nothing, only looked at me so for a minute. “Well,” he said, “go now, play, live for me!” I went out then and went to play. And many times afterwards in my life I remembered with tears how he told me to live for him. He said many more such wonderful and beautiful words, though we did not understand them then. He died in the third week after Easter, conscious, and though he had stopped speaking, he did not change until his very last hour: he looked joyfully, there was gladness in his eyes; he looked for us, smiled at us, called us. Even in the town there was much talk of his death. All this shook me then, but not too much, though I cried a great deal when they buried him. I was young, a child; but everything stayed indelibly in my heart; the feeling lay hidden. In its time it was all to rise and answer. And so it did.

## (b) Of Holy Scripture in the Life of Father Zosima

We were left alone then, my mother and I. Good acquaintances soon advised her: you have only one son left, and you are not poor, you have capital; so why not, like others, send your son to Petersburg? By keeping him here you may be depriving him of a distinguished future. And they persuaded my mother to take me to Petersburg, to the Cadet Corps, so that afterwards I could enter the Imperial Guard. My mother hesitated a long time: how could she part with her last son? But she made up her mind, though not without many tears, thinking to further my happiness. She took me to Petersburg and placed me there, and after that I never saw her again; for three years later she died herself, having grieved and trembled for us both all those three years. From my parents’ house I brought away only precious memories, for there are no memories more precious to a man than those of his earliest childhood in his parents’ house, and that is almost always so, if there is even a little love and harmony in the family. Indeed, precious memories may be kept even from the worst family, if only your own soul is able to seek what is precious. Among my memories of home I count also my memories of sacred history, which, child as I was, I was very eager to know in my parents’ house. I had a book then, a sacred history with beautiful pictures, called One Hundred and Four Sacred Stories from the Old and New Testaments, and I learned to read from it. I have it here on my shelf now; I keep it as a precious remembrance. But even before I learned to read, I remember how a certain spiritual insight visited me for the first time, when I was eight years old. My mother took me alone (I don’t remember where my brother was then) to the Lord’s temple, to the liturgy on the Monday of Holy Week. It was a clear day, and, remembering it now, I seem to see again how the incense rose from the censer and slowly went up, and from above, through a narrow little window in the dome, God’s rays poured down on us in the church, and, rising to them in waves, the incense seemed to melt into them. I looked with tender feeling, and for the first time in my life I consciously received into my soul the first seed of the word of God. A youth came out into the middle of the church with a great book, so big that it seemed to me then he could hardly carry it, and laid it on the lectern, opened it and began to read; and suddenly then, for the first time, I understood something; for the first time in my life I understood what is read in God’s church. There was a man in the land of Uz, upright and God-fearing, and he had so much wealth, so many camels, so many sheep and asses, and his children made merry, and he loved them very much and prayed to God for them: perhaps they had sinned in their merrymaking. And now the devil goes up to God together with the sons of God and says to the Lord that he has gone to and fro in all the earth and under the earth. “And hast thou seen my servant Job?” God asks him. And God boasted to the devil, pointing to his great and holy servant. And the devil smiled at God’s words: “Give him over to me, and thou wilt see that thy servant will murmur and curse thy name.” And God gave over his righteous one, whom he loved so, to the devil, and the devil struck down his children and his cattle and scattered his wealth, all at once, as with the thunder of God; and Job rent his garments and threw himself on the ground and cried out: “Naked came I out of my mother’s womb, and naked shall I return to the earth; the Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord from this time forth and for evermore!” Fathers and teachers, forgive my tears now, for all my childhood seems to rise before me again, and I breathe now as I breathed then with my little eight-year-old breast, and I feel, as then, wonder and confusion and joy. And the camels took hold of my imagination so then, and Satan, who talks so with God, and God, who gave his servant over to destruction, and his servant crying out: “Blessed be thy name, though thou punishest me”; and then the quiet, sweet singing in the church: “Let my prayer be set forth”, and again the incense from the priest’s censer and the kneeling prayer! Since then (I took it up only yesterday) I cannot read that most holy story without tears. And how much there is in it that is great, secret, unimaginable! Afterwards I heard the words of the mockers and blasphemers, proud words: how could the Lord give up the most beloved of his saints to be the devil’s sport, take his children from him, strike him himself with sickness and sores so that he scraped the pus from his wounds with a potsherd, and what for? Only to boast before Satan: “See what my saint can endure for my sake!” But the greatness of it lies just in this, that it is a mystery: that the passing face of the earth and eternal truth touched each other here. Before earthly justice the act of eternal justice is accomplished. Here the Creator, as in the first days of creation, ending each day with praise, “That which I have made is good,” looks on Job and again boasts of his creation. And Job, praising the Lord, serves not only him but will serve his whole creation from generation to generation and for ever and ever, for that was what he was destined for. Lord, what a book this is, and what lessons! What a book this holy scripture is, what a miracle and what a power are given with it to man! It is like a sculpture of the world and of man and of human characters, and everything is named and shown for ever and ever. And how many mysteries resolved and revealed: God restores Job again, gives him wealth again; many years pass again, and now he has new children, different ones, and he loves them. Lord, “but how could he love these new ones, it would seem, when those first ones are no more, when he has lost them? Remembering them, how could he be fully happy, as before, with the new ones, however dear the new ones might be?” But he can, he can: old grief, by the great mystery of human life, passes little by little into quiet, tender joy; instead of the young, seething blood comes a meek, serene old age. I bless the rising of the sun each day, and my heart sings to it as before, but now I love its setting more, its long slanting rays, and with them quiet, meek, tender memories, dear images from all my long and blessed life; and over all, God’s truth, moving, reconciling, all-forgiving! My life is ending; I know it and hear it; but I feel, in each day left to me, how my earthly life is already touching a new, infinite, unknown life that is coming close, at the foretaste of which my soul trembles with rapture, my mind shines and my heart weeps with joy… Friends and teachers, I have heard more than once, and in these last days it has become still more audible, how our priests of God, and above all the village priests, complain tearfully and everywhere of their small stipend and of their humiliation, and declare outright, even in print (I have read it myself), that they can no longer, it seems, expound the scriptures to the people because their stipend is so small, and if Lutherans and heretics come and begin to lure the flock away, then let them, for our stipend is small. Lord! I think: God grant them more of this stipend so precious to them (for their complaint is just too); but truly I say: if anyone is to blame for this, half of it is we ourselves! For suppose he has no time, suppose he says rightly that he is weighed down all the time with work and services; still, it is not all the time; surely he has even one hour in the whole week to remember God. And the work does not go on all the year round. Let him gather at his house once a week, in an evening hour, at first only the children; the fathers will hear of it, and the fathers will begin to come too. And there is no need to build mansions for this work; simply take them into your cottage; don’t be afraid, they won’t foul your cottage; you are gathering them for one hour only. Let him open this book for them and begin to read, without wise words and without conceit, without setting himself above them, but tenderly and meekly, himself rejoicing that he is reading to them and that they are listening and understand him, himself loving these words; let him only stop now and then to explain some word a simple man does not understand. Do not worry, they will understand everything; the Orthodox heart will understand everything! Read to them of Abraham and Sarah, of Isaac and Rebecca, of how Jacob went to Laban and wrestled in his dream with the Lord and said, “How dreadful is this place,” and you will strike the pious mind of the simple man. Read to them, and to the children especially, of how the brothers sold into slavery their own brother, the dear boy Joseph, the dreamer and great prophet, and told their father that a wild beast had torn his son, showing him his bloodstained coat. Read how afterwards the brothers came to Egypt for corn, and Joseph, now a great courtier, unrecognised by them, tormented them, accused them, kept back their brother Benjamin, and all out of love: “I love you, and loving you I torment you.” For all his life he had remembered unceasingly how they sold him somewhere out there in the hot steppe, by a well, to merchants, and how he, wringing his hands, had wept and begged his brothers not to sell him as a slave into a strange land; and now, seeing them after so many years, he loved them again beyond measure, but he tormented and tortured them, loving them all the while. At last he goes away from them, unable himself to bear the torment of his heart, throws himself on his bed and weeps; then he wipes his face and comes out radiant and bright and declares to them: “Brothers, I am Joseph your brother!” Let him read further of how old Jacob rejoiced on learning that his dear boy was still alive, and set out for Egypt, leaving even his homeland, and died in a strange land, uttering for ever and ever in his testament the greatest word, which had lain mysteriously in his meek and timid heart all his life: that from his line, from Judah, would come the great hope of the world, its reconciler and saviour! Fathers and teachers, forgive me and do not be angry that like a little child I talk of what you have long known and could teach me a hundred times more skilfully and beautifully. I say it only from rapture; and forgive my tears, for I love this book! Let him weep too, the priest of God, and he will see that the hearts of those who listen to him are shaken in answer. Only a little seed is needed, a tiny one: let him cast it into the soul of a simple man, and it will not die; it will live in his soul all his life, hidden in him amid the darkness, amid the stench of his sins, like a point of light, like a great reminder. And there is no need, no need to explain much and teach much; he will understand everything simply. Do you think the simple man will not understand? Try reading him next the touching and tender story of the beautiful Esther and the haughty Vashti; or the wonderful tale of the prophet Jonah in the belly of the whale. Do not forget the parables of the Lord either, chiefly from the Gospel of Luke (that is what I did), and then from the Acts of the Apostles the conversion of Saul (that without fail, without fail!), and finally from the Lives of the Saints, if only the life of Alexis, the man of God, and of the greatest of the great, the joyful sufferer, the beholder of God and bearer of Christ, our mother Mary of Egypt; and you will pierce his heart with these simple tales, and all in only one hour a week, in spite of your small stipend, just one little hour. And he will see for himself that our people are merciful and grateful; they will repay him a hundredfold; remembering the priest’s zeal and his tender words, they will help him in his fields of their own accord, help him in his house too, and repay him with more respect than before; and there his stipend will have grown already. The thing is so simple-hearted that sometimes we are even afraid to say it, for people will laugh at you; and yet how true it is! He who does not believe in God will not believe in God’s people either. But he who has come to believe in God’s people will see his holiness too, even if he did not believe in it at all before. Only the people and their coming spiritual power will convert our atheists, who have torn themselves away from their native soil. And what is the word of Christ without an example? The people perish without the word of God, for their soul thirsts for the word and for every beautiful impression. In my youth, long ago, nearly forty years since, Father Anfim and I went all over Russia collecting alms for the monastery, and once we spent the night on the bank of a great navigable river with some fishermen, and with us sat a comely youth, a peasant, eighteen years old to look at; he was hurrying to his place, to haul a merchant’s barge by towrope the next day. And I saw him looking before him tenderly and serenely. The night was bright, still, warm, a July night; the river was wide, mist rose from it and refreshed us; now and then a fish splashed; the birds had fallen silent; everything was still and beautiful; everything was praying to God. And only we two were not asleep, I and this youth, and we got to talking of the beauty of God’s world and its great mystery. Every blade of grass, every little beetle, the ant, the golden bee, all of them know their path to the point of wonder; without reason they bear witness to the mystery of God; they themselves accomplish it unceasingly; and I saw that the dear youth’s heart was kindled. He told me that he loved the forest and the forest birds; he was a bird-catcher; he understood every whistle of theirs and knew how to lure every bird. “I know nothing better than being in the forest,” he said, “though everything is good.” “Truly,” I answered him, “everything is good and glorious, because everything is truth. Look,” I said to him, “at the horse, a great animal that stands near to man, or at the ox that feeds him and works for him, downcast and thoughtful; look at their faces: what meekness, what attachment to man, who often beats him without mercy, what gentleness, what trustfulness and what beauty in his face. It is touching even to know that there is no sin on him, for everything is perfect; everything except man is sinless, and Christ is with them even before us.” “Can it be,” the youth asks, “that Christ is with them too?” “How could it be otherwise?” I tell him. “For the Word is for all; all creation and every creature, every little leaf, is striving towards the Word, sings glory to God, weeps to Christ, unknown to itself; it accomplishes this by the mystery of its sinless life. Look,” I tell him, “there in the forest wanders the fearsome bear, terrible and fierce, and not guilty of anything in it.” And I told him how once a bear came to a great saint who was working out his salvation in the forest in a little cell, and the great saint was moved with tenderness for him, went out to him fearlessly and gave him a piece of bread: “Go,” he said, “Christ be with you,” and the fierce beast went away obediently and meekly, without doing harm. And the youth was moved that it went away without doing harm, and that Christ was with it too. “Ah,” he said, “how good that is, how good and wonderful everything of God’s is!” He sat thinking, quietly and sweetly. I saw that he had understood. And he fell asleep beside me with a light, sinless sleep. Lord, bless youth! And I prayed for him myself as I lay down to sleep. Lord, send peace and light to thy people!
