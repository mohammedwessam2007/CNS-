## III

Take people who know how to avenge themselves and, in general, how to stand up for themselves. How do they do it? Once the feeling of revenge takes hold of them, say, there is nothing else left in their whole being for that time but that feeling. Such a gentleman charges straight at his goal like a maddened bull, horns lowered, and only a wall, perhaps, will stop him. (By the way: in front of a wall such gentlemen, that is, spontaneous people and men of action, sincerely give up. For them a wall is not an excuse, as it is for us, people who think and who therefore do nothing; not a pretext for turning back, a pretext which people like us usually don’t believe in ourselves, but which we are always very glad of. No, they give up in all sincerity. The wall has something soothing about it for them, something morally decisive and final, perhaps even something mystical... But more about the wall later.) Well, it is exactly this spontaneous man that I regard as the real, normal man, the one tender Mother Nature herself wished to see when she graciously brought him into being on earth. I envy such a man until I choke on my own bile. He is stupid, I won’t argue with you about that; but perhaps a normal man ought to be stupid, how do you know? Perhaps it is even very beautiful. And I am all the more convinced of this suspicion, so to speak, because if you take the opposite of the normal man, that is, the man of heightened consciousness, who came, of course, not out of the lap of nature but out of a laboratory flask (this is almost mysticism, gentlemen, but I suspect that too), then this test-tube man sometimes gives up so completely before his opposite that he himself, with all his heightened consciousness, honestly regards himself as a mouse and not a man. A highly conscious mouse, perhaps, but a mouse all the same, while the other is a man, and therefore... and so on. And the main thing is that he himself, he himself regards himself as a mouse; nobody asks him to; and that is an important point.

Now let us look at this mouse in action. Suppose, for example, that it too has been insulted (and it is almost always insulted) and that it too wants revenge. There may be even more spite piled up in it than in *l’homme de la nature et de la vérité*, the natural, truthful man. The nasty, low little wish to pay back the offender in kind may scratch at it even more nastily than at the natural man, because the natural man, in his inborn stupidity, regards his revenge quite simply as justice, while the mouse, because of its heightened consciousness, denies that there is any justice in it. At last it comes to the deed itself, to the very act of revenge. On top of the one original nastiness, the unhappy mouse has already managed to pile up around itself, in the form of questions and doubts, so many other nastinesses, and has hung so many unresolved questions on the one question, that whether it likes it or not a kind of fatal swill gathers around it, a stinking mess made of its doubts, its agitations and, finally, of the spit showered on it by the men of action, who stand solemnly around it like judges and dictators and laugh at it with all their healthy throats. Of course, there is nothing left for it but to wave the whole thing away with its little paw and, with a smile of pretended contempt that it doesn’t believe in itself, to slip shamefully back into its crack.

There, in its nasty, stinking underground, our insulted, beaten-down and ridiculed mouse at once sinks into a cold, venomous and, above all, everlasting spite. For forty years on end it will remember its insult down to the last, most shameful details, and each time it will add details of its own that are more shameful still, spitefully teasing and goading itself with its own imagination. It will be ashamed of its imagination, but it will still remember everything, go over everything, invent things about itself that never happened, on the pretext that they too might have happened, and it will forgive nothing. Perhaps it will even begin to take revenge, but in fits and starts, in petty ways, from behind the stove, incognito, believing neither in its right to revenge nor in the success of its revenge, and knowing in advance that from all its attempts at revenge it will suffer a hundred times more than the one it is taking revenge on, who perhaps won’t even scratch himself. On its deathbed it will remember everything again, with the interest that has piled up over all that time, and... But it is precisely in this cold, loathsome half-despair and half-belief, in this conscious burying of oneself alive out of grief, in the underground, for forty years; in this hopelessness, so carefully constructed and yet partly doubtful; in all this poison of unsatisfied desires turned inward; in all this fever of hesitations, of decisions taken forever and of regrets that come again a minute later: it is in all this that the juice of that strange pleasure I spoke of lies. It is so subtle, sometimes so hard for consciousness to grasp, that people who are even slightly limited, or simply people with strong nerves, will not understand a single feature of it. “Perhaps,” you will add with a grin, “people who have never had their faces slapped won’t understand it either,” and in this way you will politely hint that I too, perhaps, have had my face slapped in my time, and so I speak as an expert. I bet that is what you are thinking. But don’t worry, gentlemen, nobody has ever slapped my face, though it is all the same to me what you think about it. Perhaps I am even sorry that I have handed out so few slaps in my life. But enough: not another word about this subject, which you find so extremely interesting.

I will go on calmly about the people with strong nerves who do not understand a certain refinement of pleasure. Although these gentlemen, in certain circumstances, bellow like bulls at the top of their lungs, and although this, let us suppose, does them the greatest credit, yet, as I have already said, before the impossible they submit at once. The impossible means a stone wall? What stone wall? Why, the laws of nature, of course, the conclusions of the natural sciences, mathematics. Once they prove to you, for example, that you are descended from a monkey, there is no use making faces: accept it as it is. Once they prove to you that, in essence, one little drop of your own fat must be dearer to you than a hundred thousand people like you, and that in the end all so-called virtues and duties and other ravings and prejudices will be resolved by this conclusion, then just accept it; there is nothing to be done, because two times two is mathematics. Just try to object.

“Excuse me,” they will shout at you, “you can’t rebel: this is two times two makes four! Nature doesn’t ask your permission; it doesn’t care about your wishes, or whether you like its laws or not. You are obliged to accept it as it is, and therefore all its results as well. A wall, you see, is a wall... and so on and so on.” Good Lord, what do I care about the laws of nature and arithmetic, when for some reason I don’t like these laws, or two times two makes four? Of course I won’t break through such a wall with my forehead if I really don’t have the strength to break through it; but I won’t make peace with it either, just because it is a stone wall and I didn’t have the strength.

As if such a stone wall really were a comfort, and really held some word of peace for the world, simply because it is two times two makes four. Oh, absurdity of absurdities! How much better to understand everything, to be conscious of everything, all the impossibilities and stone walls; not to make peace with a single one of these impossibilities and stone walls if making peace disgusts you; to arrive, by the most inevitable logical steps, at the most revolting conclusions on the eternal theme that you are somehow to blame even for the stone wall, although once again it is perfectly obvious that you are not to blame at all; and as a result, silently and helplessly grinding your teeth, to freeze voluptuously in inertia, dreaming that it turns out there is not even anyone to be angry with; that no object can be found, and perhaps never will be; that there is a switch here, a sleight of hand, a card-sharper’s trick, that it is simply a mess: nobody knows what and nobody knows who. But in spite of all these unknowns and tricks, it still hurts you, and the more you don’t know, the more it hurts!

## IV

“Ha, ha, ha! Next you’ll be finding pleasure in toothache!” you will cry, laughing.

“Well, why not? There is pleasure in toothache too,” I will answer. “I had toothache for a whole month; I know there is. Of course, in that case people don’t rage in silence; they moan. But these are not honest moans; they are moans with malice in them, and the malice is the whole point. These moans express the sufferer’s pleasure; if he felt no pleasure in them, he wouldn’t moan. This is a good example, gentlemen, and I will develop it. What these moans express is, first, the whole pointlessness of your pain, so humiliating to our consciousness; the whole lawfulness of nature, which of course you don’t give a damn about, but which makes you suffer all the same, while it does not suffer at all. They express the awareness that no enemy is to be found, and yet the pain is there; the awareness that, for all the dentists in Petersburg, you are completely the slave of your teeth; that if someone wishes it, your teeth will stop aching, and if not, they will go on aching for another three months; and finally, that if you still don’t agree and go on protesting, all that is left for your own consolation is to whip yourself, or to beat your wall harder with your fist, and absolutely nothing else. Well, it is out of these bloody insults, out of this mockery from nobody knows whom, that the pleasure finally begins, a pleasure that sometimes reaches the height of voluptuousness. I beg you, gentlemen, listen some time to the moans of an educated man of the nineteenth century who has toothache, on the second or third day, when he is no longer moaning the way he did on the first day, that is, not simply because his teeth ache; not like some coarse peasant, but like a man touched by progress and European civilisation, a man who has ‘torn himself away from the soil and from the roots of the people’, as they put it nowadays. His moans become somehow nasty, vilely spiteful, and they go on for whole days and nights. And he knows himself that his moaning will do him no good at all; he knows better than anyone that he is only straining and irritating himself and others for nothing; he knows that even the audience he is performing for, and his whole family, have by now come to listen to him with disgust, don’t believe a word of it, and understand in their hearts that he could moan differently, more simply, without trills and flourishes, and that he is only doing it out of spite and malice. Well, it is in all these realisations and disgraces that the voluptuousness lies. ‘I am disturbing you, I am tearing at your hearts, I am keeping everyone in the house awake. Well then, stay awake; you too, feel every minute that I have toothache. I am no longer the hero to you that I wanted to seem before, just a nasty little man, a good-for-nothing. Well, so be it! I am very glad you have seen through me. You find it nasty to listen to my mean little moans? Well, let it be nasty; here, I’ll give you an even nastier trill...’ You still don’t understand, gentlemen? No, it seems you have to develop very far and become very conscious to understand all the twists of this voluptuousness. You are laughing? Delighted, sir. My jokes, gentlemen, are in bad taste, of course, uneven, muddled, full of self-distrust. But that is because I do not respect myself. Can a conscious man respect himself at all?”
