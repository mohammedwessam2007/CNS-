## Chapter XI

Levin drank his glass, and they were silent for a while.

“There is one more thing I must tell you. Do you know Vronsky?” Stepan Arkadyich asked Levin.

“No, I don’t. Why do you ask?”

“Bring another,” said Stepan Arkadyich, turning to the Tatar, who was filling up their glasses and hovering about them precisely when he was not wanted.

“Why should I know Vronsky?”

“The reason you should know Vronsky is that he is one of your rivals.”

“And what is Vronsky?” said Levin, and his face changed suddenly from that childishly rapturous expression which Oblonsky had just been admiring to an angry and unpleasant one.

“Vronsky is one of the sons of Count Kirill Ivanovich Vronsky, and one of the finest specimens of the gilded youth of Petersburg. I got to know him in Tver, when I was in the service there and he came for the recruiting. Terribly rich, handsome, great connections, an aide-de-camp, and at the same time a very nice, good-natured fellow. But more than just a good-natured fellow. As I have come to know him here, he is both cultivated and very clever; he is a man who will go far.”

Levin frowned and was silent.

“Well, he turned up here soon after you left, and, as I understand it, he is head over ears in love with Kitty, and you understand that her mother…”

“Excuse me, but I don’t understand anything,” said Levin, scowling gloomily. And at once he remembered his brother Nikolai, and how vile he was to have been able to forget him.

“Wait, wait,” said Stepan Arkadyich, smiling and touching his hand. “I have told you what I know, and I repeat that in this delicate, tender business, as far as one can guess, it seems to me that the chances are on your side.”

Levin leaned back in his chair; his face was pale.

“But I would advise you to settle the matter as quickly as possible,” Oblonsky went on, filling up his glass.

“No, thank you, I can’t drink any more,” said Levin, pushing his glass away. “I shall be drunk… Well, and how are you getting on?” he went on, evidently wanting to change the subject.

“One word more: in any case I advise you to settle the question soon. I don’t advise you to speak tonight,” said Stepan Arkadyich. “Go tomorrow morning, in the classic way, and make your proposal, and God bless you…”

“Why is it you kept meaning to come and shoot with me? Come in the spring, for the evening flight of the woodcock,” said Levin.

Now he repented with all his soul that he had begun this conversation with Stepan Arkadyich. His special feeling had been profaned by talk of the rivalry of some Petersburg officer, by Stepan Arkadyich’s suppositions and advice.

Stepan Arkadyich smiled. He understood what was going on in Levin’s soul.

“I’ll come some time,” he said. “Yes, my dear fellow, women are the screw on which everything turns. My own affairs are bad, very bad. And all because of women. Tell me frankly,” he went on, taking out a cigar and keeping one hand on his glass, “give me some advice.”

“But about what?”

“About this. Suppose you are married, you love your wife, but you have been carried away by another woman…”

“Excuse me, but I simply don’t understand that; it’s just as if… just as I can’t understand how I could now, after eating my fill here, walk past a baker’s shop and steal a roll.”

Stepan Arkadyich’s eyes shone more than usual.

“Why not? A roll sometimes smells so good that one can’t resist.

> Heavenly it is when I have conquered
> All my earthly appetite;
> But if I should fail to conquer,
> Still I’ve had a fine delight!”

As he said this, Stepan Arkadyich smiled subtly. Levin too could not help smiling.

“Yes, but joking aside,” Oblonsky went on. “You must understand that the woman is a sweet, gentle, loving creature, poor, alone, and she has sacrificed everything. Now, when the thing is done, you understand, can one really abandon her? Suppose we part, so as not to destroy family life; but is one not to pity her, not to provide for her, not to soften things for her?”

“Well, you must excuse me. You know, for me all women are divided into two sorts… that is, no… rather: there are women, and there are… I have never seen any charming fallen creatures and never shall, and women like that painted Frenchwoman at the counter, with her ringlets, are vermin to me, and all fallen women are the same.”

“And the one in the Gospel?”

“Oh, stop it! Christ would never have said those words if he had known how they would be abused. Out of the whole Gospel those are the only words people remember. However, I am saying not what I think but what I feel. I have a loathing for fallen women. You are afraid of spiders, and I am afraid of these vermin. You have probably never studied spiders and don’t know their habits: it’s the same with me.”

“It’s all very well for you to talk like that; it’s like that gentleman in Dickens who tosses every awkward question over his right shoulder with his left hand. But denying a fact is not an answer. What is to be done, you tell me, what is to be done? Your wife grows old, and you are full of life. Before you know where you are, you feel that you cannot love your wife with love, however much you may respect her. And then suddenly love turns up, and you are lost, lost!” Stepan Arkadyich said with dejected despair.

Levin smiled.

“Yes, lost,” Oblonsky went on. “But what is to be done?”

“Don’t steal rolls.”

Stepan Arkadyich laughed.

“Oh, you moralist! But you must understand, there are two women: one insists only on her rights, and those rights are your love, which you cannot give her; and the other sacrifices everything to you and demands nothing. What are you to do? How are you to act? There is a terrible drama here.”

“If you want my confession on that, I will tell you that I don’t believe there is any drama here. And here is why. To my mind, love… both kinds of love, which, you remember, Plato defines in his Symposium, both kinds serve as a touchstone for men. Some men understand only the one, others the other. And those who understand only the non-Platonic love talk of drama for nothing. With that kind of love there can be no drama at all. ‘I humbly thank you for the pleasure, my respects,’ and that is the whole drama. And for Platonic love there can be no drama, because in such a love everything is clear and pure, because…”

At that moment Levin remembered his own sins and the inner struggle he had lived through. And he added unexpectedly:

“But then, perhaps you are right. It may very well be… But I don’t know, I really don’t know.”

“There, you see,” said Stepan Arkadyich, “you are a very whole man. That is your quality and your failing. You have a whole character yourself, and you want all of life to be made up of whole things, and that is not how it is. You despise public service, for instance, because you want the work always to correspond to its aim, and that is not how it is. You want, too, every man’s activity always to have an aim, and love and family life always to be one and the same. And that is not how it is. All the variety, all the charm, all the beauty of life is made up of light and shade.”

Levin sighed and made no answer. He was thinking of his own affairs and not listening to Oblonsky.

And suddenly they both felt that, though they were friends, though they had dined together and drunk wine that should have drawn them still closer, each was thinking only of his own affairs, and neither cared about the other. Oblonsky had more than once experienced this extreme division instead of closeness that happens after dinner, and he knew what to do in such cases.

“The bill!” he called, and went into the next room, where he at once met an aide-de-camp he knew and got into a conversation with him about an actress and the man who kept her. And at once, in his talk with the aide-de-camp, Oblonsky felt relief and rest from his conversation with Levin, who always drew him into too great a strain of mind and heart.

When the Tatar appeared with the bill for twenty-six roubles and some kopecks, plus something for himself, Levin, who at another time, as a countryman, would have been horrified at his share of fourteen roubles, now paid no attention to it, paid, and went home to change and go to the Shcherbatskys’, where his fate would be decided.
