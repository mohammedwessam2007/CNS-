It was then that Akaky Akakievich saw that he could not do without a new overcoat, and his spirits sank completely. How, indeed, with what, with what money could it be made? Of course, he could rely partly on the future holiday bonus, but that money had long ago been allotted and divided up in advance. He needed new trousers; he had to pay the cobbler an old debt for putting new uppers on old boot-legs; and he ought to order three shirts from the seamstress, and two of those undergarments which it is improper to name in print; in short, all the money was bound to go; and even if the director were so gracious as to award him forty-five or fifty roubles instead of the forty roubles bonus, there would still be only some trifle left, which in the capital needed for an overcoat would be a drop in the ocean. Although, of course, he knew that Petrovich had a whim of suddenly asking the devil knows what outrageous price, so that even his wife could not help crying out: “Are you out of your mind, you fool! Another time he’ll take a job for nothing, and now the devil has put it into him to ask a price he isn’t worth himself.” Although, of course, he knew that Petrovich would take the job on for eighty roubles; but even so, where could he get those eighty roubles? Half might be found: half could be found; perhaps even a little more; but where to get the other half?.. But first the reader must learn where the first half came from. Akaky Akakievich had the habit, out of every rouble he spent, of putting aside a kopeck into a little box, locked with a key, with a hole cut in the lid for dropping money in. At the end of every half year he would inspect the copper sum that had accumulated and exchange it for small silver. He had kept this up for a long time, and so in the course of several years the accumulated sum had come to more than forty roubles. So half was in hand; but where to get the other half? Where to get another forty roubles? Akaky Akakievich thought and thought, and decided that he would have to cut down his ordinary expenses, at least for one year: give up drinking tea in the evenings, not light a candle in the evenings, and if he needed to do anything, go into his landlady’s room and work by her candle; when walking in the streets, step as lightly and carefully as possible on the stones and flagstones, almost on tiptoe, so as not to wear out his soles too soon; send his linen to the laundress as rarely as possible, and so that it would not wear out, take it off every time he came home and stay in only his cotton dressing gown, very old and spared even by time itself. It must be told truthfully that at first it was somewhat hard for him to get used to such restrictions, but then he somehow got used to them and things went smoothly; he even became quite accustomed to going hungry in the evenings; but on the other hand he was nourished spiritually, carrying in his thoughts the eternal idea of the future overcoat. From then on it was as if his very existence had somehow become fuller, as if he had married, as if some other person were present with him, as if he were not alone but some pleasant companion of life had agreed to walk the road of life together with him; and this companion was none other than that same overcoat with thick wadding and a strong lining that would never wear out. He became somehow livelier, even firmer in character, like a man who has already defined and set himself a goal. Doubt and indecision disappeared of their own accord from his face and his actions; in a word, all wavering and indefinite traits. At times a fire showed in his eyes; the boldest and most daring thoughts even flashed through his head: why not, indeed, put marten on the collar? Thinking about it almost made him absent-minded. Once, while copying a paper, he very nearly made a mistake, so that he almost cried “Oh!” aloud and crossed himself. At least once every month he called on Petrovich to talk about the overcoat, where best to buy the cloth, and what colour, and at what price; and, though somewhat anxious, he always came home contented, thinking that the time would come at last when all this would be bought and the overcoat would be made. Things went even faster than he had expected. Contrary to all expectations, the director awarded Akaky Akakievich not forty or forty-five roubles, but a whole sixty; whether he had a presentiment that Akaky Akakievich needed an overcoat, or whether it just happened so, at any rate this left him with twenty roubles extra. This circumstance hastened matters. Another two or three months of a little hunger, and Akaky Akakievich had indeed saved about eighty roubles. His heart, generally very calm, began to beat. On the very first day he went with Petrovich to the shops. They bought very good cloth, and no wonder, since they had been thinking about it for half a year before, and hardly a month had gone by without their going into the shops to compare prices; so Petrovich himself said there was no better cloth to be had. For the lining they chose calico, but calico so sound and thick that, according to Petrovich, it was even better than silk, and even handsomer and glossier to look at. They did not buy marten, because it really was expensive; instead they chose cat, the best to be found in the shop, cat which from a distance could always be taken for marten. Petrovich spent only two weeks on the overcoat, because there was a great deal of quilting; otherwise it would have been ready sooner. Petrovich charged twelve roubles for the work; less was quite impossible: everything was sewn in silk, with a double fine seam, and afterwards Petrovich went over every seam with his own teeth, pressing various patterns into it. It was... it is hard to say on exactly which day, but probably on the most solemn day in the life of Akaky Akakievich, that Petrovich brought the overcoat at last. He brought it in the morning, just before the time when Akaky Akakievich had to leave for the department. At no other time could an overcoat have come more opportunely, because rather hard frosts were already beginning and seemed to threaten to grow harder still. Petrovich appeared with the overcoat as a good tailor should. His face wore an expression so significant that Akaky Akakievich had never seen anything like it. He seemed to feel to the full that he had done no small thing, and had suddenly revealed in himself the abyss that separates tailors who only put in linings and do alterations from those who make new garments. He took the overcoat out of the handkerchief in which he had brought it; the handkerchief had just come from the laundress, and afterwards he folded it and put it in his pocket for use. Having taken out the overcoat, he looked at it very proudly and, holding it in both hands, threw it very deftly over Akaky Akakievich’s shoulders; then he pulled it and smoothed it down at the back with his hand; then he draped it on Akaky Akakievich somewhat open in front. Akaky Akakievich, as a man of a certain age, wanted to try it with his arms in the sleeves; Petrovich helped him put it on with his arms in the sleeves, and it turned out that with the sleeves too it was good. In a word, it turned out that the overcoat fitted perfectly and exactly. Petrovich did not miss the opportunity to say that it was only because he lived without a signboard on a small street, and had besides known Akaky Akakievich for a long time, that he had charged so little; on Nevsky Prospect they would have charged him seventy-five roubles for the work alone. Akaky Akakievich did not want to discuss this with Petrovich, and was afraid besides of all the big sums with which Petrovich liked to throw dust in people’s eyes. He paid him, thanked him, and went out at once in his new overcoat to the department. Petrovich went out after him and, staying in the street, looked for a long time from a distance at the overcoat, and then deliberately went off to one side so that, cutting round by a crooked lane, he could run out into the street again and look at his overcoat once more from the other side, that is, straight in the face. Meanwhile Akaky Akakievich walked along in the most festive frame of mind. Every instant of every minute he felt that he had a new overcoat on his shoulders, and several times he even smiled from inner pleasure. Indeed, there were two advantages: one, that it was warm, and the other, that it was fine. He did not notice the road at all and found himself suddenly in the department; in the porter’s lodge he took off his overcoat, looked it all over, and entrusted it to the porter’s special care. Nobody knows how, but everyone in the department suddenly learned that Akaky Akakievich had a new overcoat and that the dressing gown no longer existed. At that very minute they all ran out to the porter’s lodge to look at Akaky Akakievich’s new overcoat. They began to congratulate him and greet him, so that at first he only smiled, and then he even began to feel ashamed. And when they all came up to him and began to say that the new overcoat must be wetted, and that at the very least he must give them all a party, Akaky Akakievich lost his head completely; he did not know what to do, what to answer, or how to get out of it. A few minutes later, blushing all over, he began quite simple-heartedly to assure them that it was not a new overcoat at all, that it was nothing, that it was an old overcoat. At last one of the clerks, an assistant head clerk no less, probably to show that he was not proud at all and mixed even with those beneath him, said: “Very well, I will give a party instead of Akaky Akakievich, and I invite you all to tea at my place today: as it happens, it’s my name day today.” The clerks, naturally, at once congratulated the assistant head clerk and accepted the invitation gladly. Akaky Akakievich began to make excuses, but they all began to say that it was impolite, that it was simply a shame and a disgrace, and he could not possibly refuse. Afterwards, though, he felt pleased when he remembered that it would give him the chance to walk out in his new overcoat even in the evening. That whole day was for Akaky Akakievich like the greatest solemn holiday. He came home in the happiest frame of mind, took off his overcoat and hung it carefully on the wall, admiring the cloth and the lining once more; and then, for comparison, he deliberately pulled out his former dressing gown, which had completely fallen apart. He looked at it and even laughed himself: such a vast difference! And for a long time afterwards, at dinner, he kept smiling whenever he thought of the state the dressing gown was in. He dined cheerfully, and after dinner he wrote nothing at all, no papers, but just played the sybarite a little on his bed until it grew dark. Then, without delaying, he dressed, put the overcoat on his shoulders, and went out into the street. Where exactly the clerk who had invited him lived, we regret we cannot say: our memory is beginning to fail us badly, and everything in Petersburg, all the streets and houses, have so blended and mixed together in our head that it is very hard to get anything out of it in decent order. Be that as it may, it is certain at least that the clerk lived in the best part of the town, and therefore very far from Akaky Akakievich. At first Akaky Akakievich had to pass through some deserted streets with scanty lighting, but as he came nearer to the clerk’s flat the streets became livelier, more crowded and more brightly lit. Pedestrians began to flash by more often; ladies began to appear, beautifully dressed; beaver collars could be seen on the men; there were fewer poor cabbies with their wooden latticed sledges studded with gilt nails; on the contrary, there were more and more smart drivers in raspberry velvet caps, with lacquered sledges and bearskin rugs, and carriages with ornamented boxes flew down the street, their wheels squealing on the snow. Akaky Akakievich looked at all this as at something new. For several years he had not gone out into the street in the evening. He stopped with curiosity in front of the lighted window of a shop to look at a picture of some beautiful woman taking off her shoe, thereby baring her whole leg, a very nice one; while behind her back, from the door of another room, a man with side-whiskers and a handsome little tuft of beard under his lip was poking his head out. Akaky Akakievich shook his head and smiled, and then went on his way. Why he smiled, whether because he had come across something completely unfamiliar to him, but of which everyone nevertheless keeps some sort of instinct, or whether he thought, like many other clerks, something like this: “Well, those French! What can one say! If they want something, you know, then it’s really, you know...” Or perhaps he did not even think that: after all, one cannot climb into a man’s soul and find out everything he thinks. At last he reached the house where the assistant head clerk lived. The assistant head clerk lived in grand style: a lantern shone on the stairs, and the flat was on the second floor. Going into the hall, Akaky Akakievich saw whole rows of galoshes on the floor. Among them, in the middle of the room, stood a samovar, humming and sending out clouds of steam. On the walls hung overcoats and cloaks, some of them even with beaver collars or velvet lapels. Beyond the wall one could hear noise and talk, which suddenly became clear and loud when the door opened and a footman came out with a tray of empty glasses, a cream jug and a basket of rusks. It was evident that the clerks had gathered long ago and had already drunk their first glass of tea. Akaky Akakievich, having hung up his overcoat himself, went into the room, and before him flashed all at once candles, clerks, pipes, card tables, and his ears were vaguely struck by the rapid talk rising on all sides and the noise of chairs being moved. He stopped very awkwardly in the middle of the room, looking about and trying to think what to do. But they had already noticed him, received him with a shout, and they all went out at once into the hall and looked over his overcoat again. Although Akaky Akakievich was somewhat embarrassed, being a simple-hearted man he could not help rejoicing to see how everyone praised the overcoat. Then, of course, they all abandoned both him and the overcoat and turned, as usual, to the tables set out for whist. All this, the noise, the talk and the crowd of people, was somehow strange to Akaky Akakievich. He simply did not know what to do, where to put his hands, his feet and his whole figure; at last he sat down by the players, looked at the cards, peered into one face and another, and after a while began to yawn and feel bored, all the more since the time when he usually went to bed had long since come. He wanted to say goodbye to the host, but they would not let him go, saying that he absolutely must drink a glass of champagne in honour of the new acquisition. An hour later supper was served, consisting of salad, cold veal, pâté, pastries and champagne. They made Akaky Akakievich drink two glasses, after which he felt that the room had become merrier; but he could not forget that it was already midnight and long past time to go home. So that the host should not somehow take it into his head to keep him, he left the room quietly, found his overcoat in the hall, which he saw, not without regret, lying on the floor, shook it, picked off every bit of fluff, put it on his shoulders and went down the stairs into the street. In the street it was still light. Some little shops, those permanent clubs of house servants and all sorts of people, were open; others, which were shut, still showed a long stream of light all along the crack of the door, showing that they were not yet without company, and that probably maids or menservants were still finishing their gossip and talk, leaving their masters in complete bewilderment as to where they were. Akaky Akakievich walked along in a cheerful frame of mind; he even suddenly began, nobody knows why, to run after some lady who passed by like lightning and every part of whose body was full of extraordinary movement. But he stopped at once and went on again very slowly as before, even wondering himself at the trot that had come from heaven knows where. Soon there stretched before him those deserted streets which are not very cheerful even in the daytime, still less in the evening. Now they had become even more remote and lonely: the street lamps began to flicker more rarely (less oil was evidently being supplied); wooden houses and fences began; nowhere a soul; only the snow glittered in the streets, and the low, sleeping hovels with their shutters closed showed sadly black. He came near the place where the street was cut across by an endless square with houses barely visible on its far side, which looked like a terrible desert.

Far away, God knows where, a light flickered in some watchman’s box which seemed to stand at the end of the world. Akaky Akakievich’s cheerfulness somehow diminished considerably here. He stepped out onto the square not without some involuntary fear, as though his heart had a presentiment of something bad. He looked back and to either side: it was just like a sea around him. “No, better not even look,” he thought, and walked on with his eyes closed; and when he opened them to see whether the end of the square was near, he suddenly saw standing in front of him, almost under his nose, some men with moustaches; who exactly, he could not even make out. Everything went misty before his eyes and his heart began to pound. “But that overcoat is mine!” said one of them in a voice like thunder, seizing him by the collar. Akaky Akakievich was about to shout “Help!” when the other put a fist the size of a clerk’s head right up to his mouth, adding: “Just you shout!” Akaky Akakievich felt only that they took the overcoat off him and gave him a kick with a knee, and he fell on his back in the snow and felt nothing more. A few minutes later he came to and got to his feet, but there was no one there. He felt that it was cold in that open space and that his overcoat was gone; he began to shout, but his voice seemed to have no intention of reaching the ends of the square. In despair, shouting without stopping, he set off running across the square straight to the box, beside which stood a watchman, leaning on his halberd and looking, it seemed, with curiosity, wanting to know what the devil a man was running towards him from a distance for, shouting. Akaky Akakievich, running up to him, began to shout in a breathless voice that he was asleep and was not watching anything, and did not see a man being robbed. The watchman answered that he had seen nothing; that he had seen two men stop him in the middle of the square, but had thought they were his friends; and that instead of scolding for nothing, he had better go to the inspector tomorrow, and the inspector would find out who had taken the overcoat. Akaky Akakievich ran home in complete disorder: the hair that he still had in small quantities on his temples and the back of his head was completely dishevelled; his side, his chest and his whole trousers were covered with snow. The old woman, his landlady, hearing a terrible knocking at the door, jumped hastily out of bed and, with a shoe on only one foot, ran to open the door, modestly holding her nightdress together at her breast with her hand; but when she opened it, she stepped back, seeing Akaky Akakievich in such a state. And when he told her what had happened, she threw up her hands and said that he must go straight to the district superintendent; that the local constable would cheat him, make promises and lead him a dance; that it was best to go straight to the superintendent, whom she even knew, because Anna, a Finnish woman who used to be her cook, now had a place as nanny at the superintendent’s; that she often saw him himself as he drove past their house; and that he went to church every Sunday and prayed, and at the same time looked cheerfully at everyone, and that therefore, by all appearances, he must be a kind man. Having heard this decision, Akaky Akakievich trudged sadly to his room; and how he spent the night there may be judged by anyone who can in the least imagine the position of another. Early in the morning he went to the superintendent’s; but they said he was asleep. He came at ten: again they said he was asleep. He came at eleven: they said the superintendent was not at home. He came at dinner time, but the clerks in the hall would not let him in and wanted to know at all costs on what business, and what need had brought him, and what had happened. So that at last Akaky Akakievich, for once in his life, wanted to show some character, and said flatly that he must see the superintendent himself in person, that they dare not keep him out, that he had come from his department on government business, and that if he made a complaint against them, they would see. The clerks dared not say anything against this, and one of them went to call the superintendent. The superintendent received the story of the robbery of the overcoat in an extremely strange way. Instead of paying attention to the main point of the matter, he began to question Akaky Akakievich: why was he coming home so late, and had he not been calling at some house of ill repute; so that Akaky Akakievich was completely embarrassed, and left him without knowing himself whether the matter of the overcoat would take its proper course or not. All that day he did not go to the office (the only time in his life). The next day he appeared all pale and in his old dressing gown, which had become still more pitiful. The story of the robbery of the overcoat, although there were clerks who did not miss the chance even here to laugh at Akaky Akakievich, nevertheless touched many. They decided at once to take up a collection for him, but collected the merest trifle, because the clerks had already spent a great deal, subscribing for the director’s portrait and for some book or other, at the suggestion of the head of the section, who was a friend of the author; so the sum turned out to be the merest trifle. Someone, moved by compassion, decided at least to help Akaky Akakievich with good advice, telling him not to go to the local constable, because, although it might happen that the constable, wishing to earn the approval of his superiors, would somehow find the overcoat, the overcoat would still remain with the police unless he could produce legal proof that it belonged to him; it would be best of all for him to apply to a certain important person; that the important person, by writing and communicating with the proper people, could make the matter go forward more successfully. There was nothing for it; Akaky Akakievich decided to go to the important person. What exactly the post of the important person was, and what it consisted of, has remained unknown to this day. It should be known that a certain important person had only recently become an important person, and until then had been an unimportant person. Even now, as a matter of fact, his position was not considered important in comparison with others still more important. But there will always be a circle of people for whom what is unimportant in the eyes of others is already important. However, he tried to increase his importance by many other means, namely: he introduced the rule that the lower clerks should meet him on the stairs when he arrived at the office; that no one should dare to come to him directly, but that everything should go in the strictest order: a collegiate registrar should report to a provincial secretary, the provincial secretary to a titular councillor or whoever it might be, and only in this way should a matter reach him. So it is in holy Russia: everything is infected with imitation; everyone mimics and apes his superior. They even say that a certain titular councillor, when he was made head of some small separate office, at once partitioned off a special room for himself, calling it the “audience chamber”, and put at the door some ushers with red collars and gold braid, who would take hold of the door handle and open it for everyone who came, though an ordinary writing desk could barely fit into the “audience chamber”. The manners and customs of the important person were solid and majestic, but not complicated. The main foundation of his system was strictness. “Strictness, strictness and strictness,” he used to say, and at the last word he usually looked very significantly into the face of the person he was speaking to. Though there was no reason for this, since the dozen clerks who made up the whole administrative mechanism of the office were in a proper state of fear as it was; seeing him from a distance, they would leave their work and wait standing at attention until their chief had passed through the room. His usual conversation with his subordinates rang with strictness and consisted almost entirely of three phrases: “How dare you? Do you know whom you are speaking to? Do you understand who is standing before you?” In his heart, however, he was a kind man, good to his colleagues and obliging; but the rank of general had completely turned his head. Having received the rank of general, he somehow got muddled, lost his way, and did not know at all how to behave. When he happened to be with his equals, he was still a proper man, a very decent man, in many respects even not a stupid man; but as soon as he happened to be in company where there were people even one rank below him, he was simply hopeless: he said nothing, and his position aroused pity, all the more since he himself felt that he could have spent the time incomparably better. In his eyes one could sometimes see a strong desire to join some interesting conversation and circle, but he was stopped by the thought: would this not be too much on his part, would it not be too familiar, and would he not lower his importance by it? And as a result of such reasoning he remained forever in the same silent state, uttering only now and then some monosyllabic sounds, and so he acquired the title of the most boring of men. It was to this important person that our Akaky Akakievich came; and he came at the most unfavourable time, very inopportunely for himself, though opportunely for the important person. The important person was in his study, talking very, very cheerfully with an old acquaintance and childhood friend who had recently arrived and whom he had not seen for several years. At this moment it was announced to him that someone called Bashmachkin had come. He asked abruptly: “Who is he?” They answered: “Some clerk.” “Ah! He can wait; this is not the time,” said the important man. Here it must be said that the important man was telling a downright lie: he had the time; he and his friend had long ago talked about everything and had for a long time been filling the conversation with very long silences, only patting each other lightly on the thigh and saying: “So it is, Ivan Abramovich!” “Just so, Stepan Varlamovich!” But for all that he told the clerk to wait, in order to show his friend, a man who had long been out of the service and had stayed too long at home in the country, how long clerks were kept waiting in his anteroom. At last, having talked his fill, and still more having been silent his fill, and having smoked a cigar in a very comfortable armchair with a reclining back, he seemed suddenly to remember, and said to his secretary, who had stopped at the door with papers to report: “Oh yes, I believe a clerk is standing out there; tell him he may come in.” Seeing Akaky Akakievich’s humble appearance and his old uniform, he turned to him suddenly and said: “What do you want?”, in an abrupt and firm voice which he had practised beforehand in his room, alone and in front of the mirror, a week before he received his present post and the rank of general. Akaky Akakievich had already felt the proper timidity in good time, became somewhat confused and, as well as he could, as far as the freedom of his tongue allowed, explained, adding even more often than usual the particle “you know”, that there had been an overcoat, completely new, and that now he had been robbed of it in an inhuman way, and that he was applying to him so that, by his intercession, he might somehow, you know, write to the chief of police or someone else and find the overcoat. The general, for some unknown reason, found such a way of addressing him too familiar.

“What, my dear sir,” he went on abruptly, “don’t you know the procedure? Where have you come to? Don’t you know how business is conducted? You should first have submitted a petition about this to the office; it would have gone to the head clerk, to the head of the section, then it would have been passed to the secretary, and the secretary would have brought it to me...”

“But, Your Excellency,” said Akaky Akakievich, trying to gather together all the small handful of presence of mind that he had, and feeling at the same time that he was sweating terribly, “I ventured to trouble Your Excellency because secretaries, you know... are an unreliable lot...”

“What, what, what?” said the important person. “Where did you get such spirit? Where did you get such ideas? What insubordination is this that has spread among young people against their chiefs and superiors!”
