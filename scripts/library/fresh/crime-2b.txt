## CHAPTER IV

His mother’s letter had tormented him. But about the chief, the capital point, he had not had a moment’s doubt, even while he was still reading the letter. The chief substance of the matter was decided in his head, and decided finally: “This marriage will not happen while I am alive, and to the devil with Mr Luzhin!”

“Because the thing is obvious,” he muttered to himself, grinning and maliciously triumphing in advance at the success of his decision. “No, Mama, no, Dunya, you won’t deceive me!.. And they apologise, too, for not asking my advice and deciding the matter without me! I should think so! They think it can’t be broken off now; well, we shall see whether it can or not! And what a capital excuse: ‘Pyotr Petrovich is such a man of business, such a man of business, that he can’t even get married except by post-chaise, almost by railway.’ No, Dunechka, I see everything, and I know what you mean to talk to me about so much; I know too what you thought about all night, walking about the room, and what you prayed about before the Kazan Mother of God that stands in Mama’s bedroom. It is hard to climb Golgotha. Hm... So it is finally decided: you are pleased to marry a man of business and of reason, Avdotya Romanovna, who has capital of his own (already has capital of his own, that is more solid, more impressive), who holds two posts and shares the convictions of our newest generations (as Mama writes), and who ‘seems kind’, as Dunechka herself remarks. That ‘seems’ is the most magnificent of all! And this same Dunechka is marrying for this same ‘seems’!.. Magnificent! Magnificent!..

“...But I wonder why Mama wrote to me about ‘the newest generations’? Simply to describe the person, or with a further aim: to win me over to Mr Luzhin? Oh, the cunning things! I should like to clear up one more circumstance: how far were they frank with each other, that day and that night, and all the time since? Were all the words between them said straight out, or did both understand that they had the same thing in their hearts and minds, so that there was no point in saying it all aloud and letting things slip for nothing? Probably it was partly like that; one can see from the letter: he seemed abrupt to Mama, a little, and naive Mama went to Dunya with her remarks. And she, of course, was angry and ‘answered with vexation’. I should think so! Who would not be furious, when the thing is clear without naive questions, and when it is decided that there is nothing to say? And why does she write to me: ‘Love Dunya, Rodya, for she loves you more than herself’? Is it not her conscience secretly tormenting her for agreeing to sacrifice her daughter to her son? ‘You are our hope, you are our everything!’ Oh, Mama!..” Anger boiled up in him more and more, and if Mr Luzhin had met him now, he would, it seems, have killed him!

“Hm, it is true,” he went on, following the whirlwind of thoughts that spun in his head, “it is true that one must ‘approach a man gradually and carefully to find out what he is like’; but Mr Luzhin is clear. Above all, ‘a man of business and, it seems, kind’: no small thing, he has taken on the luggage, he is sending the big trunk at his own expense! How can he not be kind? And they, the two of them, the bride and her mother, hire a peasant, in a cart covered with matting (I have travelled like that)! Never mind! Only ninety versts, ‘and from there we shall ride very comfortably third class’, a thousand versts or so. And sensibly: cut your coat according to your cloth; but you, Mr Luzhin, what about you? She is your bride, after all... And you cannot have failed to know that her mother is borrowing on her pension for the journey? Of course, with you it is a joint commercial transaction, an enterprise for mutual advantage and in equal shares, so the expenses are halved too; bread and salt together, but each pays for his own tobacco, as the proverb says. And even here the man of business has cheated them a little: the luggage costs less than their fare, and it may even go for nothing. Do they really not see this, the two of them, or are they not noticing it on purpose? And they are pleased, pleased! And to think that these are only the blossoms, and the real fruits are still to come! For what matters here is not the meanness, not the stinginess, but the tone of it all. For that is the tone of the future after the marriage, a prophecy... And Mama, though, why is she on such a spree? What will she arrive in Petersburg with? With three roubles, or two ‘little notes’, as that... old woman says... hm! What does she hope to live on in Petersburg afterwards? For she has already, for some reason, managed to guess that she cannot live with Dunya after the marriage, even at first? The dear man must have let something slip there somehow, made himself known, though Mama waves it away with both hands: ‘I shall refuse myself.’ What, then, who is she relying on: on her hundred and twenty roubles’ pension, less the deduction for the debt to Afanasy Ivanovich? She knits winter shawls there, and embroiders cuffs, and ruins her old eyes. But the shawls only add twenty roubles a year to the hundred and twenty, that I know. So they are relying after all on the nobility of Mr Luzhin’s feelings: ‘He will offer himself, he will beg us.’ Don’t count on it! And that is how it always is with these beautiful Schillerian souls: to the last moment they dress a man in peacock feathers, to the last moment they hope for good and not for bad; and although they have a presentiment of the other side of the medal, they will on no account say the real word to themselves in advance; the mere thought of it makes them shudder; they wave the truth away with both hands, until the very moment when the dressed-up man makes fools of them with his own hands. I wonder whether Mr Luzhin has any decorations; I bet he has the Anna in his buttonhole, and wears it to dinners with contractors and merchants. Perhaps he will wear it at his wedding too! But to the devil with him!..

“...Well, let Mama be, God be with her, that is how she is; but what about Dunya? Dunechka, my dear, I know you! You were already in your twentieth year when we last saw each other: I understood your character already. Mama writes that ‘Dunechka can bear a great deal’. I knew that. I knew that two and a half years ago, and for two and a half years since I have been thinking about it, about precisely that, that ‘Dunechka can bear a great deal’. If she can bear Mr Svidrigailov, with all the consequences, then she really can bear a great deal. And now they have imagined, she and Mama, that Mr Luzhin too can be borne, setting out his theory of the superiority of wives taken from destitution and made beholden to their husbands, and setting it out almost at the first meeting. Well, suppose he ‘let it slip’, though he is a man of reason (so that perhaps he did not let it slip at all, but meant precisely to make it clear as soon as possible); but Dunya, Dunya? The man is clear to her, and she has to live with the man. She would eat nothing but black bread and drink water with it, but she would not sell her soul, she would not give up her moral freedom for comfort; she would not give it up for the whole of Schleswig-Holstein, let alone for Mr Luzhin. No, Dunya was not like that, as far as I knew her, and... well, of course, she has not changed now either!.. What is there to say! The Svidrigailovs are hard to bear! It is hard to drag around the provinces all one’s life as a governess for two hundred roubles; but still I know that my sister would sooner become a negro slave to a planter, or a Latvian serf to a Baltic German, than debase her spirit and her moral sense by binding herself to a man she does not respect and with whom she has nothing to do, for ever, for her own personal advantage alone! And even if Mr Luzhin were made all of the purest gold or of one whole diamond, even then she would not agree to become Mr Luzhin’s lawful concubine! Why does she agree now, then? What is the trick? What is the answer to the riddle? The thing is clear: for herself, for her own comfort, even to save herself from death, she would not sell herself; but for someone else, there, she does sell herself! For someone dear, for someone adored, she will sell herself! That is what our whole trick consists of: for her brother, for her mother she will sell herself! She will sell everything! Oh, in this case we will even crush our moral sense if need be; freedom, peace, even conscience, everything, everything we will take to the flea market. Let life go! As long as these beloved beings of ours are happy. More than that, we will invent our own casuistry, we will learn from the Jesuits, and for a while, perhaps, we will even calm ourselves, convince ourselves that it is necessary, really necessary for a good end. That is just how we are, and everything is as clear as day. It is clear that it is none other than Rodion Romanovich Raskolnikov who is the point here and stands in the foreground. Well, of course: she can arrange his happiness, keep him at the university, make him a partner in the office, secure his whole fate; perhaps he will be a rich man later on, honoured, respected, and perhaps even end his life a famous man! And Mother? But this is Rodya, precious Rodya, the first-born! How could one not sacrifice even such a daughter for such a first-born! Oh, loving and unjust hearts! Why, here we would not even refuse Sonechka’s lot, perhaps! Sonechka, Sonechka Marmeladova, eternal Sonechka, as long as the world stands! Have you both fully measured the sacrifice, the sacrifice? Have you? Is it within your strength? Is it for the good? Is it reasonable? Do you know, Dunechka, that Sonechka’s lot is no fouler than the lot with Mr Luzhin? ‘There can be no love here,’ Mama writes. And what if, besides love, there can be no respect either, but on the contrary there is already aversion, contempt, disgust, what then? Then it turns out that once again one will have to ‘keep oneself clean’. Is that not so? Do you understand, do you understand what that cleanliness means? Do you understand that Luzhin’s cleanliness is just the same as Sonechka’s cleanliness, and perhaps even worse, fouler, viler, because with you, Dunechka, after all, there is a calculation on some extra comfort, while there it is simply a matter of starving to death! ‘It costs dear, dear, Dunechka, that cleanliness!’ Well, and if it becomes too much for you afterwards, will you repent? How much sorrow, grief, cursing, how many tears hidden from everyone, for you are no Marfa Petrovna, are you? And what will happen to Mother then? For she is uneasy even now, she is tormented; and then, when she sees everything clearly? And to me?.. Yes, what have you really been thinking of me? I do not want your sacrifice, Dunechka, I do not want it, Mama! It will not be, while I am alive, it will not be, it will not be! I do not accept it!”

He suddenly came to himself and stopped.

“It will not be? And what will you do to stop it? Forbid it? And what right have you? What can you promise them in return, to have such a right? To devote your whole fate, your whole future to them, when you have finished your course and got a post? We have heard that, but that is pie in the sky; and now? For something has to be done now, do you understand that? And what are you doing now? Robbing them. For the money comes to them as a loan on a hundred-rouble pension and as an advance from the Svidrigailov gentry! How will you protect them from the Svidrigailovs, from Afanasy Ivanovich Vakhrushin, you future millionaire, you Zeus who disposes of their fate? In ten years’ time? In ten years Mother will have had time to go blind from shawls, and perhaps from tears too; she will waste away from fasting; and your sister? Well, think, what may become of your sister in ten years, or during those ten years? Have you guessed?”

So he tormented and teased himself with these questions, even with a kind of pleasure. But all these questions were not new, not sudden, but old, aching, long-standing. They had begun to torture him long ago and had worn out his heart. Long, long ago all this present anguish had been born in him; it had grown, accumulated, and lately it had ripened and concentrated, taking the form of a terrible, wild and fantastic question, which had tormented his heart and mind, irresistibly demanding to be resolved. Now his mother’s letter had suddenly struck him like a thunderbolt. It was clear that now he must not pine, not suffer passively, with mere reasoning that the questions could not be resolved, but must certainly do something, and at once, and quickly. At all costs he must make up his mind, to something at least, or...

“Or give up life altogether!” he suddenly cried in a frenzy. “Accept fate obediently as it is, once and for all, and stifle everything in myself, renouncing every right to act, to live and to love!”

“Do you understand, do you understand, my dear sir, what it means when there is nowhere else to go?” Marmeladov’s question of yesterday suddenly came back to him. “For every man must have somewhere he can go...”

Suddenly he started: a thought, also from yesterday, had flashed through his head again. But he did not start because this thought had flashed through it. For he had known, he had sensed, that it would certainly “flash through”, and he had already been waiting for it; and this thought was not from yesterday at all. But the difference was that a month ago, and even yesterday, it had only been a dream, while now... now it appeared suddenly not as a dream, but in some new, menacing and quite unfamiliar form, and he suddenly realised this himself... The blood beat in his head, and his eyes went dark.

He looked round hastily; he was looking for something. He wanted to sit down, and he was looking for a bench; he was walking along K. Boulevard at that moment. There was a bench ahead, about a hundred paces away. He walked as fast as he could; but on the way a little adventure happened to him, which for a few minutes drew all his attention.

Looking out for the bench, he noticed a woman walking about twenty paces ahead of him, but at first he paid no attention to her, any more than to all the other objects that had flickered before him until then. It had already happened to him many times, for instance, to walk home and not remember at all the way he had come, and he was used to walking like that by now. But there was something so strange about the woman walking ahead, something that caught the eye from the very first glance, that little by little his attention began to fasten on her, at first reluctantly and as if with annoyance, and then more and more firmly. He suddenly wanted to understand what exactly was so strange about this woman. In the first place, she must be a very young girl, and she was walking in such heat bareheaded, without a parasol or gloves, swinging her arms in a funny way. She had on a little dress of light silk material, but it too was put on very oddly, hardly fastened, and torn at the back at the waist, at the very top of the skirt; a whole piece hung loose and dangled. A little scarf was thrown over her bare neck, but it stuck out somehow crookedly and to one side. To crown it all, the girl was walking unsteadily, stumbling and even swaying from side to side. This meeting finally roused all Raskolnikov’s attention. He came up with the girl right by the bench; but when she reached the bench she simply collapsed on it, in the corner, threw her head back on the back of the bench and closed her eyes, apparently from extreme exhaustion. Looking closely at her, he guessed at once that she was completely drunk. It was strange and wild to look at such a sight. He even wondered whether he was mistaken. Before him was an extremely young little face, sixteen or so, perhaps even only fifteen: small, fair-haired, pretty, but all flushed and as if swollen. The girl seemed to understand very little by now; she had crossed one leg over the other, showing much more of it than was proper, and by every sign she was very little aware that she was in the street.

Raskolnikov did not sit down, and did not want to go away, but stood before her in bewilderment. This boulevard is always deserted, and now, at past one o’clock and in such heat, there was almost no one. And yet a little way off, about fifteen paces, at the edge of the boulevard, a gentleman had stopped who, by every sign, would very much have liked to go up to the girl too, with some purposes of his own. He too had probably seen her from a distance and had been catching up with her, but Raskolnikov had got in his way. He kept throwing angry looks at him, trying, though, not to let him notice them, and waited impatiently for his turn, for the annoying ragamuffin to go away. The thing was clear. The gentleman was about thirty, stout, fat, ruddy as blood and milk, with pink lips and a little moustache, and very foppishly dressed. Raskolnikov was terribly angry; he suddenly wanted somehow to insult this fat dandy. He left the girl for a minute and went up to the gentleman.

“Hey, you, Svidrigailov! What do you want here?” he shouted, clenching his fists and laughing with lips that were foaming with rage.

“What does this mean?” the gentleman asked sternly, frowning and haughtily surprised.

“Clear off, that’s what!”

“How dare you, you scoundrel!..”

And he raised his riding whip. Raskolnikov rushed at him with his fists, not even reckoning that the stout gentleman could deal with two of his kind. But at that moment someone seized him firmly from behind; a policeman had come between them.

“That will do, gentlemen; no fighting in public places, if you please. What do you want? Who are you?” he turned sternly to Raskolnikov, taking in his rags.

Raskolnikov looked at him attentively. It was a fine soldierly face with grey moustaches and side-whiskers and a sensible look.

“You are just the man I need,” he cried, seizing him by the arm. “I am a former student, Raskolnikov... You can find that out too,” he turned to the gentleman, “but you, come along, I will show you something...”

And, seizing the policeman by the arm, he dragged him to the bench.

“Here, look, she is completely drunk; she was walking along the boulevard just now; who knows what sort she is, but she does not look as if she does it for a living. Most likely someone gave her drink somewhere and took advantage of her... for the first time... do you understand? and then let her out into the street like that. Look how her dress is torn, look how it is put on: someone dressed her, she did not dress herself, and they were clumsy hands that dressed her, a man’s hands. That is plain. And now look over here: this dandy I was about to fight with just now is a stranger to me, I see him for the first time; but he too marked her down on the way just now, drunk, not knowing what she is doing, and now he terribly wants to come up and get hold of her, since she is in such a state, and take her off somewhere... And that is surely so; believe me, I am not mistaken. I saw him myself watching her and following her, only I got in his way, and now he is waiting for me to go. There, he has moved off a little now, he is standing as if rolling a cigarette... How can we stop him? How can we send her home? Think!”

The policeman understood and grasped everything in a moment. The fat gentleman, of course, was clear; that left the girl. The policeman bent over her to look more closely, and sincere compassion showed in his features.

“Ah, what a pity!” he said, shaking his head. “Quite a child still. Somebody took advantage, that’s just it. Listen, miss,” he began calling to her, “where do you live?” The girl opened her tired, bleary eyes, looked dully at the men questioning her, and waved them away.

“Listen,” said Raskolnikov, “here” (he fumbled in his pocket and pulled out twenty kopecks; he found them), “here, take a cab and tell him to drive her to her address. If only we could find out the address!”

“Miss, eh, miss?” the policeman began again, taking the money. “I’ll get you a cab right now and take you myself. Where shall I take you? Eh? Where do you live?”

“Go 'way!.. Pestering me!..” the girl muttered, and waved them away again.

“Ah, ah, how bad! Ah, how shameful, miss, what a shame!” He shook his head again, shaming her, pitying her and indignant. “That’s a problem!” he turned to Raskolnikov, and at the same time, in passing, looked him up and down again. He must have seemed strange to him too: in such rags, and yet handing out money!

“Did you find her far from here?” he asked him.

“I tell you: she was walking ahead of me, staggering, right here on the boulevard. As soon as she got to the bench, she collapsed.”

“Ah, what shamefulness there is in the world nowadays, Lord! Such a simple little thing, and already drunk! Somebody took advantage, that’s how it is! And her little dress is torn too... Ah, what depravity there is nowadays!.. And she may well be of good family, from some poor ones... There are many like that nowadays. To look at her, one of the delicate ones, just like a young lady,” and he bent over her again.

Perhaps he too had daughters growing up like that, “just like young ladies and delicate ones”, with the manners of the well brought up and with every fashionable affectation already picked up...

“The main thing,” Raskolnikov fussed, “is how to stop this scoundrel! He will only abuse her! One can see by heart what he wants; look at the scoundrel, he won’t go away!”

Raskolnikov spoke loudly and pointed straight at him. The man heard and was about to get angry again, but thought better of it and confined himself to a contemptuous look. Then he slowly moved off another ten paces or so and stopped again.

“We can stop them, sir,” the sergeant answered thoughtfully. “If only she would say where to take her; but as it is... Miss, eh, miss!” He bent down again.

She suddenly opened her eyes wide, looked attentively, as if she had understood something, got up from the bench and walked back in the direction she had come from.

“Pah, shameless things, pestering me!” she said, waving them away once more. She walked fast, but still staggering badly. The dandy followed her, but along another path, without taking his eyes off her.

“Don’t worry, I won’t let him, sir,” the moustached man said firmly, and set off after them.

“Eh, what depravity there is nowadays!” he repeated aloud, sighing.

At that moment something seemed to sting Raskolnikov; in an instant he seemed to be turned upside down.

“Listen, hey!” he shouted after the moustached man.

The man turned round.

“Let them be! What is it to you? Drop it! Let him have his fun” (he pointed at the dandy). “What is it to you?”

The policeman did not understand and stared at him wide-eyed. Raskolnikov laughed.

“Eh-eh!” said the policeman, waving his hand, and went off after the dandy and the girl, probably taking Raskolnikov either for a madman or for something even worse.

“He has carried off my twenty kopecks,” Raskolnikov said angrily, left alone. “Well, let him take something from the other one too and let the girl go with him, and that will be the end of it... And why did I get mixed up in helping here! Is it for me to help? Have I the right to help? Let them swallow one another alive: what is it to me? And how did I dare give away those twenty kopecks? Are they mine?”

In spite of these strange words, he felt very heavy at heart. He sat down on the bench, now empty. His thoughts were scattered... And in general it was hard for him at that moment to think about anything at all. He would have liked to forget himself completely, forget everything, then wake up and begin all over again...

“Poor girl!..” he said, looking at the empty corner of the bench. “She will come to, she will cry, then her mother will find out... First she will beat her, and then she will thrash her, painfully and shamefully, and perhaps drive her out... And if she does not drive her out, the Darya Frantsevnas will sniff her out all the same, and my girl will start slipping out here and there... Then straight away the hospital (that is always how it is with those who live with very honest mothers and get up to mischief on the quiet), and then... and then the hospital again... drink... taverns... and the hospital again... in two or three years, a wreck, so her whole life will have been nineteen years or eighteen in all... Haven’t I seen such girls? And how did they become like that? Why, just like this... Pah! But let it be! That, they say, is how it ought to be. A certain percentage, they say, must go every year... somewhere... to the devil, it must be, so as to freshen up the rest and not get in their way. A percentage! Splendid little words they have, really: so soothing, so scientific. Once you say ‘percentage’, there is nothing to worry about. Now if it were another word, well then... it might be more disturbing... And what if Dunechka somehow falls into the percentage!.. If not that one, then another?..

“But where am I going?” he suddenly thought. “Strange. I set out for some reason. As soon as I read the letter, I set out... To Vasilyevsky Island, to Razumikhin, that is where I was going, now... I remember. But why, though? And how did the idea of going to Razumikhin come into my head just now? That is remarkable.”

He wondered at himself. Razumikhin was one of his former fellow students at the university. It is remarkable that Raskolnikov, while he was at the university, had almost no friends; he kept away from everyone, visited no one, and did not like receiving anyone himself. And soon everyone turned away from him too. He somehow took no part in common gatherings, or in conversations, or in amusements, or in anything. He studied hard, without sparing himself, and for that he was respected, but no one liked him. He was very poor, and somehow haughtily proud and uncommunicative, as if he were keeping something to himself. To some of his fellow students it seemed that he looked down on them all as on children, as if he were ahead of them all in development, and knowledge, and convictions, and as if he regarded their convictions and interests as something inferior.

But with Razumikhin he had for some reason got on, that is, not exactly got on, but he was more communicative, more open with him. Indeed, with Razumikhin it was impossible to be on any other terms. He was an unusually cheerful and sociable fellow, kind to the point of simplicity. But under this simplicity there were also depth and dignity. The best of his fellow students understood this, and everyone loved him. He was far from stupid, though sometimes he really was a bit simple. His appearance was striking: tall, thin, always badly shaved, black-haired. Sometimes he made a row and had the reputation of a strong man. Once at night, in company, he knocked down with one blow a guardian of the peace who was a good six foot four. He could drink without end, but he could also not drink at all; sometimes he played pranks that went too far, but he could also do without pranks entirely. Razumikhin was remarkable too in that no failures ever discouraged him, and no bad circumstances, it seemed, could crush him. He could lodge even on a roof, and put up with hellish hunger and extraordinary cold. He was very poor, and kept himself entirely on his own, earning money by odd jobs of one kind or another. He knew a bottomless number of sources from which he could draw, by work, of course. Once he went a whole winter without heating his room at all, and maintained that it was even more pleasant, because one sleeps better in the cold. At present he too had been obliged to leave the university, but not for long, and he was hurrying with all his strength to put his affairs right so that he could continue. Raskolnikov had not been to see him for about four months, and Razumikhin did not even know where he lived. Once, about two months ago, they had met in the street, but Raskolnikov had turned away and even crossed to the other side, so that he would not be noticed. And Razumikhin, though he did notice, walked past, not wishing to trouble his friend.
