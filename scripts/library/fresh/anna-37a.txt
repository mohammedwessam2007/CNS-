## Part Eight · Chapter XII

Levin walked with long strides along the high road, listening not so much to his thoughts (he could not yet make them out) as to a state of soul he had never known before.

The words the peasant had spoken acted on his soul like an electric spark, suddenly transforming and welding into one a whole swarm of scattered, powerless, separate thoughts that had never ceased to occupy him. These thoughts had occupied him, without his noticing it himself, even while he was talking about letting the land.

He felt something new in his soul, and with delight felt his way about this new thing, not yet knowing what it was.

“To live not for one’s needs, but for God. For what God? For God. And what could be more senseless than what he said? He said that one must not live for one’s needs, that is, one must not live for what we understand, what attracts us, what we want, but must live for something incomprehensible, for God, whom no one can understand or define. And what then? Did I not understand these senseless words of Fyodor’s? And, having understood them, did I doubt their truth? Did I find them stupid, unclear, inexact?

“No, I understood him, and exactly as he understands it; I understood it completely and more clearly than I understand anything in life, and never in my life have I doubted it, nor can I doubt it. And not I alone, but everyone, the whole world, understands this one thing completely, and about this one thing has no doubt and is always agreed.

“Fyodor says that Kirillov, the innkeeper, lives for his belly. That is understandable and reasonable. All of us, as reasonable beings, cannot live otherwise than for our bellies. And suddenly that same Fyodor says that it is bad to live for one’s belly, that one must live for the truth, for God, and I understand him from a hint! I, and millions of people who lived centuries ago and are living now, peasants, the poor in spirit, and sages who have thought and written about this, saying the same thing in their unclear language: we are all agreed on this one thing: what one must live for and what is good. I, with all people, have only one firm, undoubted and clear knowledge, and this knowledge cannot be explained by reason: it is outside it, and has no causes, and can have no consequences.

“If goodness has a cause, it is no longer goodness; if it has a consequence, a reward, it is not goodness either. So goodness is outside the chain of cause and effect.

“And it is this that I know, and all of us know.

“And I was looking for miracles, I was sorry I had not seen a miracle that would convince me. And here it is, the miracle, the only one possible, existing always, surrounding me on all sides, and I did not notice it!

“What miracle could be greater than this?

“Can it be that I have found the solution of everything, can it be that my sufferings are over now?” thought Levin, striding along the dusty road, noticing neither the heat nor his tiredness, and feeling the easing of a long suffering. This feeling was so joyful that it seemed incredible to him. He was breathless with emotion and, unable to walk further, he left the road, went into the wood, and sat down in the shade of the aspens on the unmown grass. He took his hat off his sweating head and lay down, leaning on his elbow, on the juicy, broad-leaved woodland grass.

“Yes, I must come to myself and think it over,” he thought, gazing intently at the untrampled grass before him and following the movements of a green beetle climbing up a stalk of couch-grass and held up in its climb by a leaf of goutweed. “Everything from the beginning,” he said to himself, turning aside the goutweed leaf so that it should not hinder the beetle, and bending down another blade of grass so that the beetle could cross over to it. “What is it that gladdens me? What have I discovered?

“Before, I used to say that in my body, in the body of this grass and of this beetle (there, it didn’t want to go onto that grass, it spread its wings and flew away), an exchange of matter takes place according to physical, chemical and physiological laws. And in all of us, together with the aspens and the clouds and the nebulae, development takes place. Development from what? Into what? Endless development and struggle?… As if there could be any direction and struggle in the infinite! And I was surprised that, in spite of the greatest effort of thought along this path, the meaning of life, the meaning of my impulses and strivings, was still not revealed to me. And yet the meaning of my impulses is so clear in me that I live by it constantly, and I was surprised and glad when the peasant put it into words for me: to live for God, for the soul.

“I have discovered nothing. I have only recognized what I know. I have understood the force which not only gave me life in the past, but gives me life now. I have been set free from deceit; I have recognized the master.”

And he briefly went over to himself the whole course of his thought during these last two years, the beginning of which had been the clear, obvious thought of death at the sight of his beloved brother, hopelessly ill.

Understanding clearly then for the first time that for every man, and for himself, there was nothing ahead but suffering, death and eternal oblivion, he had decided that it was impossible to live like that, that he must either explain his life so that it did not seem the wicked mockery of some devil, or shoot himself.

But he had done neither the one nor the other; he had gone on living, thinking and feeling, and even at that very time had married and known many joys, and had been happy when he was not thinking about the meaning of his life.

What did this mean? It meant that he had been living well but thinking badly.

He had lived (without being aware of it) by those spiritual truths which he had sucked in with his mother’s milk, while he thought not only without acknowledging these truths, but carefully going round them.

Now it was clear to him that he had been able to live only thanks to the beliefs in which he had been brought up.

“What would I be, and how would I have lived my life, if I had not had these beliefs, had not known that one must live for God and not for one’s needs? I would have robbed, lied, killed. Nothing of what makes up the chief joys of my life would have existed for me.” And, making the greatest efforts of imagination, he still could not imagine the brutal being he himself would have been if he had not known what he was living for.

“I was looking for an answer to my question. But thought could not give me an answer to my question: it is incommensurable with the question. The answer was given me by life itself, in my knowledge of what is good and what is bad. And this knowledge I did not acquire by anything; it was given me together with everyone else, given because I could not have taken it from anywhere.

“Where did I get this? Was it by reason that I arrived at knowing that one must love one’s neighbour and not throttle him? I was told it in childhood, and I believed it joyfully, because I was told what was already in my soul. And who discovered it? Not reason. Reason discovered the struggle for existence and the law which demands that I throttle everyone who hinders the satisfaction of my desires. That is the conclusion of reason. But reason could not discover loving another, because it is unreasonable.”

“Yes, pride,” he said to himself, rolling over onto his stomach and beginning to tie stalks of grass into a knot, trying not to break them.

“And not only the pride of the mind, but the stupidity of the mind. And above all the trickery, precisely the trickery of the mind. Precisely the swindling of the mind,” he repeated.

## Chapter XIII

And Levin remembered a recent scene with Dolly and her children. The children, left alone, had started cooking raspberries over the candles and pouring milk into each other’s mouths like a fountain. Their mother, catching them at it, had begun in Levin’s presence to impress on them how much work what they were destroying cost the grown-ups, and that this work was done for them; that if they broke the cups they would have nothing to drink their tea out of, and if they spilled the milk they would have nothing to eat and would die of hunger.

And Levin had been struck by the calm, dejected disbelief with which the children listened to their mother’s words. They were only upset that their amusing game had been stopped, and did not believe a word of what their mother said. And they could not believe it, because they could not imagine the whole extent of what they made use of, and so could not imagine that what they were destroying was the very thing they lived by.

“All this happens by itself,” they thought, “and there is nothing interesting or important in it, because it has always been so and always will be. And it is always one and the same. We needn’t think about it; it is ready; but we want to invent something of our own, something new. So we have thought of putting raspberries in a cup and cooking them over a candle, and pouring milk straight into each other’s mouths like a fountain. That is fun and new, and no worse than drinking out of cups.”

“Isn’t that just what we do, what I did, searching by reason for the significance of the forces of nature and the meaning of human life?” he went on thinking.

“And isn’t that just what all philosophical theories do, leading man by the path of thought, a strange path not natural to him, to the knowledge of what he has long known, and knows so surely that without it he could not even live? Isn’t it clearly seen in the development of every philosopher’s theory that he knows beforehand, just as undoubtedly as the peasant Fyodor and not a bit more clearly than he, the chief meaning of life, and only wants to return by a doubtful mental path to what everyone knows?

“Now then, leave the children alone to get things for themselves, make the crockery, milk the cows and so on. Would they play pranks? They would die of hunger. Now then, leave us with our passions and thoughts, without any idea of the one God and Creator! Or without any idea of what is good, without an explanation of moral evil.

“Now then, try building anything without these ideas!

“We only destroy because we are spiritually well fed. Exactly like children.

“Where did I get this joyful knowledge, which I share with the peasant, and which alone gives me peace of soul? Where did I get it?

“I, brought up with the idea of God, a Christian, having filled my whole life with the spiritual blessings Christianity gave me, full of them all through and living by them, I, like the children, not understanding them, destroy, that is, want to destroy, what I live by. And as soon as an important moment of life comes, like children when they are cold and hungry, I go to Him; and even less than the children, whose mother scolds them for their childish pranks, do I feel that my childish attempts to run wild from too much good living are counted against me.

“Yes, what I know, I know not by reason; it has been given to me, revealed to me, and I know it with my heart, by faith in the chief thing that the church professes.”

“The church? The church!” Levin repeated to himself, turned over onto his other side and, leaning on his elbow, began gazing into the distance at the herd that was coming down to the river on the other side.

“But can I believe in everything the church professes?” he thought, testing himself and thinking up everything that might destroy his present peace. He deliberately began recalling those teachings of the church which had always seemed strangest to him and had tempted him most. “The Creation? And how did I explain existence? By existence? By nothing? The devil and sin? And how do I explain evil?… The Redeemer?…

“But I know nothing, nothing, and can know nothing, except what has been told me together with everyone else.”

And it seemed to him now that there was not one of the beliefs of the church that violated the main thing: faith in God, in goodness, as man’s only purpose.

For each belief of the church could be substituted the belief in serving the truth instead of one’s needs. And each of them not only did not violate this, but was necessary in order that there should be accomplished that chief miracle, constantly manifesting itself on earth, which consists in its being possible for each person, together with millions of the most various people, sages and holy fools, children and old men, together with everyone, with the peasant, with Lvov, with Kitty, with beggars and kings, to understand undoubtedly one and the same thing, and to build up that life of the soul for which alone it is worth living and which alone we value.

Lying on his back, he was now looking up into the high, cloudless sky. “Don’t I know that that is infinite space, and that it is not a round vault? But however I screw up my eyes and strain my sight, I cannot see it as not round and not bounded; and, in spite of my knowledge of infinite space, I am undoubtedly right when I see a firm blue vault, more right than when I strain to see beyond it.”

Levin had stopped thinking now and was only, as it were, listening to mysterious voices talking among themselves about something, joyfully and earnestly.

“Can this be faith?” he thought, afraid to believe in his happiness. “My God, I thank Thee!” he said, swallowing the sobs that were rising and wiping away with both hands the tears that filled his eyes.

## Chapter XIV

Levin looked in front of him and saw the herd; then he caught sight of his trap, drawn by Voronoi, and of the coachman, who drove up to the herd and said something to the herdsman; then, quite close to him, he heard the sound of wheels and the snorting of a well-fed horse; but he was so absorbed in his thoughts that he did not even think why the coachman was coming to him.

He remembered this only when the coachman, having driven right up to him, called out to him.

“The mistress sent me. Your brother has come, and some other gentleman.”

Levin got into the trap and took the reins.

As if waking from sleep, Levin could not come to himself for a long time. He looked at the well-fed horse, lathered between its haunches and on its neck where the reins rubbed; he looked at Ivan the coachman sitting beside him, and remembered that he had been expecting his brother, that his wife was probably worried by his long absence; and he tried to guess who the guest was who had come with his brother. And his brother, and his wife, and the unknown guest now appeared to him differently from before. It seemed to him that now his relations with all people would be different.

“With my brother there will no longer be that estrangement that has always been between us: there will be no arguments; with Kitty there will never be quarrels; with the guest, whoever he may be, I shall be kind and friendly; with the servants, with Ivan, everything will be different.”

Holding back on taut reins the good horse, which was snorting with impatience and begging to go, Levin kept glancing at Ivan sitting beside him, who did not know what to do with his hands, left without work, and kept pressing down his shirt; and he was looking for a pretext to start a conversation with him. He wanted to say that Ivan had pulled the saddle-girth up too high, but that would have sounded like a reproach, and he wanted a loving conversation. Nothing else came into his head.

“Please keep to the right, there’s a stump,” said the coachman, putting Levin right by pulling on the rein.

“Please don’t touch them and don’t teach me!” said Levin, irritated by this interference of the coachman’s. Just as always, such interference made him angry, and at once he felt with sadness how mistaken his supposition had been that his state of soul could change him at once on contact with reality.

A quarter of a verst before reaching the house, Levin saw Grisha and Tanya running to meet him.

“Uncle Kostya! Mama is coming, and grandpapa, and Sergei Ivanych, and someone else,” they said, climbing into the trap.

“But who?”

“A terribly frightening man! And he does like this with his hands,” said Tanya, standing up in the trap and mimicking Katavasov.

“But is he old or young?” said Levin, laughing; Tanya’s performance reminded him of someone.

“Oh, if only it isn’t some disagreeable person!” thought Levin.

Only when he had come round the bend in the road and seen the people coming to meet him did Levin recognize Katavasov in a straw hat, walking and waving his arms just as Tanya had shown.

Katavasov was very fond of talking about philosophy, having his notion of it from natural scientists who had never studied philosophy; and in Moscow Levin had argued with him a great deal lately.

And one of those conversations, in which Katavasov evidently thought he had come off best, was the first thing Levin remembered on recognizing him.

“No, I won’t argue and express my thoughts lightly for anything,” he thought.

Getting out of the trap and greeting his brother and Katavasov, Levin asked about his wife.

“She has taken Mitya to the Kolok” (this was a wood near the house). “She wanted to settle him there, it’s so hot in the house,” said Dolly.

Levin had always advised his wife against taking the baby into the wood, considering it dangerous, and the news was unpleasant to him.

“She carries him about from place to place,” said the prince, smiling. “I advised her to try taking him into the ice-house.”

“She meant to come to the apiary. She thought you were there. We are going there,” said Dolly.

“Well, what are you doing?” said Sergei Ivanovich, falling behind the others and drawing level with his brother.

“Oh, nothing special. As always, I’m busy with the estate,” answered Levin. “And you, will you be staying long? We have been expecting you for a long time.”

“A couple of weeks or so. There is a great deal to do in Moscow.”

At these words the brothers’ eyes met, and Levin, in spite of his constant and now particularly strong wish to be on friendly and, above all, simple terms with his brother, felt that it was awkward for him to look at him. He lowered his eyes and did not know what to say.

Going over subjects of conversation that would be pleasant to Sergei Ivanovich and would draw him away from talk about the Serbian war and the Slav question, at which he had hinted by mentioning his business in Moscow, Levin began talking about Sergei Ivanovich’s book.

“Well, have there been reviews of your book?” he asked.

Sergei Ivanovich smiled at the deliberateness of the question.

“No one is interested in it, and I less than anyone,” he said. “Look, Darya Alexandrovna, there will be rain,” he added, pointing with his umbrella at some white clouds that had appeared above the tops of the aspens.

And these words were enough for that relation between the brothers, not hostile but cold, which Levin had so wanted to avoid, to be established again.

Levin went up to Katavasov.

“How good of you to think of coming,” he said to him.

“I’ve been meaning to for a long time. Now we shall have some talks and see things. Have you read Spencer?”

“No, I haven’t finished him,” said Levin. “But I don’t need him now.”

“How so? That’s interesting. Why not?”

“I mean, I have become finally convinced that I shall not find the solution of the questions that occupy me in him and his like. Now…”

But the calm and cheerful expression of Katavasov’s face suddenly struck him, and he felt so sorry for his own mood, which he was evidently disturbing by this conversation, that, remembering his intention, he stopped.

“But we’ll talk later,” he added. “If you are going to the apiary, it’s this way, along this path,” he said, turning to them all.

Reaching by the narrow path a little unmown clearing, covered on one side with a solid mass of bright cow-wheat, among which tall dark-green clumps of hellebore grew thickly here and there, Levin settled his guests in the dense fresh shade of the young aspens, on a bench and some stumps put there on purpose for visitors to the apiary who were afraid of bees, and went himself to the enclosure to bring bread, cucumbers and fresh honey for the children and the grown-ups.

Trying to make as few quick movements as possible, and listening to the bees flying past him more and more often, he went along the path to the hut. Right by the entrance one bee began to whine, tangled in his beard, but he carefully freed it. Going into the shady entry, he took down his net from the peg on the wall, and, putting it on and thrusting his hands into his pockets, he went out into the fenced apiary, where, in regular rows, tied to stakes with strips of bast, there stood in the middle of a mown space all the old hives familiar to him, each with its own history, and along the walls of the wattle fence the young ones, put in this year. Before the entrances of the hives the eye was dazzled by bees and drones circling and jostling in one place at play, and among them, all in one direction, out into the wood to the flowering lime trees and back to the hives, the worker bees were flying with their loads and for more.

His ears were constantly filled with various sounds: now of a busy worker bee flying quickly past, now of a trumpeting, idling drone, now of the alarmed sentry bees, guarding their property from an enemy and getting ready to sting. On the other side of the fence the old man was shaving a hoop and did not see Levin. Levin did not call to him, and stood in the middle of the apiary.

He was glad of the chance to be alone, to come to himself after the reality that had already managed to bring his mood so low.

He remembered that he had already managed to get angry with Ivan, to show coldness to his brother, and to talk lightly with Katavasov.

“Can it really have been only a passing mood, and will it pass without leaving a trace?” he thought.

But at the same moment, returning to his mood, he felt with joy that something new and important had happened in him. Reality had only for a time veiled the peace of soul he had found; but it was whole within him.

Just as the bees, now circling round him, threatening and distracting him, depriving him of complete physical peace, made him shrink to avoid them, so the cares that had crowded round him from the moment he got into the trap had deprived him of his freedom of soul; but this lasted only while he was among them. Just as, in spite of the bees, his bodily strength was all whole in him, so was the spiritual strength he had newly become aware of.

## Chapter XV

“And do you know, Kostya, who Sergei Ivanovich travelled here with?” said Dolly, having shared out the cucumbers and honey among the children. “With Vronsky! He is going to Serbia.”

“And not alone, either: he is taking a squadron at his own expense!” said Katavasov.

“That suits him,” said Levin. “And are volunteers still going?” he added, glancing at Sergei Ivanovich.

Sergei Ivanovich, without answering, was carefully taking out with a blunt knife, from a cup in which a white piece of honeycomb lay corner-wise, a still living bee that had got stuck in the oozing honey.

“I should think so! You should have seen what went on at the station yesterday!” said Katavasov, biting a cucumber with a loud crunch.

“Well, how is one to understand that? For Christ’s sake, explain to me, Sergei Ivanovich, where are all these volunteers going, whom are they fighting?” asked the old prince, evidently continuing a conversation that had begun before Levin came.

“The Turks,” answered Sergei Ivanovich, smiling calmly, as he freed the bee, darkened with honey and helplessly moving its legs, and set it down from the knife onto a firm aspen leaf.

“But who has declared war on the Turks? Ivan Ivanych Ragozov and Countess Lidia Ivanovna with Madame Stahl?”

“No one has declared war, but people sympathize with the sufferings of their neighbours and wish to help them,” said Sergei Ivanovich.

“But the prince is not talking about help,” said Levin, standing up for his father-in-law, “but about war. The prince says that private persons cannot take part in a war without the permission of governments.”

“Kostya, look, that’s a bee! Really, we shall be stung!” said Dolly, waving away a wasp.

“But that isn’t a bee, it’s a wasp,” said Levin.

“Well, well, what’s your theory?” said Katavasov to Levin with a smile, evidently challenging him to an argument. “Why do private persons have no right?”

“Why, my theory is this: war, on the one hand, is such a bestial, cruel, terrible thing that no man, not to speak of a Christian, can personally take on himself the responsibility of beginning a war; only a government can, which is called to it and is inevitably drawn into war. On the other hand, both by science and by common sense, in affairs of state, and especially in the business of war, citizens renounce their personal will.”

Sergei Ivanovich and Katavasov, with objections ready, began speaking at the same time.

“That’s just the point, my dear fellow: there may be a case when the government does not carry out the will of the citizens, and then society declares its will,” said Katavasov.

But Sergei Ivanovich evidently did not approve of this objection. He frowned at Katavasov’s words and said something else:

“You are wrong to put the question that way. There is no declaration of war here, but simply the expression of a human, Christian feeling. Our brothers are being killed, our kin by blood and by faith. Well, let us even suppose they were not our brothers, not of our faith, but simply children, women, old men; feeling revolts, and Russian people run to help put a stop to these horrors. Imagine that you were walking along the street and saw drunken men beating a woman or a child; I think you would not stop to ask whether war had or had not been declared on that man, but would throw yourself on him and defend the victim.”

“But I would not kill him,” said Levin.

“Yes, you would kill him.”

“I don’t know. If I saw that, I would give myself up to my immediate feeling; but I cannot say beforehand. And there is not, and cannot be, any such immediate feeling about the oppression of the Slavs.”

“Perhaps for you there is not. But for others there is,” said Sergei Ivanovich, frowning with displeasure. “Among the people the traditions are alive of Orthodox people suffering under the yoke of ‘the godless sons of Hagar’. The people have heard of the sufferings of their brothers and have spoken out.”

“Perhaps,” said Levin evasively, “but I don’t see it; I am one of the people myself, and I don’t feel it.”

“Nor do I,” said the prince. “I was living abroad, reading the newspapers, and, I confess, even before the Bulgarian horrors I could not understand why all Russians had so suddenly come to love their brother Slavs, while I felt no love for them at all. I was very upset; I thought I was a monster, or that Carlsbad was having that effect on me. But since I came here I have been reassured; I see that there are people besides me who are interested only in Russia and not in their brother Slavs. Here is Konstantin, for one.”

“Personal opinions mean nothing here,” said Sergei Ivanovich. “Personal opinions do not matter when all Russia, the people, has expressed its will.”

“But excuse me. I don’t see it. The people don’t know anything about it,” said the prince.

“No, papa… how can you say they don’t? And on Sunday in church?” said Dolly, who had been listening to the conversation. “Give me a towel, please,” she said to the old man, who was looking at the children with a smile. “It can’t be that everyone…”

“Well, what about Sunday in church? The priest was told to read it out. He read it. They understood nothing and sighed, as at every sermon,” the prince went on. “Then they were told that a collection was being made in church for a soul-saving cause; well, they each took out a kopeck and gave it. But what for, they don’t know themselves.”

“The people cannot help knowing; the consciousness of its destiny is always in a people, and at moments such as the present it becomes clear to it,” said Sergei Ivanovich, glancing at the old beekeeper.

The handsome old man with a black beard streaked with grey and thick silvery hair was standing motionless, holding a cup of honey, looking down kindly and calmly from his height at the gentlefolk, evidently understanding nothing and not wishing to understand.

“That’s just so,” he said, shaking his head significantly at Sergei Ivanovich’s words.

“There, ask him. He knows nothing and thinks nothing,” said Levin. “Have you heard about the war, Mikhailych?” he said, turning to him. “What they read out in church? What do you think? Ought we to fight for the Christians?”

“What should we think? Alexander Nikolaevich, the Emperor, has thought for us, and he’ll think for us in all things. He sees better… Shouldn’t I bring a little more bread? Shall I give the lad some more?” he said, turning to Darya Alexandrovna and pointing to Grisha, who was finishing a crust.

“I don’t need to ask,” said Sergei Ivanovich. “We have seen and are seeing hundreds and hundreds of people who are giving up everything to serve a just cause, who come from all ends of Russia and express their thought and aim directly and clearly. They bring their pennies, or go themselves, and say straight out why. What does that mean?”

“It means, in my opinion,” said Levin, who was beginning to get heated, “that in a nation of eighty million there will always be found, not hundreds, as now, but tens of thousands of people who have lost their place in society, reckless people, who are always ready to join Pugachev’s band, or go to Khiva, or Serbia…”

“I tell you that they are not hundreds, and not reckless people, but the best representatives of the people!” said Sergei Ivanych with as much irritation as if he were defending his last possession. “And the donations? Here the whole people is directly expressing its will.”

“That word ‘people’ is so vague,” said Levin. “District clerks, teachers, and, of the peasants, perhaps one in a thousand know what it is about. The other eighty million, like Mikhailych, not only do not express their will, but have not the slightest idea what they ought to express their will about. What right have we to say that this is the will of the people?”

## Chapter XVI

Sergei Ivanovich, experienced in dialectics, did not object, but at once carried the conversation into another field.

“Yes, if you want to find out the spirit of a people by arithmetic, then of course it is very hard to achieve. And voting has not been introduced among us, and cannot be, because it does not express the will of the people; but there are other ways for that. It is felt in the air, it is felt with the heart. Not to speak of those undercurrents which have started moving in the stagnant sea of the people, and which are clear to every unprejudiced person, look at society in the narrow sense. All the most various parties of the educated world, so hostile before, have all merged into one. All discord has ended; all the organs of society say one and the same thing; all have sensed an elemental force that has seized them and is carrying them in one direction.”

“Yes, the newspapers all say the same thing,” said the prince. “That’s true. So much the same that it’s just like frogs before a storm. Because of them you can’t hear anything.”

“Frogs or not frogs, I don’t publish newspapers and don’t want to defend them; but I am speaking of the unanimity of the educated world,” said Sergei Ivanovich, turning to his brother.

Levin wanted to answer, but the old prince interrupted him.

“Well, about that unanimity there is something else to be said,” said the prince. “Take my son-in-law, Stepan Arkadyich, you know him. He is now getting a post as member of a committee of a commission and something else, I don’t remember. Only there is nothing to do there (well, Dolly, it’s no secret!), and eight thousand in salary. Try asking him whether his post is useful: he’ll prove to you that it’s the most necessary of all. And he is a truthful man; but one can’t help believing in the usefulness of eight thousand.”

“Yes, he asked me to tell Darya Alexandrovna about his getting the post,” said Sergei Ivanovich with displeasure, supposing that the prince was talking beside the point.

“And it’s the same with the unanimity of the newspapers. It was explained to me: as soon as there is a war, their income doubles. How can they help considering that the destinies of the people and of the Slavs… and all that?”

“I don’t like many newspapers, but that is unjust,” said Sergei Ivanovich.

“I would make only one condition,” the prince went on. “Alphonse Karr wrote it splendidly before the war with Prussia. ‘You consider war necessary? Very well. Whoever preaches war: into a special legion of the vanguard, and to the storming, to the attack, ahead of everyone!’”

“A fine lot the editors would be,” said Katavasov, laughing loudly as he pictured the editors he knew in that select legion.

“Oh, well, they would run away,” said Dolly, “they would only be in the way.”

“And if they ran, put grapeshot behind them, or Cossacks with whips,” said the prince.

“But that is a joke, and not a good joke, if you’ll excuse me, Prince,” said Sergei Ivanovich.

“I don’t see that it is a joke, it…” Levin began, but Sergei Ivanovich interrupted him.

“Every member of society is called to do his own work, the work proper to him,” he said. “And men of thought do their work by expressing public opinion. And the unanimous and full expression of public opinion is the merit of the press and at the same time a joyful phenomenon. Twenty years ago we would have kept silent, and now the voice of the Russian people is heard, which is ready to rise up as one man and ready to sacrifice itself for its oppressed brothers; that is a great step and a pledge of strength.”

“But not only to sacrifice, you know, but to kill Turks,” said Levin timidly. “The people sacrifice, and are always ready to sacrifice, for their soul, but not for murder,” he added, involuntarily connecting the conversation with the thoughts that so occupied him.

“What do you mean, for their soul? That, you understand, is an awkward expression for a natural scientist. What is this soul, now?” said Katavasov, smiling.

“Oh, you know!”

“There, upon my word, I haven’t the slightest idea!” said Katavasov with a loud laugh.

“‘I came not to send peace, but a sword,’ says Christ,” Sergei Ivanovich objected for his part, simply, as if it were the most understandable thing, quoting the very passage of the Gospel that had always troubled Levin most.

“That’s just so,” the old man standing near them repeated again, in answer to a glance that happened to fall on him.

“No, my dear fellow, beaten, beaten, utterly beaten!” Katavasov cried gaily.

Levin flushed with vexation, not at having been beaten but at not having held back and having begun to argue.

“No, I can’t argue with them,” he thought. “They wear impenetrable armour, and I am naked.”

He saw that his brother and Katavasov could not be convinced, and still less did he see any possibility of agreeing with them himself. What they were preaching was that very pride of mind which had nearly destroyed him. He could not agree that some dozens of people, among them his brother, had the right, on the strength of what had been told them by hundreds of glib volunteers who had come to the capitals, to say that they and the newspapers were expressing the will and thought of the people, and such a thought as is expressed in vengeance and murder. He could not agree with this, because he neither saw the expression of these thoughts in the people among whom he lived, nor found these thoughts in himself (and he could not consider himself anything but one of the people who make up the Russian nation); and above all because he, together with the people, did not know and could not know what the common good consists in, but knew firmly that the attainment of that common good is possible only through strict fulfilment of that law of goodness which is revealed to every man, and so he could not wish for war or preach it for any common aims whatever. He said, with Mikhailych and with the people who expressed their thought in the legend of the calling of the Varangians: “Rule and reign over us. We joyfully promise complete obedience. All the labour, all the humiliations, all the sacrifices we take upon ourselves; but it is not we who judge and decide.” And now, according to the Sergei Ivanyches, the people were renouncing this right, bought at such a high price.

He wanted to say too that if public opinion is an infallible judge, then why are revolution and the commune not just as lawful as the movement in favour of the Slavs? But all these were thoughts that could decide nothing. One thing could be seen beyond doubt: that at this moment the argument was irritating Sergei Ivanovich, and so it was wrong to argue; and Levin fell silent and drew his guests’ attention to the clouds having gathered, and to its being better to go home before the rain.

## Chapter XVII

The prince and Sergei Ivanovich got into the trap and drove off; the rest of the party, quickening their pace, went home on foot.

But the storm-cloud, now whitening, now blackening, was coming on so fast that they had to walk still faster to get home before the rain. Its front clouds, black and low, like smoke full of soot, were racing across the sky with extraordinary speed. It was still about two hundred paces to the house, and already the wind had risen, and a downpour might be expected any second.

The children ran ahead with frightened and joyful squeals. Darya Alexandrovna, struggling with difficulty against her skirts, which clung to her legs, was no longer walking but running, never taking her eyes off the children. The men, holding on to their hats, walked with long strides. They were already at the very porch when a big drop struck and broke on the edge of the iron gutter. The children, and the grown-ups after them, ran with cheerful talk under the shelter of the roof.

“Katerina Alexandrovna?” Levin asked Agafya Mikhailovna, who met them in the hall with cloaks and rugs.

“We thought she was with you,” she said.

“And Mitya?”

“In the Kolok, he must be, and nurse with them.”

Levin seized the rugs and ran to the Kolok.

In that short space of time the middle of the storm-cloud had moved so far over the sun that it had grown dark, as in an eclipse. The wind stubbornly, as though insisting on its own way, held Levin back, and, tearing leaves and blossom from the limes and stripping the white branches of the birches strangely and hideously bare, bent everything to one side: acacias, flowers, burdocks, grass and the tops of the trees. The girls who had been working in the garden ran shrieking under the roof of the servants’ quarters. The white curtain of pouring rain had already taken in the whole of the distant wood and half the near field, and was moving fast towards the Kolok. The dampness of the rain, breaking into fine drops, could be felt in the air.

Bending his head forward and struggling with the wind, which was tearing the rugs from him, Levin was already running up to the Kolok and could already see something white behind the oak, when suddenly everything blazed, the whole earth seemed to catch fire, and the vault of heaven seemed to crack overhead. Opening his dazzled eyes, Levin, through the thick curtain of rain that now separated him from the Kolok, saw with horror first of all that the green crown of the oak he knew so well, in the middle of the wood, had strangely changed its position. “Can it have been struck?” Levin barely had time to think, when, moving faster and faster, the crown of the oak disappeared behind the other trees, and he heard the crash of a big tree falling on the other trees.

The flash of lightning, the sound of the thunder, and the sensation of his body suddenly drenched with cold merged for Levin into one impression of horror.

“My God! My God, not on them!” he said.

And though he at once thought how senseless his prayer was that they should not have been killed by the oak, which had already fallen, he repeated it, knowing that he could do nothing better than this senseless prayer.

Running up to the place where they usually were, he did not find them.

They were at the other end of the wood, under an old lime tree, and were calling him. Two figures in dark dresses (they had been wearing light ones) were standing bent over something. It was Kitty and the nurse. The rain was already stopping, and it was beginning to grow light, when Levin ran up to them. The hem of the nurse’s dress was dry, but Kitty’s dress was soaked through and clung all over her. Although there was no more rain, they were still standing in the same position they had taken when the storm broke. Both were standing bent over a little carriage with a green hood.

“Alive? Safe? Thank God!” he said, splashing through the standing water in a shoe that had come loose and was full of water, and running up to them.

Kitty’s rosy, wet face was turned towards him and smiled timidly from under her hat, which had lost its shape.

“Well, aren’t you ashamed of yourself! I don’t understand how anyone can be so careless!” he attacked his wife with vexation.

“Honestly, it wasn’t my fault. We were just going to leave, when he made a mess. We had to change him. We had only just…” Kitty began apologizing.

Mitya was safe, dry, and had not stopped sleeping.

“Well, thank God! I don’t know what I’m saying!”

They gathered up the wet napkins; the nurse took out the baby and carried him. Levin walked beside his wife, guiltily, because of his vexation, pressing her hand when the nurse could not see.

## Chapter XVIII

All through that day, during the most various conversations, in which he took part, as it were, only with the outer side of his mind, Levin, in spite of his disappointment over the change that was to have taken place in him, never stopped hearing joyfully the fullness of his heart.

After the rain it was too wet to go for a walk; besides, the storm-clouds did not leave the horizon, and passed now here, now there, thundering and blackening, along the edges of the sky. The whole party spent the rest of the day at home.

No more arguments were started; on the contrary, after dinner everyone was in the best of spirits.

Katavasov at first amused the ladies with his original jokes, which always pleased people so much on first acquaintance with him; but then, prompted by Sergei Ivanovich, he told them his very interesting observations on the difference of character, and even of physiognomy, between the female and male house-fly, and on their life. Sergei Ivanovich too was in good spirits, and at tea, prompted by his brother, he set out his view of the future of the Eastern question, so simply and well that everyone listened with rapt attention.

Only Kitty could not hear him to the end: she was called away to bath Mitya.

A few minutes after Kitty had gone, Levin too was called to her in the nursery.

Leaving his tea, and also sorry to have the interesting conversation broken off, and at the same time uneasy about why he had been called, since this happened only on important occasions, Levin went to the nursery.

Although Sergei Ivanovich’s plan, which he had not heard to the end, of how the liberated world of forty million Slavs, together with Russia, was to begin a new epoch in history, interested him very much as something completely new to him; although both curiosity and uneasiness about why he had been called were troubling him; as soon as he was alone, having left the drawing-room, he at once remembered his morning’s thoughts. And all these considerations about the significance of the Slav element in world history seemed to him so insignificant compared with what was taking place in his soul that he instantly forgot it all and was carried back into the very mood he had been in that morning.

He did not now recall, as he used to before, the whole train of thought (he did not need it). He was carried at once into the feeling that guided him, which was connected with these thoughts, and he found this feeling in his soul still stronger and more definite than before. Now it was not with him as it used to be with the comforting arguments he had invented before, when he had to reconstruct the whole train of thought in order to find the feeling. Now, on the contrary, the feeling of joy and peace was more alive than before, and thought could not keep up with feeling.

He was crossing the terrace and looking at two stars that had come out in the sky, which had already grown dark, and suddenly he remembered: “Yes, looking at the sky, I was thinking that the vault I see is not an untruth, and in that I did not think something through, I hid something from myself,” he thought. “But whatever it may be, there can be no objection. One has only to think, and everything will become clear!”

Just as he was going into the nursery he remembered what it was that he had hidden from himself. It was this: if the chief proof of the Deity is His revelation of what is good, why is this revelation confined to the Christian church alone? What relation to this revelation have the beliefs of the Buddhists and the Mohammedans, who also profess and do good?

It seemed to him that he had an answer to this question; but he had not yet had time to put it into words for himself when he was already in the nursery.

Kitty was standing with her sleeves rolled up by the bath, over the baby splashing in it, and, hearing her husband’s steps, she turned her face to him and called him to her with a smile. With one hand she was supporting the head of the plump baby, who was floating on his back and stretching out his little legs; with the other, evenly tensing her muscle, she was squeezing the sponge over him.

“There, look, look!” she said, when her husband came up to her. “Agafya Mikhailovna is right. He knows us.”

The point was that from that day on Mitya evidently, undoubtedly, knew all his own people.

As soon as Levin came up to the bath, the experiment was shown to him at once, and it was entirely successful. The cook, called in on purpose, took Kitty’s place and bent over the baby. He frowned and shook his head in refusal. Kitty bent over him, and he beamed with a smile, propped his little hands against the sponge and blew bubbles with his lips, making such a contented and strange sound that not only Kitty and the nurse but Levin too were seized with unexpected delight.

The baby was lifted out of the bath on one hand, rinsed with water, wrapped in a sheet, dried, and, after a piercing cry, handed to his mother.

“Well, I am glad you are beginning to love him,” said Kitty to her husband, after she had settled down calmly in her usual place with the baby at her breast. “I am very glad. Because it was beginning to grieve me. You said you felt nothing for him.”

“No, did I say I felt nothing? I only said I was disappointed.”

“What, disappointed in him?”

“Not exactly disappointed in him, but in my feeling; I expected more. I expected that a new, pleasant feeling would open in me, like a surprise. And suddenly, instead of that: squeamishness, pity…”

She listened to him attentively over the baby, putting back on her slender fingers the rings she had taken off to bath Mitya.

“And above all, that there is much more fear and pity than pleasure. Today, after that fear during the storm, I understood how I love him.”

Kitty beamed with a smile.

“And were you very frightened?” she said. “So was I, but I am more frightened now that it is over. I shall go and look at the oak. And how nice Katavasov is! And the whole day has been so pleasant altogether. And you are so good with Sergei Ivanych when you want to be… Well, go back to them. It is always hot and steamy here after the bath…”

## Chapter XIX

Coming out of the nursery and finding himself alone, Levin at once remembered again the thought in which there had been something unclear.

Instead of going into the drawing-room, from which voices could be heard, he stopped on the terrace and, leaning his elbows on the balustrade, began looking at the sky.

It was quite dark now, and in the south, where he was looking, there were no clouds. The clouds stood on the opposite side. From there lightning flashed and distant thunder could be heard. Levin listened to the drops falling evenly from the limes in the garden and looked at the familiar triangle of stars and at the Milky Way running through the middle of it with its branching. At each flash of lightning not only the Milky Way but the bright stars vanished; but as soon as the lightning went out they appeared again in the same places, as though thrown there by some unerring hand.

“Well, what is it that troubles me?” Levin said to himself, feeling beforehand that the solution of his doubts, though he did not yet know it, was already ready in his soul.

“Yes, the one obvious, undoubted manifestation of the Deity is the laws of goodness, which have been shown to the world by revelation, and which I feel within myself, and in acknowledging which I am not so much joined as, willy-nilly, joined together with other people into one community of believers, which is called the church. Well, and the Jews, the Mohammedans, the Confucians, the Buddhists: what are they?” he put to himself the very question that had seemed dangerous to him.

“Can these hundreds of millions of people be deprived of that best blessing without which life has no meaning?” He grew thoughtful, but at once corrected himself. “But what am I asking about?” he said to himself. “I am asking about the relation to the Deity of all the various beliefs of all mankind. I am asking about the general manifestation of God for the whole world with all those nebulae. What am I doing? To me personally, to my heart, a knowledge has been revealed beyond doubt, a knowledge beyond the grasp of reason, and I obstinately want to express this knowledge by reason and in words.

“Don’t I know that the stars don’t move?” he asked himself, looking at a bright planet which had already changed its position in relation to the top branch of a birch. “But, looking at the movement of the stars, I cannot imagine the rotation of the earth, and I am right when I say that the stars move.

“And could the astronomers have understood and calculated anything if they had taken into account all the complex and various movements of the earth? All their wonderful conclusions about the distances, weights, movements and perturbations of the heavenly bodies are based only on the visible movement of the luminaries round a motionless earth, on that very movement which is before me now and which has been so for millions of people through the ages, and was and always will be the same, and can always be verified. And just as the conclusions of the astronomers would be idle and shaky if they were not based on observations of the visible sky in relation to one meridian and one horizon, so my conclusions would be idle and shaky if they were not based on that understanding of goodness which has always been and always will be the same for everyone, and which has been revealed to me by Christianity and can always be verified in my soul. The question of other beliefs and their relation to the Deity I have neither the right nor the possibility to decide.”

“Oh, you haven’t gone in?” said Kitty’s voice suddenly; she was going the same way to the drawing-room. “Is anything upsetting you?” she said, peering attentively at his face by the light of the stars.

But even so she would not have made out his face if the lightning, hiding the stars again, had not lit it up. By the light of the lightning she made out his whole face, and, seeing that he was calm and joyful, she smiled at him.

“She understands,” he thought; “she knows what I am thinking about. Shall I tell her or not? Yes, I’ll tell her.” But at the moment when he was about to begin speaking, she began too.

“Listen, Kostya! Do me a favour,” she said. “Go into the corner room and see how they have arranged everything for Sergei Ivanovich. I feel awkward about it. Have they put in the new washstand?”

“All right, I’ll certainly go,” said Levin, getting up and kissing her.

“No, I must not speak of it,” he thought, when she had gone on ahead of him. “It is a secret, needed and important for me alone, and not to be expressed in words.

“This new feeling has not changed me, has not made me happy, has not enlightened me all at once, as I dreamed it would: just like the feeling for my son. There was no surprise in it either. And faith, or not faith, I don’t know what it is; but this feeling has come in just as imperceptibly through sufferings and has settled firmly in my soul.

“I shall still get angry with Ivan the coachman in the same way, I shall argue in the same way, I shall express my thoughts out of place; there will still be the same wall between the holy of holies of my soul and other people, even my wife; I shall still blame her for my own fear and repent of it; I shall still not understand with my reason why I pray, and I shall still pray; but my life now, my whole life, independently of everything that may happen to me, every minute of it, is not only not meaningless, as it was before, but has the undoubted meaning of the good, which it is in my power to put into it!”

## The End
