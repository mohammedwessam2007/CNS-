## Part Seven · Chapter XXVI

Never before had a day passed in a quarrel. Today was the first time. And this was not a quarrel. It was an open confession of complete coldness. How could he look at her as he had looked when he came into the room for the certificate? Look at her, see that her heart was breaking with despair, and walk past in silence with that indifferently calm face? It was not that he had grown cold to her; he hated her, because he loved another woman: that was clear.

And, remembering all the cruel words he had said, Anna invented still other words that he evidently wished to say and could have said to her, and grew more and more irritated.

“I am not keeping you,” he might say. “You may go where you like. You did not want to divorce your husband, probably so as to go back to him. Go back. If you need money, I will give it to you. How many roubles do you need?”

All the cruellest words a coarse man could say, he said to her in her imagination, and she did not forgive him for them, as though he had really said them. “And wasn’t it only yesterday that he was swearing he loved me, he, a truthful and honest man? Haven’t I despaired for nothing many times already?” she said to herself straight after.

All that day, except for her visit to Mrs Wilson, which took her two hours, Anna spent in doubts about whether everything was over or there was hope of a reconciliation, and whether she ought to leave at once or see him once more. She waited for him the whole day, and in the evening, going to her room and leaving word that he should be told she had a headache, she made a wager with herself: “If he comes in spite of what the maid says, it means he still loves me. If not, it means everything is over, and then I shall decide what to do!…”

In the evening she heard the rumble of his carriage stopping, his ring, his steps and his talk with the maid: he believed what he was told, did not want to find out anything more, and went to his own rooms. So everything was over.

And death, as the only means of restoring in his heart his love for her, of punishing him and of winning the victory in the struggle that the evil spirit lodged in her heart was waging with him, presented itself to her clearly and vividly.

Now it was all the same: to go or not to go to Vozdvizhenskoe, to get or not to get a divorce from her husband; none of it was needed. One thing was needed: to punish him.

When she poured out her usual dose of opium and thought that she had only to drink the whole bottle to die, it seemed to her so easy and simple that she again began thinking with enjoyment of how he would suffer, repent and love her memory when it was too late. She lay in bed with her eyes open, looking by the light of a single guttering candle at the moulded cornice of the ceiling and at the shadow of the screen that covered part of it, and vividly imagined what he would feel when she was gone and was only a memory for him. “How could I say those cruel words to her?” he will say. “How could I leave the room without saying anything to her? But now she is gone. She has gone from us for ever. She is there…” Suddenly the shadow of the screen wavered, spread over the whole cornice, the whole ceiling; other shadows from the other side rushed to meet it; for an instant the shadows ran back, but then came on again with fresh speed, wavered, merged, and everything went dark. “Death!” she thought. And such horror came over her that for a long time she could not understand where she was, and for a long time could not find the matches with her trembling hands and light another candle in place of the one that had burned down and gone out. “No, anything, only to live! After all, I love him. After all, he loves me! This has been, and it will pass,” she said, feeling that tears of joy at her return to life were running down her cheeks. And, to save herself from her fear, she hurried to his study.

He was asleep in the study, sound asleep. She went up to him and, holding the light above his face, looked at him for a long time. Now, while he slept, she loved him so that at the sight of him she could not hold back tears of tenderness; but she knew that if he woke he would look at her with a cold glance, conscious of his own rightness, and that before speaking to him of her love she would have to prove to him how much he had wronged her. Without waking him, she went back to her room, and after another dose of opium fell towards morning into a heavy, incomplete sleep, during which she never ceased to be aware of herself.

In the morning a terrible nightmare, which had come to her several times in her dreams even before her liaison with Vronsky, came to her again and woke her. A little old peasant with a tousled beard was doing something, bending over some iron, muttering meaningless French words; and she, as always in this nightmare (and this was what made it terrible), felt that the little peasant was paying no attention to her, but was doing this terrible thing in the iron over her, was doing something terrible over her. And she woke in a cold sweat.

When she got up she remembered the day before as if in a mist.

“There was a quarrel. There was what has happened several times before. I said I had a headache, and he did not come in. Tomorrow we are going; I must see him and get ready for the journey,” she said to herself. And, learning that he was in his study, she went to him. As she crossed the drawing-room she heard a carriage stop at the entrance, and, looking out of the window, saw a carriage from which a young girl in a lilac hat was leaning out, giving some order to the footman who was ringing. After some talk in the hall someone came upstairs, and next to the drawing-room Vronsky’s steps were heard. He was going quickly down the stairs. Anna went to the window again. There he came out on the porch without his hat and went up to the carriage. The young girl in the lilac hat handed him a packet. Vronsky, smiling, said something to her. The carriage drove off; he ran quickly back up the stairs.

The mist that had covered everything in her soul suddenly lifted. The feelings of the day before wrung her sick heart with fresh pain. She could not understand now how she could have humiliated herself so far as to spend a whole day with him in his house. She went into his study to announce her decision to him.

“That was the Sorokina woman and her daughter, who called to bring me money and papers from maman. I couldn’t get them yesterday. How is your head, better?” he said calmly, not wishing to see and understand the gloomy and solemn expression of her face.

She stood in the middle of the room, looking at him fixedly in silence. He glanced at her, frowned for an instant, and went on reading the letter. She turned and walked slowly out of the room. He could still have brought her back, but she reached the door and he still said nothing, and only the rustling sound of a sheet of paper being turned over could be heard.

“Oh, by the way,” he said, just as she was in the doorway, “we are definitely going tomorrow? Aren’t we?”

“You are, but not I,” she said, turning round to him.

“Anna, it is impossible to live like this…”

“You are, but not I,” she repeated.

“This is becoming unbearable!”

“You… you will repent of this,” she said, and went out.

Frightened by the desperate expression with which these words had been said, he jumped up and was about to run after her, but, coming to his senses, he sat down again and, clenching his teeth tight, frowned. This unseemly threat of something, as he considered it, irritated him. “I have tried everything,” he thought; “there remains only one thing: to pay no attention.” And he began getting ready to drive to town and again to his mother’s, from whom he had to get a signature on a power of attorney.

She heard the sounds of his steps in the study and the dining-room. At the drawing-room he stopped. But he did not turn towards her; he only gave an order that the stallion should be let go to Voitov without him. Then she heard the carriage brought round, the door open, and him go out again. But now he came back into the hall, and someone ran upstairs. It was his valet, running up for the gloves he had forgotten. She went to the window and saw him take the gloves without looking, touch the coachman on the back with his hand, and say something to him. Then, without looking up at the windows, he settled into his usual position in the carriage, crossing his legs, and, putting on his glove, disappeared round the corner.

## Chapter XXVII

“He has gone! It is over!” Anna said to herself, standing at the window; and in answer to this question the impressions of the darkness when the candle went out and of the terrible dream, merging into one, filled her heart with cold horror.

“No, it cannot be!” she cried, and, crossing the room, rang hard. She was so afraid now of being left alone that, without waiting for the servant to come, she went to meet him.

“Find out where the count has gone,” she said.

The servant answered that the count had gone to the stables.

“He left word that if you wish to go out, the carriage will be back directly.”

“Very well. Wait. I will write a note at once. Send Mikhaila with the note to the stables. Quickly.”

She sat down and wrote:

“I am to blame. Come home; we must have it out. For God’s sake come, I am frightened.”

She sealed it and gave it to the servant.

She was afraid of staying alone now, and she left the room after the servant and went to the nursery.

“Why, this is not it, this is not he! Where are his blue eyes, his sweet, shy smile?” was her first thought when she saw her plump, rosy little girl with black hair instead of Seryozha, whom, in the confusion of her thoughts, she had expected to see in the nursery. The little girl, sitting at the table, was banging on it stubbornly and hard with a cork and looking senselessly at her mother with her two currants of black eyes. Having answered the English nurse that she was quite well and was leaving for the country the next day, Anna sat down by the little girl and began twirling the stopper of a decanter in front of her. But the child’s loud, ringing laughter and the movement she made with her eyebrow reminded her so vividly of Vronsky that, holding back her sobs, she got up hurriedly and went out. “Can it really be all over? No, it can’t be,” she thought. “He will come back. But how will he explain to me that smile, that animation, after he had been talking to her? But even if he doesn’t explain, I shall still believe him. If I don’t believe him, there is only one thing left for me, and I don’t want it.”

She looked at the clock. Twelve minutes had passed. “Now he has got the note and is coming back. Not long, another ten minutes… But what if he doesn’t come? No, that can’t be. He mustn’t see me with tear-stained eyes. I’ll go and wash. Yes, yes, have I done my hair or not?” she asked herself. And she could not remember. She felt her head with her hand. “Yes, my hair is done, but when, I simply don’t remember.” She did not even trust her hand, and went to the pier-glass to see whether her hair really was done or not. It was done, and she could not remember when she had done it. “Who is that?” she thought, looking in the mirror at an inflamed face with strangely shining eyes looking at her in fright. “Why, it is I,” she suddenly understood; and, looking herself all over, she felt his kisses on her, and, shuddering, moved her shoulders. Then she raised her hand to her lips and kissed it.

“What is this? I am going out of my mind,” and she went into the bedroom, where Annushka was tidying the room.

“Annushka,” she said, stopping in front of her and looking at the maid, not knowing herself what she would say to her.

“You meant to go to Darya Alexandrovna’s,” said the maid, as though understanding.

“To Darya Alexandrovna’s? Yes, I shall go.”

“Fifteen minutes there, fifteen back. He is coming already, he will be here directly.” She took out her watch and looked at it. “But how could he go away and leave me in such a state? How can he live without making it up with me?” She went to the window and began looking out into the street. By the time, he could already have come back. But her reckoning might be wrong, and again she began remembering when he had left and counting the minutes.

Just as she was going to the big clock to check her watch, someone drove up. Looking out of the window, she saw his carriage. But no one came up the stairs, and voices could be heard below. It was the messenger, who had come back in the carriage. She went down to him.

“The count had already gone. He has gone to the Nizhny Novgorod station.”

“What do you want? What?…” she said to the rosy, cheerful Mikhaila, who was handing her back her note.

“Why, he never got it,” she remembered.

“Go with this note to the country, to Countess Vronskaya’s. You know where? And bring back an answer at once,” she said to the messenger.

“And what shall I do myself?” she thought. “Yes, I shall go to Dolly’s, that’s true, otherwise I shall go out of my mind. Yes, and I can telegraph as well.” And she wrote a telegram:

“I must talk to you; come at once.”

Having sent off the telegram, she went to dress. Dressed now, and in her hat, she looked again into the eyes of the plump, placid Annushka. Open compassion could be seen in those small, kind grey eyes.

“Annushka, dear, what am I to do?” said Anna, sobbing, sinking helplessly into an armchair.

“Why worry so, Anna Arkadyevna! These things happen, you know. Go out, it will take your mind off things,” said the maid.

“Yes, I shall go,” said Anna, coming to herself and getting up. “And if there is a telegram while I am out, send it on to Darya Alexandrovna’s… No, I shall come back myself.”

“Yes, I mustn’t think, I must do something, drive somewhere, above all get out of this house,” she said, listening in horror to the terrible tumult going on in her heart; and she went out hurriedly and got into the carriage.

“Where shall I tell him to drive?” asked Pyotr, before getting up on the box.

“To Znamenka, to the Oblonskys’.”

## Chapter XXVIII

The weather was clear. All morning a fine, close drizzle had been falling, and now it had lately cleared up. The iron roofs, the flagstones of the pavements, the cobbles of the roadway, the wheels and leather, the brass and tin of the carriages: everything was shining brightly in the May sun. It was three o’clock, the liveliest time in the streets.

Sitting in the corner of the comfortable carriage, which swayed slightly on its springy suspension as the greys went quickly along, Anna, amid the unceasing rattle of the wheels and the quickly changing impressions of the open air, going over the events of the last days again, saw her position quite differently from how it had seemed to her at home. Now even the thought of death no longer seemed so terrible and so clear to her, and death itself no longer seemed inevitable. Now she reproached herself for the humiliation to which she had stooped. “I beg him to forgive me. I have submitted to him. I have admitted I was to blame. Why? Can’t I live without him?” And, not answering the question of how she would live without him, she began reading the signboards. “Office and warehouse. Dentist. Yes, I shall tell Dolly everything. She doesn’t like Vronsky. It will be shameful and painful, but I shall tell her everything. She loves me, and I shall follow her advice. I won’t submit to him; I won’t let him educate me. Filippov, bakers. They say they send their dough to Petersburg. The Moscow water is so good. And the wells of Mytishchi, and the pancakes.” And she remembered how long, long ago, when she was still seventeen, she had gone with her aunt to the Trinity monastery. “By horses, too, in those days. Was that really I, with red hands? How much of what seemed so beautiful and out of reach to me then has become worthless, and what was mine then is now out of reach for ever. Would I have believed then that I could come down to such humiliation? How proud and pleased he will be when he gets my note! But I shall prove to him… How badly that paint smells. Why do they keep painting and building? Fashions and millinery,” she read. A man bowed to her. It was Annushka’s husband. “Our parasites,” she remembered Vronsky saying. “Ours? Why ours? The terrible thing is that one cannot tear the past out by the roots. One cannot tear it out, but one can hide the memory of it. And I shall hide it.” And here she remembered her past with Alexei Alexandrovich, and how she had wiped him out of her memory. “Dolly will think I am leaving a second husband, and that therefore I must surely be in the wrong. As if I wanted to be in the right! I can’t!” she said, and she felt like crying. But at once she began wondering what those two girls could be smiling about so. “About love, I expect? They don’t know how joyless it is, how low… The boulevard and children. Three boys running, playing at horses. Seryozha! And I shall lose everything and not get him back. Yes, I shall lose everything if he doesn’t come back. Perhaps he missed the train and is back already. Wanting humiliation again!” she said to herself. “No, I shall go in to Dolly and tell her straight out: I am unhappy, I deserve it, I am to blame, but still I am unhappy; help me. These horses, this carriage, how loathsome I am to myself in this carriage: all of it is his; but I shall not see them any more.”

Thinking out the words in which she would tell Dolly everything, and deliberately working her heart up, Anna went up the stairs.

“Is anyone here?” she asked in the hall.

“Katerina Alexandrovna Levina,” answered the footman.

“Kitty! That same Kitty with whom Vronsky was in love,” thought Anna, “the one he remembered with love. He is sorry he didn’t marry her. And me he remembers with hatred, and he is sorry he took up with me.”

When Anna arrived, the sisters were having a consultation about nursing the baby. Dolly came out alone to meet the visitor, who at that moment was interrupting their talk.

“So you haven’t gone yet? I meant to come to you myself,” she said. “I had a letter from Stiva today.”

“We had a telegram too,” answered Anna, looking round to see Kitty.

“He writes that he can’t understand what exactly Alexei Alexandrovich wants, but that he won’t leave without an answer.”

“I thought you had someone here. May I read the letter?”

“Yes, Kitty,” said Dolly, embarrassed. “She stayed in the nursery. She has been very ill.”

“I heard. May I read the letter?”

“I’ll bring it at once. But he does not refuse; on the contrary, Stiva is hopeful,” said Dolly, stopping in the doorway.

“I am not hopeful, and I don’t even wish it,” said Anna.

“What is this, does Kitty consider it humiliating to meet me?” thought Anna, left alone. “Perhaps she is right. But it is not for her, who was in love with Vronsky, it is not for her to show me that, even if it is true. I know that in my position no decent woman can receive me. I know that from that first moment I sacrificed everything to him! And here is my reward! Oh, how I hate him! And why have I come here? It is even worse for me, even harder.” She heard from the other room the voices of the sisters talking together. “And what am I to say to Dolly now? Comfort Kitty by showing her I am unhappy, submit to her patronage? No, and Dolly would understand nothing. And I have nothing to say to her. It would only be interesting to see Kitty and show her how I despise everyone and everything, how nothing matters to me now.”

Dolly came in with the letter. Anna read it and handed it back in silence.

“I knew all that,” she said. “And it doesn’t interest me in the least.”

“But why not? On the contrary, I am hopeful,” said Dolly, looking at Anna with curiosity. She had never seen her in such a strange, irritated state. “When are you leaving?” she asked.

Anna, screwing up her eyes, looked in front of her and did not answer.

“Why is Kitty hiding from me?” she said, looking at the door and blushing.

“Oh, what nonsense! She is nursing, and it isn’t going well, and I was advising her… She is very glad. She’ll be here directly,” said Dolly awkwardly, not knowing how to tell an untruth. “Ah, here she is.”

Learning that Anna had come, Kitty had wanted not to come out; but Dolly had persuaded her. Gathering her strength, Kitty came out and, blushing, went up to her and gave her her hand.

“I am very glad,” she said in a trembling voice.

Kitty was confused by the struggle going on in her between hostility to this bad woman and a wish to be indulgent to her; but as soon as she saw Anna’s beautiful, sympathetic face, all the hostility vanished at once.

“I would not have been surprised if you hadn’t wanted to meet me. I am used to everything. You have been ill? Yes, you have changed,” said Anna.

Kitty felt that Anna was looking at her with hostility. She put this hostility down to the awkward position in which Anna, who had once patronized her, now felt herself before her, and she felt sorry for her.

They talked about the illness, the baby, Stiva, but evidently nothing interested Anna.

“I came to say goodbye to you,” she said, getting up.

“When are you leaving, then?”

But again Anna, without answering, turned to Kitty.

“Yes, I am very glad to have seen you,” she said with a smile. “I have heard so much about you from all sides, even from your husband. He came to see me, and I liked him very much,” she added, evidently with bad intent. “Where is he?”

“He has gone to the country,” said Kitty, blushing.

“Give him my regards; be sure to give him my regards.”

“I certainly will!” Kitty repeated naively, looking into her eyes with compassion.

“Well, goodbye, Dolly!” And, kissing Dolly and pressing Kitty’s hand, Anna went out hurriedly.

“She is just the same, and just as attractive. Very lovely!” said Kitty, left alone with her sister. “But there is something pitiful about her! Terribly pitiful!”

“No, today there is something special about her,” said Dolly. “When I was seeing her out in the hall, it seemed to me she wanted to cry.”

## Chapter XXIX

Anna got into the carriage in a still worse state than when she had left home. To her former torments was now added a feeling of insult and rejection, which she had clearly felt at the meeting with Kitty.

“Where to? Home?” asked Pyotr.

“Yes, home,” she said, not even thinking now of where she was going.

“How they looked at me, as at something terrible, incomprehensible and curious. What can he be telling the other so heatedly?” she thought, looking at two people walking. “Can one ever tell another what one feels? I wanted to tell Dolly, and it is a good thing I didn’t. How glad she would have been at my unhappiness! She would have hidden it; but her main feeling would have been joy that I am punished for the pleasures she envied me. Kitty would have been gladder still. How I see right through her! She knows I was more than usually charming to her husband. And she is jealous and hates me. And despises me besides. In her eyes I am an immoral woman. If I were an immoral woman, I could have made her husband fall in love with me… if I had wanted to. And I did want to. Now there is someone pleased with himself,” she thought of a fat, red-faced gentleman driving past the other way, who took her for an acquaintance and raised his glossy hat over his bald, glossy head, and then realized he was mistaken. “He thought he knew me. And he knows me as little as anyone in the world knows me. I don’t know myself. I know my appetites, as the French say. Those two want that dirty ice-cream. That they know for certain,” she thought, looking at two boys who had stopped an ice-cream seller, who was taking the tub off his head and wiping his sweaty face with the end of a towel. “All of us want something sweet and tasty. If there are no sweets, then dirty ice-cream. And Kitty the same: if not Vronsky, then Levin. And she envies me. And hates me. And we all hate one another. I Kitty, Kitty me. That is the truth. Tyutkin, coiffeur… Je me fais coiffer par Tyutkin… I’ll tell him that when he comes,” she thought, and smiled. But at that same moment she remembered that she now had no one to tell anything funny to. “And there is nothing funny, nothing gay. Everything is loathsome. They are ringing for vespers, and how carefully that merchant crosses himself! As if he were afraid of dropping something. What are these churches for, this ringing and this lie? Only to hide that we all hate one another, like these cabmen swearing at each other so viciously. Yashvin says: he wants to strip me to my shirt, and I him. That is the truth!”

She was in the midst of these thoughts, which carried her away so that she even stopped thinking of her position, when the carriage stopped at the porch of her house. Only when she saw the porter coming out to meet her did she remember that she had sent the note and the telegram.

“Is there an answer?” she asked.

“I’ll look directly,” answered the porter, and, glancing at the desk, he took out and handed her the thin square envelope of a telegram. “I cannot come before ten o’clock. Vronsky,” she read.

“And has the messenger not come back?”

“No, madam,” answered the porter.

“Ah, if so, then I know what to do,” she said, and, feeling a vague anger and a need for revenge rising in her, she ran upstairs. “I shall go to him myself. Before going away for ever, I shall tell him everything. Never have I hated anyone as I hate this man!” she thought. Seeing his hat on the stand, she shuddered with revulsion. She did not reflect that his telegram was an answer to her telegram and that he had not yet received her note. She pictured him now talking calmly with his mother and with the Sorokina girl and rejoicing at her sufferings. “Yes, I must go quickly,” she said to herself, not yet knowing where to go. She wanted to get away as quickly as possible from the feelings she was experiencing in this terrible house. The servants, the walls, the things in this house: everything aroused revulsion and anger in her and pressed on her with a kind of weight.

“Yes, I must go to the railway station, and if he is not there, go on there and catch him out.” Anna looked in the newspaper at the timetable of the trains. In the evening one left at two minutes past eight. “Yes, I shall be in time.” She ordered other horses to be harnessed and busied herself packing in a travelling-bag the things she would need for a few days. She knew she would not come back here again. Among the plans that came into her head she vaguely decided that, after whatever happened there at the station or at the countess’s estate, she would go on along the Nizhny Novgorod line to the first town and stay there.

Dinner was on the table; she went up to it, smelled the bread and cheese, and, satisfied that the smell of anything to eat was repulsive to her, ordered the carriage and went out. The house was already throwing its shadow right across the street, and it was a clear evening, still warm in the sun. And Annushka, who came out with her things, and Pyotr, putting the things into the carriage, and the coachman, evidently displeased: all of them were repulsive to her and irritated her with their words and movements.

“I don’t need you, Pyotr.”

“But what about the ticket?”

“Well, as you like, it’s all the same to me,” she said with vexation.

Pyotr jumped up on the box and, setting his arms akimbo, told the coachman to drive to the station.

## Chapter XXX

“There it is again! Again I understand everything,” Anna said to herself as soon as the carriage set off and, swaying, rattled over the small cobbles, and again impressions began to follow one another.

“Yes, what was the last thing I was thinking so well about?” she tried to remember. “Tyutkin, coiffeur? No, not that. Yes, about what Yashvin says: the struggle for existence and hatred are the one thing that binds people together. No, you are going for nothing,” she addressed in her mind a party in a carriage and four, evidently going out of town to enjoy themselves. “And the dog you are taking with you won’t help you. You won’t get away from yourselves.” Glancing in the direction Pyotr had turned to look, she saw a factory worker, almost dead drunk, his head lolling, whom a policeman was taking somewhere. “That one sooner,” she thought. “Count Vronsky and I have not found that pleasure either, though we expected a great deal of it.” And now for the first time Anna turned that bright light in which she was seeing everything on her relations with him, which she had avoided thinking about before. “What was he seeking in me? Not love so much as the satisfaction of vanity.” She remembered his words, the expression of his face, like a submissive setter dog’s, in the first days of their liaison. And everything now confirmed it. “Yes, there was in him the triumph of a vain success. Of course there was love too, but the greater part was pride in success. He boasted of me. Now that is past. There is nothing to be proud of. Not proud but ashamed. He has taken from me all he could, and now I am no use to him. He finds me a burden and tries not to behave dishonourably towards me. He let it slip yesterday: he wants the divorce and the marriage so as to burn his boats. He loves me, but how? The zest is gone. That one wants to astonish everyone and is very pleased with himself,” she thought, looking at a red-faced shop assistant riding a riding-school horse. “Yes, I no longer have the same savour for him. If I go away from him, in the depths of his soul he will be glad.”

This was not a supposition: she saw it clearly in that piercing light which now revealed to her the meaning of life and of human relations.

“My love keeps growing more passionate and more selfish, and his keeps going out and going out, and that is why we are drifting apart,” she went on thinking. “And it cannot be helped. For me everything is in him alone, and I demand that he give himself to me more and more entirely. And he wants more and more to get away from me. We were walking towards each other until our liaison, and then we have been irresistibly drifting in different directions. And that cannot be changed. He tells me I am senselessly jealous, and I have told myself I am senselessly jealous; but it is not true. I am not jealous, I am dissatisfied. But…” She opened her mouth and shifted in the carriage from the agitation roused in her by a thought that had suddenly come to her. “If I could be anything but a mistress, passionately loving only his caresses; but I cannot and do not want to be anything else. And by that wish I rouse disgust in him, and he anger in me, and it cannot be otherwise. Don’t I know that he would not deceive me, that he has no designs on the Sorokina girl, that he is not in love with Kitty, that he will not be unfaithful to me? I know all that, but it makes it no easier for me. If he, not loving me, is kind and tender to me out of duty, and what I want is not there, why, that is a thousand times worse even than anger! That is hell! And that is just what it is. He has not loved me for a long time now. And where love ends, hatred begins. I don’t know these streets at all. Hills of some sort, and houses, houses everywhere… And in the houses people, people… How many of them there are, no end, and all hating one another. Well, let me imagine what I want in order to be happy. Well? I get the divorce, Alexei Alexandrovich gives me Seryozha, and I marry Vronsky.” Remembering Alexei Alexandrovich, she at once pictured him with extraordinary vividness, as if alive before her, with his meek, lifeless, dull eyes, the blue veins on his white hands, his intonations and the cracking of his fingers; and, remembering the feeling there had been between them, which had also been called love, she shuddered with revulsion. “Well, I get the divorce and become Vronsky’s wife. What then, will Kitty stop looking at me as she looked today? No. And will Seryozha stop asking or thinking about my two husbands? And between Vronsky and me, what new feeling shall I invent? Is there any possibility, not of happiness now, but only of not being in torment? No and no!” she answered herself now without the slightest hesitation. “Impossible! Life is drawing us apart, and I make his unhappiness and he mine, and neither he nor I can be made over. Every attempt has been made; the screw is stripped. Yes, a beggar woman with a child. She thinks people pity her. Aren’t we all flung into the world only to hate one another, and so to torment ourselves and others? Schoolboys going along, laughing. Seryozha?” she remembered. “I too thought I loved him, and was touched by my own tenderness. And yet I have lived without him, I exchanged him for another love and did not complain of the exchange as long as I was satisfied with that love.” And with revulsion she remembered what she had called that love. And the clearness with which she now saw her own life and the life of all people gladdened her. “So it is with me, and with Pyotr, and with Fyodor the coachman, and with that merchant, and with all the people who live along the Volga, where those advertisements invite you to, and everywhere, and always,” she thought, when she had already driven up to the low building of the Nizhny Novgorod station and the porters ran out to meet her.

“Shall I take a ticket to Obiralovka?” said Pyotr.

She had quite forgotten where she was going and why, and only with a great effort could she understand the question.

“Yes,” she said to him, handing him her purse with the money, and, taking her little red bag in her hand, she got out of the carriage.

As she made her way through the crowd to the first-class waiting-room, she gradually recalled all the details of her position and the decisions between which she was wavering. And again, now hope, now despair, began to inflame the wounds of her tormented, terribly fluttering heart in the old sore places. Sitting on the star-shaped sofa waiting for the train, looking with revulsion at the people coming in and going out (all of them were repulsive to her), she thought now of how she would arrive at the station, write him a note, and what she would write to him; now of how he was at that moment complaining to his mother (not understanding her sufferings) about his position, and how she would come into the room and what she would say to him. Now she thought of how life might still be happy, and how tormentingly she loved and hated him, and how terribly her heart was beating.

## Chapter XXXI

A bell rang; some young men went past, ugly, insolent and hurried, and at the same time attentive to the impression they were making; Pyotr too crossed the room in his livery and gaiters, with his dull, animal face, and came up to her to see her to the carriage. The noisy men fell silent as she passed them on the platform, and one whispered something about her to another, something vile, of course. She climbed the high step and sat down alone in a compartment on the sprung, soiled sofa that had once been white. Her bag bounced on the springs and settled; Pyotr, with a foolish smile, raised his braided hat at the window by way of goodbye; an insolent guard slammed the door and the latch. A lady, ugly, wearing a bustle (Anna undressed this woman in her mind and was horrified at her ugliness), and a little girl, laughing unnaturally, ran past below.

“Katerina Andreevna has it, she has it all, ma tante!” cried the little girl.

“Even the little girl is warped and putting on airs,” thought Anna. So as not to see anyone, she got up quickly and sat down at the opposite window of the empty carriage. A dirty, ugly peasant in a cap, from under which his tangled hair stuck out, went past that window, bending down to the wheels of the carriage. “There is something familiar about that ugly peasant,” thought Anna. And, remembering her dream, trembling with fear, she moved away to the opposite door. The guard was opening the door, letting in a husband and wife.

“Do you wish to get out?”

Anna did not answer. The guard and the people coming in did not notice, under her veil, the horror on her face. She went back to her corner and sat down. The couple sat down on the opposite side, attentively but furtively looking over her dress. Both husband and wife seemed repulsive to Anna. The husband asked whether she would allow him to smoke, evidently not in order to smoke but to start a conversation with her. Receiving her consent, he began talking to his wife in French about things he needed to talk about even less than he needed to smoke. They talked nonsense, pretending, only so that she would hear. Anna saw clearly how sick they were of each other and how they hated each other. And it was impossible not to hate such pitiful monstrosities.

The second bell was heard, and after it the moving of luggage, noise, shouting and laughter. It was so clear to Anna that no one had anything to be glad about that this laughter irritated her painfully, and she wanted to stop her ears so as not to hear it. At last the third bell rang, a whistle sounded, the engine shrieked, the coupling-chain jerked, and the husband crossed himself. “It would be interesting to ask him what he means by that,” thought Anna, looking at him angrily. She looked past the lady out of the window at the people who had seen the train off and were standing on the platform, and who seemed to be sliding backwards. Jolting evenly at the joints of the rails, the carriage in which Anna sat rolled past the platform, a stone wall, a signal, past other carriages; the wheels sounded more smoothly and oilily on the rails, with a light ringing; the window was lit up by the bright evening sun, and a breeze played with the curtain. Anna forgot her neighbours in the carriage and, breathing in the fresh air with the light rocking of the journey, began to think again.

“Yes, where was I? At this: that I cannot imagine any position in which life would not be torment, that we are all made in order to suffer, and that we all know it and all invent means of deceiving ourselves. But when one sees the truth, what is one to do?”

“That is why man has been given reason, to rid himself of what troubles him,” said the lady in French, evidently pleased with her phrase and grimacing with her tongue.

These words seemed to answer Anna’s thought.

“To rid himself of what troubles him,” Anna repeated. And, glancing at the red-cheeked husband and the thin wife, she understood that the sickly wife considered herself a misunderstood woman, and that the husband deceived her and kept up in her this opinion of herself. Anna seemed to see their whole story and all the nooks and corners of their souls, turning the light on them. But there was nothing interesting in it, and she went on with her thought.

“Yes, it troubles me very much, and reason has been given us to rid ourselves of it; so I must rid myself of it. Why not put out the candle, when there is nothing more to look at, when it is loathsome to look at all this? But how? Why did that guard run along the footboard, why are they shouting, those young men in that carriage? Why are they talking, why are they laughing? It is all untruth, all lies, all deceit, all evil!…”

When the train came into the station, Anna got out among the crowd of other passengers and, keeping away from them as from lepers, stood on the platform, trying to remember why she had come here and what she had meant to do. Everything that had seemed possible to her before was now so hard to think out, especially in the noisy crowd of all these ugly people who would not leave her in peace. Now porters ran up to her offering their services; now young men, clattering their heels on the boards of the platform and talking loudly, looked her over; now people coming the other way stepped aside to the wrong side. Remembering that she had meant to go on further if there was no answer, she stopped a porter and asked whether there was a coachman here with a note for Count Vronsky.

“Count Vronsky? Someone from him was here just now. They were meeting Princess Sorokina and her daughter. What does the coachman look like?”

While she was talking to the porter, the coachman Mikhaila, rosy and cheerful, in a smart blue coat and a watch-chain, evidently proud of having carried out his errand so well, came up to her and handed her a note. She broke it open, and her heart contracted even before she read it.

“I am very sorry the note did not reach me. I shall be back at ten,” Vronsky had written in a careless hand.

“So! I expected it!” she said to herself with a malicious smile.

“Very well, go home, then,” she said quietly, turning to Mikhaila. She spoke quietly because the rapid beating of her heart kept her from breathing. “No, I won’t let you torment me,” she thought, addressing her threat not to him, not to herself, but to whoever was making her suffer, and she walked along the platform past the station.

Two maids walking on the platform turned their heads back to look at her, saying something aloud about her dress: “Real,” they said of the lace she was wearing. The young men would not leave her in peace. Again, peering into her face and shouting something with laughter in an unnatural voice, they went past. The station-master, passing, asked whether she was travelling. A boy selling kvass did not take his eyes off her. “My God, where am I to go?” she thought, walking further and further along the platform. At the end she stopped. Some ladies and children, who had met a gentleman in spectacles and were laughing and talking loudly, fell silent, looking her over as she came level with them. She quickened her step and moved away from them to the edge of the platform. A goods train was coming in. The platform shook, and it seemed to her that she was moving again.

And suddenly, remembering the man who had been crushed on the day of her first meeting with Vronsky, she understood what she must do. With a quick, light step she went down the steps that led from the water-tower to the rails and stopped right beside the passing train. She looked at the undersides of the carriages, at the bolts and chains and the tall cast-iron wheels of the first carriage slowly rolling by, and tried to measure by eye the middle point between the front and back wheels, and the moment when that middle would be opposite her.

“There!” she said to herself, looking into the shadow of the carriage, at the sand mixed with coal dust that covered the sleepers. “There, right in the middle, and I shall punish him and be rid of everyone and of myself.”

She meant to fall under the first carriage as its middle came level with her. But the little red bag, which she began taking off her arm, held her back, and it was too late: the middle had gone past her. She had to wait for the next carriage. A feeling like the one she used to have when, bathing, she was about to go into the water came over her, and she crossed herself. The familiar gesture of making the sign of the cross called up in her soul a whole series of memories of girlhood and childhood, and suddenly the darkness that had covered everything for her was torn apart, and life rose before her for an instant with all its bright past joys. But she did not take her eyes off the wheels of the second carriage as it came up. And exactly at the moment when the middle between the wheels came level with her, she threw away the red bag and, drawing her head down into her shoulders, fell under the carriage on her hands, and with a light movement, as though preparing to get up again at once, sank on to her knees. And at that same instant she was horrified at what she was doing. “Where am I? What am I doing? Why?” She wanted to get up, to throw herself back; but something enormous and merciless struck her on the head and dragged her by the back. “Lord, forgive me everything!” she said, feeling the impossibility of struggling. A little peasant, muttering something, was working at the iron. And the candle by which she had been reading the book filled with troubles, deceptions, grief and evil flared up with a brighter light than ever before, lit up for her everything that had been in darkness, crackled, began to dim, and went out for ever.

## Part Eight · Chapter I

Almost two months had passed. It was already the middle of a hot summer, and only now was Sergei Ivanovich getting ready to leave Moscow.

During this time events of their own had been taking place in Sergei Ivanovich’s life. About a year before, his book, the fruit of six years’ labour, had been finished; it was entitled “An Essay in the Survey of the Foundations and Forms of Statehood in Europe and in Russia”. Some sections of this book and its introduction had been printed in periodicals, and other parts had been read by Sergei Ivanovich to people of his circle, so that the ideas of the work could no longer be complete news to the public; but still Sergei Ivanovich expected that his book, by its appearance, would make a serious impression on society, and, if not a revolution in learning, then at any rate a strong stir in the scholarly world.

After careful polishing, this book had been published the year before and sent out to the booksellers.

Asking no one about it, answering his friends’ questions about how his book was doing reluctantly and with feigned indifference, not even asking the booksellers how it was selling, Sergei Ivanovich watched keenly, with strained attention, for the first impression his book would make in society and in literature.

But one week passed, a second, a third, and no impression of any kind was noticeable in society; his friends, specialists and scholars, sometimes, evidently out of politeness, began to talk about it. The rest of his acquaintances, not interested in a book of scholarly content, did not speak to him about it at all. And in society, especially now that it was occupied with something else, there was complete indifference. In literature too, for a whole month, there was not a word about the book.

Sergei Ivanovich calculated to the last detail the time needed to write a review, but a month passed, a second, and there was the same silence.

Only in the Northern Beetle, in a humorous feuilleton about the singer Drabanti, who had lost his voice, were a few contemptuous words said in passing about Koznyshev’s book, showing that this book had long since been condemned by everyone and given up to general ridicule.

At last, in the third month, a critical article appeared in a serious journal. Sergei Ivanovich knew the author of the article too. He had met him once at Golubtsov’s.

The author of the article was a very young and sickly feuilletonist, very lively as a writer, but extremely poorly educated and timid in personal relations.

In spite of his complete contempt for the author, Sergei Ivanovich set about reading the article with complete respect. The article was terrible.

The feuilletonist had evidently deliberately understood the whole book in a way in which it was impossible to understand it. But he had picked out his quotations so cleverly that for those who had not read the book (and evidently almost no one had read it) it was perfectly clear that the whole book was nothing but a collection of high-flown words, used, moreover, out of place (as the question marks showed), and that the author of the book was a completely ignorant man. And all this was so witty that Sergei Ivanovich would not himself have disowned such wit; and that was just what was terrible.

In spite of the complete conscientiousness with which Sergei Ivanovich checked the justice of the reviewer’s arguments, he did not dwell for a minute on the faults and mistakes that were being ridiculed (it was too obvious that all this had been picked out on purpose), but at once, without wishing it, he began remembering to the smallest detail his meeting and conversation with the author of the article.

“Did I offend him in some way?” Sergei Ivanovich asked himself.

And, remembering how at their meeting he had corrected this young man over a word that revealed his ignorance, Sergei Ivanovich found the explanation of the meaning of the article.

After this article there followed a dead silence about the book, both in print and in speech, and Sergei Ivanovich saw that his six years’ work, worked out with such love and labour, had passed without a trace.

Sergei Ivanovich’s position was still harder because, having finished the book, he had no more study work, which had formerly taken up the greater part of his time.

Sergei Ivanovich was clever, educated, healthy, active, and did not know where to apply all his activity. Conversations in drawing-rooms, at congresses, meetings, committees, everywhere where it was possible to talk, took up part of his time; but he, a long-time town dweller, did not allow himself to go entirely into talk, as his inexperienced brother did when he was in Moscow; a great deal of leisure and mental strength remained.

Fortunately for him, at this very time, so hard for him because of the failure of his book, the questions of the dissenters, of the American friends, of the Samara famine, of the exhibition, of spiritualism, were replaced by the Slav question, which had formerly only smouldered in society; and Sergei Ivanovich, who had been one of those who raised this question before, gave himself up to it entirely.

In the circle of people to which Sergei Ivanovich belonged, at this time nothing else was talked or written about but the Slav question and the Serbian war. Everything that the idle crowd usually does to kill time was now being done for the benefit of the Slavs. Balls, concerts, dinners, speeches, ladies’ dresses, beer, taverns: everything bore witness to sympathy with the Slavs.

With much of what was said and written on this occasion Sergei Ivanovich disagreed in the details. He saw that the Slav question had become one of those fashionable enthusiasms which always, replacing one another, serve society as an object of occupation; he saw too that there were many people who were taking up the cause with self-interested, vain aims. He admitted that the newspapers printed a great deal that was unnecessary and exaggerated, with the sole aim of drawing attention to themselves and shouting down the others. He saw that in this general upsurge of society all the failures and the aggrieved had jumped forward and were shouting louder than the rest: commanders-in-chief without armies, ministers without ministries, journalists without journals, party leaders without partisans. He saw that there was much here that was frivolous and ridiculous; but he also saw and admitted an undoubted, ever-growing enthusiasm, uniting all classes of society into one, with which it was impossible not to sympathize. The massacre of fellow believers and brother Slavs had called forth sympathy for the sufferers and indignation against the oppressors. And the heroism of the Serbs and Montenegrins, fighting for a great cause, had given rise in the whole people to a wish to help their brothers no longer in word but in deed.

But besides this there was another phenomenon that gladdened Sergei Ivanovich: it was the manifestation of public opinion. Society had definitely expressed its wish. The soul of the people had found expression, as Sergei Ivanovich said. And the more he occupied himself with this cause, the more obvious it seemed to him that it was a cause destined to take on enormous dimensions, to make an epoch.

He devoted himself entirely to the service of this great cause and forgot to think about his book.

All his time was now so occupied that he did not have time to answer all the letters and requests addressed to him.

Having worked all spring and part of the summer, it was only in July that he got ready to go to his brother’s in the country.

He was going both to rest for two weeks and, in the very holy of holies of the people, in the depths of the country, to enjoy the sight of that rising of the people’s spirit of which he and all the inhabitants of the capitals and towns were completely convinced. Katavasov, who had long been meaning to keep a promise he had made Levin to visit him, went with him.
