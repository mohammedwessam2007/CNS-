## III

I found two more of my schoolfellows there with him. They were evidently discussing an important matter. Neither of them paid almost any attention to my arrival, which was even strange, since I had not seen them for years. Evidently they took me for something like the most ordinary fly. I had not been treated like that even at school, though everyone there hated me. I understood, of course, that they must despise me now for the failure of my career in the service, and because I had let myself go badly, went about in shabby clothes and so on, which in their eyes was the signboard of my incompetence and my small importance. But all the same I had not expected such contempt. Simonov was even surprised that I had come. He had always seemed surprised when I came, even before. All this disconcerted me; I sat down in some distress and began to listen to what they were discussing.

It was a serious and even heated conversation about a farewell dinner which these gentlemen wanted to give, all together, the very next day, to a schoolfellow of theirs, Zverkov, an army officer, who was leaving for a distant province. Monsieur Zverkov had been my schoolfellow too, all through. I had begun to hate him particularly in the upper classes. In the lower classes he had been simply a pretty, lively boy whom everyone loved. I hated him in the lower classes as well, though, precisely because he was a pretty, lively boy. He always did badly at his lessons, and the further he went, the worse he did; yet he left school successfully, because he had patrons. In his last year at our school he came into an inheritance of two hundred serfs, and since almost all of us were poor, he even began to show off in front of us. He was vulgar in the highest degree, but a good-natured fellow all the same, even when he was showing off. And among us, in spite of our outward, fantastic and high-flown forms of honour and pride, everyone except a very few fawned on Zverkov, the more he showed off. And they did not fawn out of any self-interest, but simply because he was a man favoured by the gifts of nature. Besides, it was somehow accepted among us to regard Zverkov as an expert in matters of smartness and good manners. This last particularly infuriated me. I hated the sharp, self-assured sound of his voice, his adoration of his own witticisms, which came out terribly stupid, though he was bold with his tongue; I hated his handsome but silly face (for which, by the way, I would gladly have exchanged my clever one), and his free-and-easy officer’s manners of the forties. I hated the way he talked about his future successes with women (he did not dare begin with women before he had his officer’s epaulettes, and waited for them impatiently) and about how he would be fighting duels every minute. I remember how I, always silent, suddenly locked horns with Zverkov when, talking once in free time with his friends about future pleasures and frolicking at last like a young puppy in the sun, he suddenly announced that he would not leave a single village girl on his estate untouched, that it was his *droit de seigneur*, the lord’s right; and that if the peasants dared to protest, he would have them all flogged and double the rent of every one of those bearded rascals. Our louts applauded; but I locked horns with him, not at all out of pity for the girls and their fathers, but simply because such an insect was being applauded. I won that time; but Zverkov, stupid as he was, was cheerful and bold, and so he laughed it off, and in such a way that, to tell the truth, I did not quite win after all: the laugh stayed on his side. Afterwards he got the better of me several more times, but without malice, somehow in fun, in passing, laughing. I did not answer him, spitefully and contemptuously. After we left school he made a move towards me; I did not resist much, because I was flattered; but we soon and naturally drifted apart. Then I heard of his lieutenant’s successes in the barracks, of how he went on the spree. Then came other rumours: of how he was getting on in the service. He no longer greeted me in the street, and I suspected he was afraid of compromising himself by bowing to so insignificant a person as me. I saw him once in the theatre too, in the third tier, already wearing the aiguillettes of an aide-de-camp. He was fawning and bowing before the daughters of some ancient general. In three years or so he had gone off a great deal, though he was still rather handsome and smart; he had become somehow puffy and was putting on weight; one could see that by thirty he would be completely flabby. And it was to this Zverkov, now leaving at last, that our schoolfellows wanted to give a dinner. They had kept up with him all these three years, though inwardly, I am sure, they did not consider themselves his equals.

Of Simonov’s two guests, one was Ferfichkin, a Russian of German descent, short, with a monkey’s face, a fool who made fun of everyone, my bitterest enemy since the lower classes; mean, insolent, a little braggart who acted the most touchy pride, though of course he was a coward at heart. He was one of those admirers of Zverkov who made up to him out of calculation and often borrowed money from him. Simonov’s other guest, Trudolyubov, was an unremarkable person, a tall military fellow with a cold face, fairly honest, but one who worshipped every kind of success and could talk only about promotion. He was some sort of distant relative of Zverkov’s, and this, stupid as it is to say, gave him a certain importance among us. He had always considered me a nobody; he treated me, though not quite politely, at least tolerably.

“Well, at seven roubles each,” Trudolyubov began, “there are three of us, twenty-one roubles: we can dine well. Zverkov doesn’t pay, of course.”

“Of course not, since we’re inviting him,” Simonov decided.

“Do you really think,” Ferfichkin broke in arrogantly and hotly, like an insolent footman boasting of his general master’s decorations, “do you really think Zverkov will let us pay alone? He’ll accept out of delicacy, but then he’ll stand us half a dozen bottles from his own pocket.”

“What do four of us want with half a dozen?” remarked Trudolyubov, paying attention only to the half dozen.

“So, three of us, four with Zverkov, twenty-one roubles, at the Hôtel de Paris, tomorrow at five o’clock,” Simonov concluded finally; he had been chosen to organise it.

“How do you mean, twenty-one?” I said, in some agitation and even, apparently, offended. “If you count me, it won’t be twenty-one but twenty-eight roubles.”

It seemed to me that to offer myself suddenly and so unexpectedly would even be very handsome, and that they would all be won over at once and look at me with respect.

“Do you want to come too, then?” Simonov remarked with displeasure, somehow avoiding looking at me. He knew me by heart.

It infuriated me that he knew me by heart.

“Why not, sir? I’m a schoolfellow too, I believe, and I confess I even feel hurt at being left out,” I began to splutter again.

“And where were we supposed to find you?” Ferfichkin broke in rudely.

“You were always on bad terms with Zverkov,” added Trudolyubov, frowning. But I had already seized hold of it and would not let go.

“It seems to me nobody has the right to judge that,” I retorted, with a tremor in my voice, as if heaven knows what had happened. “Perhaps that is exactly why I want to come now, because I was on bad terms with him before.”

“Well, who can understand you... these lofty notions...” Trudolyubov grinned.

“You’ll be put down,” Simonov decided, turning to me. “Tomorrow at five o’clock, at the Hôtel de Paris; don’t get it wrong.”

“And the money!” Ferfichkin began in an undertone, nodding at me to Simonov, but he stopped short, because even Simonov was embarrassed.

“That’s enough,” said Trudolyubov, getting up. “If he wants to come so badly, let him.”

“But ours is a private circle, a circle of friends,” Ferfichkin said angrily, also reaching for his hat. “It isn’t an official gathering. Perhaps we don’t want you at all...”

They left; Ferfichkin did not bow to me at all as he went, Trudolyubov barely nodded without looking. Simonov, with whom I was left alone, was in a state of vexed perplexity and looked at me strangely. He did not sit down and did not ask me to sit.

“Hm... yes... tomorrow, then. Will you pay the money now? I only ask so as to know for certain,” he muttered, embarrassed.

I flared up, but as I flared up I remembered that since time immemorial I had owed Simonov fifteen roubles, which, by the way, I had never forgotten, but had never paid either.

“You must agree, Simonov, that I couldn’t have known when I came in... and I’m very annoyed that I forgot...”

“All right, all right, it doesn’t matter. Pay tomorrow at the dinner. I only wanted to know... Please don’t...”

He broke off and began to pace the room with still greater vexation. As he paced, he began to come down on his heels and stamp harder.

“Am I keeping you?” I asked, after two minutes of silence.

“Oh no!” he said with a start. “That is, to tell the truth, yes. You see, I have to call on someone... Not far from here...” he added in an apologetic voice, somewhat ashamed.

“Oh, good heavens! Why didn’t you sa-a-ay so!” I cried, seizing my cap, with an astonishingly free-and-easy air, however, which came from heaven knows where.

“It’s not far... Just a step from here...” Simonov repeated, seeing me to the hall with a fussy air that did not suit him at all. “So tomorrow at five sharp!” he shouted after me on the stairs: he was very pleased that I was going. But I was in a fury.

“What possessed me, what possessed me to push myself forward!” I ground my teeth as I strode along the street. “And for that scoundrel, that piglet Zverkov! Of course I mustn’t go; of course, to hell with it: am I bound to, or what? I’ll write to Simonov tomorrow by the city post...”

But the reason I was furious was precisely that I knew for certain I would go; that I would go on purpose; and the more tactless, the more indecent it would be for me to go, the more surely I would go.

And there was even a positive obstacle to going: I had no money. All I had in the world was nine roubles. But seven of those had to be paid the next day to Apollon, my servant, as his month’s wages; he lived with me for seven roubles a month and fed himself.

Not to pay him was impossible, considering Apollon’s character. But of that rascal, that plague of mine, I will speak some other time.

However, I knew all the same that I would not pay him, and that I would certainly go.

That night I had the most hideous dreams. No wonder: all evening I had been oppressed by memories of the penal-servitude years of my school life, and I could not shake them off. I had been shoved into that school by distant relatives on whom I depended and of whom I have heard nothing since: shoved in, an orphan, already crushed by their reproaches, already brooding, silent, glaring wildly at everything. My schoolfellows met me with spiteful and merciless jeers because I was not like any of them. But I could not stand jeers; I could not get along with them as cheaply as they got along with each other. I hated them at once and shut myself away from everyone in a timid, wounded and boundless pride. Their coarseness revolted me. They laughed cynically at my face, at my clumsy figure; and yet what stupid faces they had themselves! In our school, faces somehow became especially stupid and degenerate. How many good-looking children came to us! A few years later it was disgusting even to look at them. Even at sixteen I wondered at them gloomily; even then I was amazed at the pettiness of their thinking, the stupidity of their occupations, their games, their conversations. They did not understand such essential things; they took no interest in such inspiring, striking subjects, that I could not help considering them beneath me. It was not wounded vanity that drove me to this, and for God’s sake do not come at me with those official objections that have become sickeningly stale: “that I was only dreaming, while they already understood real life even then.” They understood nothing, no real life at all, and I swear that was what revolted me most in them. On the contrary, they took the most obvious, glaring reality in a fantastically stupid way, and even then they had got used to worshipping nothing but success. Everything that was just, but humiliated and downtrodden, they laughed at cruelly and shamefully. They took rank for intelligence; at sixteen they were already talking about cushy jobs. Of course, much of this came from stupidity, from the bad example that had surrounded them all through their childhood and boyhood. They were depraved to the point of deformity. Of course, there was more outward show in this too, more put-on cynicism; of course, youth and a certain freshness gleamed in them even through the depravity; but even their freshness was unattractive, and showed itself in a kind of smutty buffoonery. I hated them terribly, though perhaps I was worse than they were. They paid me back in the same coin and did not hide their loathing for me. But I no longer wanted their love; on the contrary, I constantly longed for their humiliation. To rid myself of their jeers, I deliberately began to study as hard as I could and forced my way to the very top of the class. That impressed them. Besides, they all began little by little to understand that I was already reading books they could not read, and understood things (not part of our special course) of which they had never even heard. They looked at this wildly and mockingly, but they submitted morally, all the more because even the teachers paid attention to me on this account. The jeering stopped, but the hostility remained, and cold, strained relations set in. In the end I could not stand it myself: with the years a need for people, for friends, developed in me. I tried to become close to some of them; but the closeness always came out unnatural and so ended of its own accord. Once I even had a friend. But I was already a despot at heart; I wanted unlimited power over his soul; I wanted to instil in him contempt for the world around him; I demanded that he break with that world, haughtily and finally. I frightened him with my passionate friendship; I reduced him to tears, to convulsions; he was a naive and devoted soul; but when he had given himself to me entirely, I at once hated him and pushed him away, as if I had needed him only to win a victory over him, only for his submission. But I could not conquer them all; my friend too was unlike any of them and was a very rare exception. My first act on leaving school was to give up the special service for which I had been intended, so as to break all the threads, curse the past and scatter its dust... And the devil knows why, after that, I dragged myself to this Simonov!..

In the morning I leapt out of bed early, jumped up in excitement, as if it were all going to begin happening at once. But I believed that some radical turning point in my life was coming, and would certainly come that very day. Perhaps because I was not used to it, all my life, at every external event, however trivial, it always seemed to me that now some radical turning point in my life was about to come. I went to the office as usual, however, but slipped away home two hours early to get ready. The main thing, I thought, is not to arrive first, or they will think I am too delighted. But there were thousands of such main things, and they all agitated me to the point of exhaustion. I cleaned my boots once more with my own hands; Apollon would not have cleaned them twice a day for anything in the world, considering it improper. I cleaned them having stolen the brushes from the hall, so that he would not notice and despise me afterwards. Then I examined my clothes in detail and found that everything was old, threadbare and worn out. I had let myself go too much. My uniform, perhaps, was in good order, but one could hardly go to dinner in uniform. And the main thing was that on my trousers, right on the knee, there was a huge yellow stain. I had a presentiment that this stain alone would rob me of nine-tenths of my dignity. I knew too that it was very low to think like this. “But this is no time for thinking; now reality is coming,” I thought, and lost heart. I knew perfectly well, even then, that I was monstrously exaggerating all these facts; but what could I do? I could no longer control myself, and I was shaking with fever. With despair I pictured how coldly and haughtily that “scoundrel” Zverkov would receive me; with what dull, irresistible contempt that blockhead Trudolyubov would look at me; how nastily and insolently that insect Ferfichkin would snigger at my expense to curry favour with Zverkov; how perfectly Simonov would understand all this to himself, and how he would despise me for the baseness of my vanity and cowardice; and, above all, how wretched, unliterary and commonplace it would all be. Of course, it would be best of all not to go. But that was the most impossible thing of all: once I began to be drawn into something, I was drawn in completely, head first. I would have taunted myself all my life afterwards: “So, you lost your nerve, lost your nerve before reality, lost your nerve!” On the contrary, I passionately wanted to prove to all that “rabble” that I was not at all the coward I imagined myself to be. More than that: in the most violent paroxysm of cowardly fever, I dreamed of getting the upper hand, of conquering them, carrying them away, making them love me, well, “for the loftiness of my thoughts and my undoubted wit”, say. They would abandon Zverkov; he would sit to one side, silent and ashamed, and I would crush Zverkov. Then perhaps I would make it up with him and drink to our friendship; but what was most spiteful and galling to me was that I knew even then, knew fully and for certain, that I did not really need any of this, that I did not really want to crush them, conquer them or win them over at all, and that for the whole result, even if I achieved it, I myself would be the first not to give a kopeck. Oh, how I prayed to God for the day to pass quickly! In inexpressible anguish I went to the window, opened the little pane, and peered into the murky haze of the thickly falling wet snow...

At last my wretched little wall clock wheezed out five. I seized my hat and, trying not to look at Apollon, who had been waiting since morning for me to pay his wages but out of pride would not speak first, I slipped past him out of the door and, in a smart cab which I had hired on purpose with my last half-rouble, I drove up like a gentleman to the Hôtel de Paris.
