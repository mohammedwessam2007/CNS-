## CHAPTER IV

“You know, perhaps (indeed I told you myself),” Svidrigailov began, “that I was in the debtors’ prison here, for an enormous sum, and without the slightest means in view for paying it. There is no need to go into detail about how Marfa Petrovna bought me out then; do you know to what a degree of stupefaction a woman can sometimes love? She was an honest woman, far from stupid (though completely uneducated). Imagine, then, that this very jealous and honest woman resolved to condescend, after many terrible frenzies and reproaches, to a kind of contract with me, which she kept all the time of our marriage. The point is that she was considerably older than I, and besides, she always kept a clove of some sort in her mouth. I had enough swinishness in my soul, and a kind of honesty of my own, to tell her straight out that I could not be completely faithful to her. This confession drove her to frenzy, but it seems that my coarse frankness pleased her in a way: ‘So he doesn’t want to deceive me himself, since he declares it beforehand’; well, and for a jealous woman that is the first thing. After long tears, a verbal contract of the following kind was made between us: first, I would never leave Marfa Petrovna and would always remain her husband; second, I would not go away anywhere without her permission; third, I would never take a permanent mistress; fourth, in return for this, Marfa Petrovna allowed me to cast an eye on the maidservants sometimes, but not otherwise than with her secret knowledge; fifth, God forbid that I should fall in love with a woman of our own class; sixth, if by chance, which God forbid, some passion should visit me, a great and serious one, then I must reveal it to Marfa Petrovna. As regards this last point, Marfa Petrovna was, however, fairly calm all the time; she was an intelligent woman, and therefore could not look on me otherwise than as a profligate and a rake, incapable of serious love. But an intelligent woman and a jealous woman are two different things, and there lies the trouble. However, to judge certain people impartially, one must give up beforehand certain preconceived views and the everyday habit of the people and things that usually surround us. I have the right to hope for this from your judgement more than from anyone’s. Perhaps you have already heard a great deal that is funny and absurd about Marfa Petrovna. She did indeed have some very funny habits; but I will tell you straight out that I sincerely regret the countless sorrows of which I was the cause. Well, and that is enough, I think, for a very decent oraison funèbre, a funeral oration, by the tenderest husband for the tenderest wife. When we quarrelled, I was silent for the most part and did not get irritated, and this playing the gentleman almost always achieved its aim; it had an effect on her, and she even liked it; there were times when she was even proud of me. But she could not stand your sister all the same. And how did it come about that she risked taking such a beauty into her house as a governess! I explain it by Marfa Petrovna’s being a passionate and impressionable woman, and that, quite simply, she herself fell in love, literally fell in love, with your sister. Well, and Avdotya Romanovna too! I understood very well at the first glance that things were bad here, and what do you think? I resolved not even to raise my eyes to her. But Avdotya Romanovna herself made the first step: would you believe it or not? Would you believe too that Marfa Petrovna went so far as to be angry with me at first for my constant silence about your sister, for my being so indifferent to her incessant, adoring remarks about Avdotya Romanovna? I don’t understand myself what she wanted! Well, of course, Marfa Petrovna told Avdotya Romanovna the whole inside story about me. She had an unfortunate trait of telling absolutely everyone all our family secrets and complaining about me to everyone incessantly; how could she pass by such a new and beautiful friend? I suppose they talked of nothing but me, and without doubt Avdotya Romanovna came to know all those gloomy, mysterious tales that are ascribed to me... I bet you have heard something of that kind too?”

“I have. Luzhin accused you of even being the cause of a child’s death. Is it true?”

“Do me the favour of leaving all these vulgarities alone,” Svidrigailov declined with disgust and peevishly. “If you absolutely must know about all that nonsense, I will tell you some time separately, but now...”

“There was talk too of some footman of yours in the country, and that you were the cause of something there too.”

“Do me the favour, enough!” Svidrigailov interrupted again with obvious impatience.

“Isn’t that the footman who came to fill your pipe after his death... you told me about it yourself?” Raskolnikov was growing more and more irritated.

Svidrigailov looked at Raskolnikov attentively, and it seemed to the latter that a malicious smile flashed for an instant in that look, like lightning; but Svidrigailov restrained himself and answered very politely:

“That is the very one. I see that all this interests you extremely too, and I shall consider it my duty, at the first convenient opportunity, to satisfy your curiosity on every point. The devil take it! I see that I really might seem a romantic figure to someone. Judge, then, how much I am obliged after that to thank the late Marfa Petrovna for having told your sister so many mysterious and curious things about me. I dare not judge the impression; but in any case it was to my advantage. With all Avdotya Romanovna’s natural aversion to me, and in spite of my constant gloomy and repellent air, she came at last to pity me, to pity a lost man. And when a girl’s heart comes to pity, that of course is most dangerous of all for her. Then she is sure to want to ‘save’, and bring to reason, and resurrect, and call to nobler aims, and revive to a new life and activity; well, we know what can be dreamed of in that line. I grasped at once that the little bird was flying into the net of its own accord, and for my part I got ready. You seem to be frowning, Rodion Romanych? Never mind, sir; the business, as you know, came to nothing. (The devil take it, how much wine I am drinking!) You know, I have always been sorry, from the very beginning, that fate did not let your sister be born in the second or third century of our era, somewhere as the daughter of a ruling princeling or some governor or proconsul in Asia Minor. She would undoubtedly have been one of those who suffered martyrdom, and of course she would have smiled when they burned her breast with red-hot pincers. She would have gone to it on purpose herself, and in the fourth and fifth centuries she would have gone off into the Egyptian desert and lived there thirty years, feeding on roots, raptures and visions. That is all she thirsts for herself, and she demands to take some torment for someone as soon as possible, and if you don’t give her this torment, she might jump out of a window. I have heard something about a certain Mr Razumikhin. He is a sensible fellow, they say (as his name shows; a seminarist, I suppose), so let him take care of your sister. In a word, I think I have understood her, and I count that an honour to myself. But then, that is, at the beginning of our acquaintance, you know yourself, one is always somehow more frivolous and more stupid, one looks wrongly, one sees the wrong thing. The devil take it, why is she so beautiful? It’s not my fault! In a word, with me it began with the most irresistible sensual impulse. Avdotya Romanovna is chaste, terribly, unheard-of and unseen chaste. (Note that I am telling you this about your sister as a fact. She is chaste, perhaps to the point of illness, in spite of all her broad mind, and it will harm her.) There was a girl with us then, Parasha, black-eyed Parasha, who had just been brought from another village, a maidservant, whom I had never seen before; very pretty, but incredibly stupid: she burst into tears, raised a howl through the whole yard, and there was a scandal. Once, after dinner, Avdotya Romanovna deliberately sought me out alone in an avenue in the garden and with flashing eyes demanded that I leave poor Parasha in peace. That was almost our first conversation alone together. Of course I considered it an honour to satisfy her wish, tried to pretend to be struck, embarrassed; well, in a word, I played the part not badly. Relations began, mysterious conversations, moral lessons, instructions, entreaties, supplications, even tears; would you believe it, even tears! That is how strong the passion for propaganda becomes in some girls! I, of course, laid everything on my fate, pretended to be hungering and thirsting for light, and at last brought into play the greatest and most unshakeable means of conquering a woman’s heart, a means that never deceives anyone and acts decisively on every one of them, without any exception. That means is well known: flattery. There is nothing in the world harder than straightforwardness, and nothing easier than flattery. If there is only a hundredth part of a false note in straightforwardness, a dissonance occurs at once, and after it a scandal. But if in flattery everything, down to the last note, is false, even then it is pleasant and is listened to not without pleasure; with a coarse pleasure, perhaps, but still with pleasure. And however coarse the flattery, at least half of it is sure to seem true. And this is so for all levels of development and all strata of society. Even a vestal virgin can be seduced by flattery. And about ordinary people there is nothing to say. I can’t remember without laughing how I once seduced a lady devoted to her husband, her children and her virtues. How merry it was, and how little work it took! And the lady really was virtuous, at least in her own way. My whole tactic consisted in simply being crushed every minute and falling prostrate before her chastity. I flattered shamelessly, and as soon as I managed to get a pressure of the hand, even a glance, I would reproach myself for having wrested it from her by force, that she had resisted, that she had resisted so much that I would certainly never have got anything if I had not been so depraved myself; that she, in her innocence, had not foreseen the treachery and had yielded unintentionally, without knowing it herself, without being aware, and so on and so on. In a word, I achieved everything, and my lady remained in the highest degree convinced that she was innocent and chaste and fulfilled all her duties and obligations, and had fallen quite by accident. And how angry she was with me when I told her in the end that, in my sincere conviction, she had been seeking pleasure exactly as much as I. Poor Marfa Petrovna too was terribly susceptible to flattery, and if only I had wanted, I could certainly have had her whole estate made over to me in her lifetime. (But I am drinking a terrible lot of wine and chattering.) I hope you won’t be angry if I mention now that the same effect began to come about with Avdotya Romanovna too. But I was stupid and impatient myself and spoiled the whole thing. Several times before (and once somehow especially) Avdotya Romanovna had terribly disliked the expression of my eyes; would you believe that? In a word, a certain fire flared up in them more and more strongly and incautiously, which frightened her and at last became hateful to her. There is no need to tell the details, but we parted. Then I acted stupidly again. I began jeering in the coarsest way at all these propagandas and conversions; Parasha came on the scene again, and not she alone; in a word, Sodom began. Oh, if you had seen, Rodion Romanych, even once in your life, your sister’s eyes as they sometimes know how to flash! It doesn’t matter that I am drunk now and have drunk a whole glass of wine already; I am telling the truth; I assure you that I dreamed of that look; at last I could not bear even the rustle of her dress. Really, I thought I would have a fit of the falling sickness; I never imagined I could come to such a frenzy. In a word, it was necessary to make peace; but that was already impossible. And imagine what I did then? To what a degree of stupidity fury can bring a man! Never undertake anything in fury, Rodion Romanych. Reckoning that Avdotya Romanovna was, after all, essentially a beggar (ah, forgive me, I didn’t mean that... but isn’t it all the same, if the same idea is expressed?), in a word, that she lived by the work of her hands, that she was supporting both her mother and you (ah, the devil, you are frowning again...), I resolved to offer her all my money (I could have raised up to thirty thousand even then) on condition that she run away with me, even here to Petersburg. Of course I would have sworn eternal love, bliss, and so on and so on. Would you believe it, I was so smitten then that if she had said to me: cut Marfa Petrovna’s throat or poison her and marry me, it would have been done at once! But everything ended in the catastrophe you already know of, and you can judge for yourself to what fury I could come on learning that Marfa Petrovna had got hold of that vilest of pettifoggers, Luzhin, and had almost put together a marriage, which essentially would have been the same thing as I had proposed. Isn’t that so? Isn’t that so? It is, isn’t it? I notice you have begun listening very attentively... an interesting young man...”

Svidrigailov struck the table with his fist in impatience. He had turned red. Raskolnikov saw clearly that the glass or glass and a half of champagne which he had drunk, sipping imperceptibly, had had a morbid effect on him, and he resolved to take advantage of the opportunity. Svidrigailov was very suspicious to him.

“Well, after this I am fully convinced that you came here too with my sister in view,” he said to Svidrigailov directly and without concealment, so as to irritate him still more.

“Eh, come now,” Svidrigailov seemed suddenly to check himself. “I told you... and besides, your sister can’t bear me.”

“Yes, I am convinced of that too, that she can’t; but that is not the point now.”

“And you are convinced that she can’t?” (Svidrigailov narrowed his eyes and smiled mockingly.) “You are right, she doesn’t love me; but never vouch for the things that have passed between husband and wife or lover and mistress. There is always one little corner there that remains unknown to the whole world and is known only to the two of them. Do you vouch that Avdotya Romanovna looked on me with aversion?”

“From certain words and little words of yours during your story I notice that you have your own designs even now, and the most urgent intentions, on Dunya; vile ones, of course.”

“What! Such words and little words slipped out of me?” Svidrigailov suddenly took fright most naively, not paying the slightest attention to the epithet applied to his intentions.

“They are slipping out even now. Well, what are you so afraid of, for instance? What have you suddenly taken fright at now?”

“I am afraid and take fright? Take fright at you? It is rather for you to be afraid of me, cher ami, my dear friend. And what nonsense this is, though... But then, I have got tipsy, I see it; I nearly let something slip again. To the devil with wine! Hey, water!”

He seized the bottle and without ceremony flung it out of the window. Philip brought water.

“All this is nonsense,” said Svidrigailov, wetting a towel and putting it to his head, “but I can check you with one word and reduce all your suspicions to dust. Do you know, for instance, that I am getting married?”

“You told me that before too.”

“Did I? I forgot. But then I couldn’t speak positively, because I hadn’t even seen the bride yet; I only intended it. Well, but now I have a bride, and the thing is done, and if it were not for business, urgent business, I would certainly take you and drive you to them now, because I want to ask your advice. Hey, the devil! Only ten minutes left. You see, look at the clock; but then, I will tell you, because it is an interesting little thing, my marriage, in its way, that is; where are you going? Leaving again?”

“No, I won’t leave now.”

“Won’t leave at all? We shall see! I will take you there, that is true, and show you the bride, but not now; now it will soon be time for you to go. You go right, I go left. Do you know this Resslich? This same Resslich in whose place I am living now, eh? Do you hear? No, what do you think, she is the very one about whom they say that the girl, in the water, in winter... well, do you hear? Do you hear? Well, she cooked all this up for me; ‘you are so bored,’ she says, ‘amuse yourself for a while.’ And I am a gloomy, boring man, you know. You think I am merry? No, gloomy: I do no harm, but sit in a corner; sometimes no one can get a word out of me for three days. And this Resslich, the hussy, I tell you, has this in mind: I shall get bored, abandon my wife and go away, and the wife will fall to her, and she will put her into circulation; in our class, that is, and higher. There is, she says, a certain paralysed father, a retired official; he sits in an armchair and hasn’t moved his legs for two years. There is a mother too, she says, a sensible lady, the mama. A son is serving somewhere in the provinces and doesn’t help. A daughter has married and doesn’t visit, and they have two little nephews on their hands (as if their own were not enough), and they have taken their youngest daughter out of the gymnasium before she finished the course; in a month she will just turn sixteen, so in a month she can be married off. To me, that is. We went; how funny it is with them; I introduce myself: a landowner, a widower, of a well-known family, with such and such connections, with capital; well, what of it that I am fifty and she isn’t even sixteen? Who looks at that? Well, but it is tempting, isn’t it? It is tempting, ha-ha! You should have seen how I got talking with papa and mama! It would be worth paying just to see me at that moment. She comes out, curtseys; well, you can imagine, still in a short little dress, an unopened little bud, she blushes, flushes like the dawn (they had told her, of course). I don’t know what you think about women’s faces, but in my opinion those sixteen years, those still childish little eyes, that timidity and those little tears of bashfulness, in my opinion that is better than beauty, and she is pretty as a picture besides. Fair little hairs, fluffed up in little curls like a lamb’s, plump little scarlet lips, little feet, a delight!.. Well, we were introduced; I announced that I was in a hurry on account of family circumstances, and the very next day, that is, the day before yesterday, we were given their blessing. Since then, as soon as I arrive, I take her on my knees at once and don’t let her down... Well, she flushes like the dawn, and I kiss her every minute; mama, of course, impresses on her that this is your husband and this is how it must be; in a word, raspberries! And this present state of being a bridegroom is perhaps even better than being a husband. Here is what they call la nature et la vérité, nature and truth! Ha-ha! I have talked with her a couple of times; the girl is far from stupid; sometimes she gives me such a stealthy glance that it simply burns. And you know, her little face is like Raphael’s Madonna. The Sistine Madonna has a fantastic face, the face of a sorrowing holy fool; hasn’t that struck you? Well, something in that style. As soon as we had been blessed, the next day I brought fifteen hundred roubles’ worth: a set of diamonds, another of pearls, and a lady’s silver dressing case, this big, with all sorts of things, so that even she, the Madonna, flushed in her little face. I took her on my knees yesterday, and rather too unceremoniously, I suppose: she flushed all over and little tears spurted out, but she doesn’t want to show it, and she is all aflame herself. Everyone went out for a minute, we were left quite alone, and suddenly she throws herself on my neck (for the first time herself), embraces me with both little arms, kisses me and swears that she will be an obedient, faithful and good wife to me, that she will make me happy, that she will devote her whole life, every minute of her life, she will sacrifice everything, everything, and for all this she wishes to have from me only my respect, and ‘I need nothing more,’ she says, ‘nothing, nothing, no presents!’ You will agree yourself that to hear such a confession alone from such a sixteen-year-old little angel, in a tulle dress, with fluffed-up little curls, with the blush of girlish shame and little tears of enthusiasm in her eyes, you will agree yourself, it is rather tempting. It is tempting, isn’t it? It is worth something, eh? Well, it is worth it, isn’t it? Well... well, listen... well, let’s go to my bride... only not now!”

“In a word, it is this monstrous difference in age and development that arouses your lust! And are you really going to marry like this?”

“Why not? Certainly. Everyone looks after himself, and he lives merriest of all who knows best how to deceive himself. Ha-ha! But why have you driven into virtue with the whole shaft like that? Spare me, my dear fellow, I am a sinful man. He-he-he!”

“You did, however, provide for Katerina Ivanovna’s children. But then... but then you had your reasons for that... I understand everything now.”

“I love children in general; I am very fond of children,” Svidrigailov laughed loudly. “On that score I can even tell you a most curious episode, which is still going on. On the very first day after my arrival I went round to various of these sewers; well, after seven years I simply flung myself on them. You probably notice that I am in no hurry to get together with my own company, with my former friends and acquaintances. Well, and I shall hold out without them as long as possible. You know: in the country at Marfa Petrovna’s I was tormented to death by memories of all these mysterious places and little places, in which, if you know, you can find a lot. The devil take it! The people drink, the educated young burn out from inaction in impossible dreams and fancies, they deform themselves in theories; Jews have come from somewhere and hide money, and all the rest are debauched. From the first hours this city breathed on me with a familiar smell. I happened on a so-called dancing evening, a terrible sewer (and I love sewers with a little dirt in them, precisely), well, of course, a cancan such as doesn’t exist anywhere and never existed in my time. Yes, sir, there is progress in that. Suddenly I look, a girl of about thirteen, very prettily dressed, is dancing with a certain virtuoso; another is facing her, vis-à-vis. Her mother is sitting on a chair against the wall. Well, you can imagine what a cancan it was! The girl is embarrassed, blushes, at last takes offence and begins to cry. The virtuoso picks her up and begins to whirl her round and perform in front of her; everyone around laughs, and (I love our public at such moments, even a cancan public) they laugh and shout: ‘Serves her right, that’s how it should be! Don’t bring children!’ Well, I don’t care a straw, and it’s none of my business whether they console themselves logically or not! I at once marked out my place, sat down by the mother and began saying that I too was new to the place, what boors they all were here, that they didn’t know how to distinguish true merit and to show due respect; I let it be known that I had a lot of money; I invited her to let me drive them home in my carriage; drove them home, got acquainted (they were living in some little closet, sublet from lodgers; they had only just arrived). I was told that she and her daughter could only regard my acquaintance as an honour; I learn that they have neither stick nor stone, and have come to petition about something in some government office; I offer my services, money; I learn that they went to the evening by mistake, thinking that dancing really was taught there; I offer for my part to help with the education of the young lady, in French and dancing. They accept with rapture, consider it an honour, and I still know them... If you like, we’ll go, only not now.”

“Stop it, stop your vile, low anecdotes, you depraved, low, lustful man!”

“Our Schiller, our Schiller, our Schiller! Où va-t-elle la vertu se nicher? Where will virtue make its nest next? And you know, I will tell you such things on purpose, to hear your cries. A delight!”

“I should think so; am I not ridiculous to myself at this moment?” Raskolnikov muttered angrily.

Svidrigailov laughed at the top of his voice; at last he called Philip, paid and began to get up.

“Well, how drunk I am, assez causé, enough talk!” he said. “A delight!”

“Of course you feel delight,” cried Raskolnikov, getting up too. “Isn’t it a delight for a worn-out profligate to tell of such adventures, with some monstrous intention of the same kind in view, and in such circumstances, and to such a man as I... It inflames you.”

“Well, if so,” Svidrigailov answered even with some surprise, examining Raskolnikov, “if so, then you are a decent cynic yourself. At least you contain an enormous amount of material. You can be aware of much, much... and you can do much too. Well, but that’s enough. I sincerely regret that I have talked with you so little, but you won’t get away from me... Just wait...”

Svidrigailov went out of the tavern. Raskolnikov followed him. Svidrigailov, however, was not very drunk; it had gone to his head only for a moment, and the tipsiness was passing with every minute. He was very preoccupied with something, something extremely important, and he was frowning. Some expectation was evidently agitating and troubling him. Towards Raskolnikov he had somehow suddenly changed in the last few minutes, and with every minute he was becoming coarser and more mocking. Raskolnikov noticed all this and was alarmed too. Svidrigailov had become very suspicious to him; he resolved to follow him.

They came out onto the pavement.

“You go right and I go left, or perhaps the other way round, only... adieu, mon plaisir, farewell, my joy, until our happy next meeting!”

And he went to the right, towards the Haymarket.
