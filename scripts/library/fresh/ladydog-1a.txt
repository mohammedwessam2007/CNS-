## I

People were saying that a new face had appeared on the seafront: a lady with a little dog. Dmitry Dmitrich Gurov, who had been in Yalta for two weeks now and had got used to it, had also begun to take an interest in new faces. Sitting in the pavilion at Vernet’s, he saw a young lady walk along the seafront, a short blonde in a beret; a white Pomeranian trotted after her.

And after that he met her in the public garden and in the square several times a day. She walked alone, always in the same beret, with the white Pomeranian; nobody knew who she was, and people called her simply the lady with the little dog.

“If she is here without her husband and without friends,” Gurov reasoned, “it wouldn’t be a bad idea to get to know her.”

He was not yet forty, but he already had a daughter of twelve and two sons at school. He had been married off young, when he was still a second-year student, and now his wife seemed half as old again as he was. She was a tall woman with dark eyebrows, upright, dignified, serious and, as she called herself, a thinking woman. She read a great deal, used the new spelling in her letters, called her husband not Dmitry but the more old-fashioned Dimitry; and he secretly considered her limited, narrow and inelegant, was afraid of her, and did not like being at home. He had begun deceiving her long ago, deceived her often, and probably for that reason almost always spoke badly of women, and when they were discussed in his presence, he would call them:

“The lower race!”

It seemed to him that he had been taught enough by bitter experience to call them whatever he liked, and yet he could not have lived for two days without the “lower race”. In the company of men he was bored and ill at ease; with them he was unsociable and cold; but when he was among women he felt free, and knew what to talk about with them and how to behave; and even being silent with them was easy for him. In his appearance, in his character, in his whole nature there was something attractive and elusive that drew women to him and lured them; he knew it, and some force drew him to them too.

Repeated experience, bitter experience indeed, had taught him long ago that every intimacy, which at first adds such pleasant variety to life and seems a sweet and easy adventure, inevitably grows, among decent people, and especially among Muscovites, who are slow to move and indecisive, into a whole problem, extremely complicated, and in the end the situation becomes a burden. But at every new meeting with an interesting woman this experience somehow slipped from his memory, and he wanted to live, and everything seemed so simple and amusing.

And so one evening he was dining in the garden, and the lady in the beret came up slowly to take the next table. Her expression, her walk, her dress, her hair told him that she was from good society, married, in Yalta for the first time and alone, and that she was bored here... There is a lot of untruth in the stories about the loose morals of the place; he despised them and knew that such stories were mostly made up by people who would gladly sin themselves if they knew how. But when the lady sat down at the next table, three steps away from him, he remembered those stories of easy conquests, of trips to the mountains, and the tempting thought of a quick, fleeting affair, a romance with an unknown woman whose name and surname you do not know, suddenly took hold of him.

He beckoned the Pomeranian to him affectionately, and when it came, he shook his finger at it. The Pomeranian growled. Gurov shook his finger again.

The lady glanced at him and at once lowered her eyes.

“He doesn’t bite,” she said, and blushed.

“May I give him a bone?” And when she nodded, he asked pleasantly: “Have you been in Yalta long?”

“About five days.”

“And I’m already dragging out my second week here.”

They were silent for a while.

“Time passes quickly, and yet it is so boring here!” she said, not looking at him.

“It’s only the done thing to say it’s boring here. A man lives in Belyov or Zhizdra, some little town, and he isn’t bored; but he comes here and it’s ‘Oh, how boring! Oh, the dust!’ You’d think he had come from Granada.”

She laughed. Then they both went on eating in silence, like strangers; but after dinner they walked off together, and there began the light, joking conversation of people who are free and contented, to whom it makes no difference where they go or what they talk about. They walked and talked about how strangely lit the sea was; the water was a lilac colour, so soft and warm, and across it the moon laid a strip of gold. They talked about how close it was after the hot day. Gurov told her he was a Muscovite, a philologist by training, but worked in a bank; that he had once prepared to sing in a private opera company, but had given it up; that he owned two houses in Moscow... And from her he learned that she had grown up in Petersburg, but had married in the town of S., where she had lived for two years now; that she would stay in Yalta for another month or so, and that her husband, who also wanted a rest, might come to fetch her. She could not explain at all where her husband worked, at the provincial board or at the district council, and she herself found this funny. And Gurov learned too that her name was Anna Sergeyevna.

Afterwards, in his hotel room, he thought about her, about how tomorrow she would surely meet him. It was bound to be so. Going to bed, he remembered that only a short time ago she had been a schoolgirl, studying, just as his daughter was now; he remembered how much shyness and awkwardness there still was in her laugh, in her talk with a stranger; this was probably the first time in her life that she had been alone, in a setting where men followed her and looked at her and talked to her with only one secret aim, which she could not help guessing. He remembered her thin, weak neck, her beautiful grey eyes.

“All the same, there’s something pitiful about her,” he thought, and fell asleep.

## II

A week had passed since they met. It was a holiday. It was stuffy indoors, while in the streets the dust whirled about and blew people’s hats off. All day one was thirsty, and Gurov kept going into the pavilion and offering Anna Sergeyevna now fruit water, now ice cream. There was nowhere to escape to.

In the evening, when the wind had dropped a little, they went out on the pier to watch the steamer come in. There were many people strolling on the landing stage; they had gathered to meet someone, and held bouquets. And two peculiarities of the well-dressed Yalta crowd stood out clearly: the elderly ladies were dressed like young ones, and there were a great many generals.

Because of the rough sea the steamer arrived late, after the sun had set, and turned about for a long time before it came alongside the pier. Anna Sergeyevna looked at the steamer and the passengers through her lorgnette, as if looking for someone she knew, and when she turned to Gurov her eyes shone. She talked a great deal, her questions were abrupt, and she immediately forgot what she had asked; then she lost her lorgnette in the crowd.

The well-dressed crowd dispersed, faces could no longer be seen, the wind had died down completely, and Gurov and Anna Sergeyevna stood as though waiting to see whether anyone else would come off the steamer. Anna Sergeyevna was silent now, smelling her flowers without looking at Gurov.

“The weather has got better towards evening,” he said. “Where shall we go now? Shall we drive somewhere?”

She made no answer.

Then he looked at her intently, and suddenly embraced her and kissed her on the lips, and he was enveloped in the fragrance and moisture of the flowers; and at once he looked around fearfully: had anyone seen?

“Let’s go to your room...” he said softly.

And they both walked off quickly.

It was stuffy in her room, and it smelled of the scent she had bought at the Japanese shop. Looking at her now, Gurov thought: “What meetings one has in life!” From the past he had kept memories of carefree, good-natured women, cheerful with love, grateful to him for their happiness, however brief; and of women, like his wife, who loved insincerely, with too much talk, affectedly, hysterically, with an expression as if this were not love or passion but something more significant; and of two or three, very beautiful and cold, across whose faces there would suddenly flash a predatory expression, a stubborn desire to take, to snatch from life more than it could give; and these were no longer young, capricious, unreasoning, domineering, unintelligent women, and when Gurov grew cold to them, their beauty roused hatred in him, and the lace on their underclothes seemed to him then like fish scales.

But here there was still the same shyness, the awkwardness of inexperienced youth, an uneasy feeling; and there was an impression of confusion, as if someone had suddenly knocked at the door. Anna Sergeyevna, this “lady with the little dog”, took what had happened in a special way, very seriously, as if it were her fall: so it seemed, and it was strange and out of place. Her features drooped and faded, and her long hair hung sadly on both sides of her face; she sat lost in thought in a dejected pose, like a sinful woman in an old painting.

“It’s wrong,” she said. “Now you will be the first to stop respecting me.”

There was a watermelon on the table in the room. Gurov cut himself a slice and began to eat it unhurriedly. At least half an hour passed in silence.

Anna Sergeyevna was touching; there was about her the purity of a decent, naive woman who has lived very little; the single candle burning on the table barely lit her face, but one could see that she was unhappy at heart.

“Why should I stop respecting you?” asked Gurov. “You don’t know what you are saying.”

“May God forgive me!” she said, and her eyes filled with tears. “It’s terrible.”

“You seem to be justifying yourself.”

“How can I justify myself? I am a bad, low woman; I despise myself, and I don’t even think of justifying myself. It isn’t my husband I have deceived, but myself. And not just now; I have been deceiving myself for a long time. My husband may be an honest, good man, but he is a lackey! I don’t know what he does there, what his work is; I only know that he is a lackey. I was twenty when I married him; I was tormented by curiosity; I wanted something better. There must be, I told myself, another kind of life. I wanted to live! To live and live... Curiosity burned me... you don’t understand this, but I swear to God, I could no longer control myself; something was happening to me; I could not be held back; I told my husband I was ill, and came here... And here I kept walking about as if in a daze, as if I were mad... and now I have become a vulgar, worthless woman whom anyone may despise.”

Gurov was already bored listening; he was irritated by her naive tone, by this repentance, so unexpected and out of place; if it had not been for the tears in her eyes, one might have thought she was joking or playing a part.

“I don’t understand,” he said quietly. “What is it you want?”

She hid her face on his chest and pressed close to him.

“Believe me, believe me, I beg you...” she said. “I love an honest, pure life, and sin is hateful to me; I don’t know myself what I am doing. Simple people say, ‘The Evil One led me astray.’ And now I can say of myself that the Evil One has led me astray.”

“Hush, hush...” he murmured.

He looked into her fixed, frightened eyes, kissed her, spoke softly and tenderly, and little by little she calmed down, and her cheerfulness came back; they both began to laugh.

Later, when they went out, there was not a soul on the seafront; the town with its cypresses looked quite dead, but the sea was still roaring and breaking on the shore; a single boat rocked on the waves, and on it a lantern flickered sleepily.

They found a cab and drove to Oreanda.

“I found out your surname just now, downstairs in the hall: the board says von Diderits,” said Gurov. “Is your husband German?”

“No, I think his grandfather was German, but he himself is Orthodox.”

At Oreanda they sat on a bench not far from the church, looked down at the sea, and were silent. Yalta was barely visible through the morning mist; white clouds stood motionless on the mountain tops. The leaves did not stir on the trees, the cicadas were calling, and the monotonous, muffled sound of the sea, rising from below, spoke of peace, of the eternal sleep that awaits us. So it had sounded below when there was neither Yalta nor Oreanda here; so it sounds now, and so it will go on sounding, just as indifferently and dully, when we are no more. And in this constancy, in this complete indifference to the life and death of each of us, there lies hidden, perhaps, the pledge of our eternal salvation, of the unceasing movement of life on earth, of unceasing perfection. Sitting beside a young woman who at dawn seemed so beautiful, soothed and enchanted by this fairy-tale setting, the sea, the mountains, the clouds, the wide sky, Gurov thought how, when you really think about it, everything in this world is beautiful, everything except what we ourselves think and do when we forget the higher aims of existence and our own human dignity.

A man came up, a watchman probably, looked at them and went away. And this detail too seemed mysterious and beautiful. They could see the steamer from Feodosia come in, lit by the dawn, its lights already out.

“There is dew on the grass,” said Anna Sergeyevna, after a silence.

“Yes. It’s time to go home.”

They went back to the town.

After that they met every day at noon on the seafront, lunched together, dined, walked, admired the sea. She complained that she slept badly and that her heart beat anxiously; she kept asking the same questions, troubled now by jealousy, now by fear that he did not respect her enough. And often in the square or the garden, when there was no one near them, he would suddenly draw her to him and kiss her passionately. Complete idleness, these kisses in broad daylight with glances over the shoulder and fear that someone might see, the heat, the smell of the sea and the constant passing before his eyes of idle, well-dressed, well-fed people, seemed to have made him a new man; he told Anna Sergeyevna how beautiful she was, how seductive; he was impatiently passionate and would not leave her side for a moment; while she was often lost in thought and kept asking him to admit that he did not respect her, did not love her at all, and saw in her only a vulgar woman. Almost every evening, rather late, they drove out of town somewhere, to Oreanda or to the waterfall; and the outing was always a success; their impressions were invariably beautiful and majestic.

They were expecting her husband to come. But a letter came from him, in which he said that his eyes were bad and begged his wife to come home as soon as possible. Anna Sergeyevna hurried to leave.

“It’s good that I’m going,” she said to Gurov. “It is fate itself.”

She went by carriage, and he went with her to see her off. They drove all day. When she was getting into the carriage of the express train and the second bell had rung, she said:

“Let me look at you once more... One more look. There.”

She did not cry, but she was sad, as if she were ill, and her face trembled.

“I shall think of you... remember you,” she said. “God be with you; stay here. Don’t think badly of me. We are saying goodbye for ever; it must be so, because we should never have met. Well, God be with you.”

The train left quickly; its lights soon vanished, and a minute later its noise could no longer be heard, as if everything had conspired on purpose to put a quick end to this sweet oblivion, this madness. And left alone on the platform, looking into the dark distance, Gurov listened to the chirring of the grasshoppers and the humming of the telegraph wires with a feeling as if he had only just woken up. And he thought that here was one more affair or adventure in his life, and it too was over, and only a memory remained... He was moved, sad, and felt a slight remorse; after all, this young woman whom he would never see again had not been happy with him; he had been friendly and warm to her, but all the same, in his manner with her, in his tone and his caresses, there had shown, like a shadow, a light mockery, the somewhat coarse condescension of a successful man who was, besides, almost twice her age. All the time she had called him kind, remarkable, high-minded; evidently he had seemed to her other than he really was, and so he had been deceiving her without meaning to...

Here at the station there was already a smell of autumn; the evening was cool.

“It’s time for me to go north too,” thought Gurov, leaving the platform. “It’s time!”
