## Part Five · Chapter XXV

When Alexei Alexandrovich came into Countess Lidia Ivanovna’s cosy little study, filled with old china and hung with portraits, the hostess herself was not there yet. She was changing her dress.

A cloth had been laid on the round table, and on it stood a Chinese tea service and a silver spirit-kettle. Alexei Alexandrovich glanced absently at the countless familiar portraits that decorated the study and, sitting down at the table, opened the Gospel that lay on it. The rustle of the countess’s silk dress distracted him.

“Well, now we can sit in peace,” said Countess Lidia Ivanovna, with an agitated smile, squeezing hurriedly between the table and the sofa, “and talk over our tea.”

After a few preparatory words, Countess Lidia Ivanovna, breathing heavily and blushing, put into Alexei Alexandrovich’s hands the letter she had received.

When he had read the letter, he was silent for a long time.

“I do not suppose I have the right to refuse her,” he said timidly, raising his eyes.

“My friend! You see evil in no one!”

“On the contrary, I see that everything is evil. But is it just?…”

In his face there was indecision, and a search for advice, support and guidance in a matter he did not understand.

“No,” Countess Lidia Ivanovna interrupted him. “There is a limit to everything. I understand immorality,” she said, not quite sincerely, for she had never been able to understand what leads women into immorality; “but I do not understand cruelty, and towards whom? Towards you! How can she stay in the town where you are? No, live and learn. And I am learning to understand your loftiness and her baseness.”

“And who shall cast the stone?” said Alexei Alexandrovich, evidently pleased with his part. “I have forgiven everything, and so I cannot deprive her of what is a need of love for her: love for her son…”

“But is it love, my friend? Is it sincere? Suppose you have forgiven, you forgive… but have we the right to act on the soul of that angel? He thinks of her as dead. He prays for her and asks God to forgive her sins… And it is better so. And what will he think now?”

“I had not thought of that,” said Alexei Alexandrovich, evidently agreeing.

Countess Lidia Ivanovna covered her face with her hands and was silent for a while. She was praying.

“If you ask my advice,” she said, having prayed and uncovering her face, “I do not advise you to do it. Do I not see how you suffer, how this has opened all your wounds? But suppose that you, as always, forget about yourself. What can it lead to? To new sufferings on your part, to torment for the child? If there is anything human left in her, she herself ought not to wish for it. No, I do not hesitate to advise against it, and, if you allow me, I will write to her.”

And Alexei Alexandrovich consented, and Countess Lidia Ivanovna wrote the following letter in French:

“Madam,

The remembrance of you may lead your son to questions which cannot be answered without putting into the child’s soul a spirit of condemnation of what ought to be sacred to him, and so I beg you to understand your husband’s refusal in the spirit of Christian love. I pray the Most High to have mercy on you.

Countess Lidia.”

This letter achieved the hidden aim which Countess Lidia Ivanovna concealed from herself. It wounded Anna to the depths of her soul.

For his part, Alexei Alexandrovich, coming home from Lidia Ivanovna’s, could not that day give himself up to his usual occupations and find that spiritual peace of a believing and saved man which he had felt before.

The memory of his wife, who was so very guilty before him and before whom he was so holy, as Countess Lidia Ivanovna had justly told him, ought not to have troubled him; but he was not at peace: he could not understand the book he was reading, could not drive away tormenting memories of his relations with her, of the mistakes which, as it now seemed to him, he had made with regard to her. The memory of how, coming back from the races, he had received her confession of unfaithfulness (and especially that he had demanded of her only outward propriety, and had not challenged the man to a duel) tormented him like remorse. He was tormented too by the memory of the letter he had written her; and above all his forgiveness, which no one needed, and his care of another man’s child burned his heart with shame and remorse.

And exactly the same feeling of shame and remorse he felt now, going over all his past with her and remembering the awkward words in which, after long hesitation, he had proposed to her.

“But what am I to blame for?” he said to himself. And this question always called up in him another: whether these other people, these Vronskys, Oblonskys… these gentlemen of the bedchamber with fat calves, feel differently, love differently, marry differently. And there passed before him a whole row of these juicy, strong, unhesitating people, who always and everywhere drew his curious attention in spite of himself. He drove these thoughts away; he tried to convince himself that he was living not for this temporal life but for the eternal one, that there was peace and love in his soul. But that in this temporal, worthless life he had made, as it seemed to him, a few worthless mistakes tormented him as though there were no eternal salvation in which he believed. But this temptation did not last long, and soon there was restored in Alexei Alexandrovich’s soul that calm and that loftiness thanks to which he could forget what he did not want to remember.

## Chapter XXVI

“Well, Kapitonych?” said Seryozha, rosy and cheerful, coming back from his walk on the eve of his birthday and handing his gathered coat to the tall old hall porter, who smiled down at the little person from the height of his stature. “Has the clerk with the bandage been today? Did papa see him?”

“He did. The head of chancery had only just come out, and I announced him,” said the porter, winking cheerfully. “Allow me, I’ll take it off.”

“Seryozha!” said the tutor, a Slav, stopping in the doorway that led to the inner rooms. “Take it off yourself.”

But Seryozha, though he heard the tutor’s feeble voice, paid no attention to it. He stood holding the porter’s shoulder-belt with his hand and looking into his face.

“Well, and did papa do what he needed for him?”

The porter nodded his head in assent.

The clerk with the bandaged face, who had already been seven times to ask Alexei Alexandrovich for something, interested both Seryozha and the porter. Seryozha had once found him in the hall and heard him pitifully begging the porter to announce him, saying that he and his children had nothing left but to die.

Since then Seryozha, meeting the clerk in the hall another time, had taken an interest in him.

“Well, was he very glad?” he asked.

“Glad? I should think so! He went off from here almost jumping.”

“And has anything been brought?” asked Seryozha, after a silence.

“Well, sir,” said the porter in a whisper, shaking his head, “there is something from the countess.”

Seryozha understood at once that what the porter was talking about was a present from Countess Lidia Ivanovna for his birthday.

“What do you mean? Where?”

“Korney took it in to your papa. A fine thing it must be!”

“How big? As big as this?”

“Smaller, but fine.”

“A book?”

“No, a thing. Go along, go along, Vasily Lukich is calling,” said the porter, hearing the tutor’s steps approaching, and carefully unclasping the little hand in its half-pulled-off glove that was holding him by the belt; and, winking, he nodded his head towards Vunich.

“Vasily Lukich, in one little minute!” answered Seryozha, with that gay and loving smile which always won over the conscientious Vasily Lukich.

Seryozha was too happy, everything was too joyful, for him not to share with his friend the porter yet another family joy, which he had learned of on his walk in the Summer Garden from Countess Lidia Ivanovna’s niece. This joy seemed especially important to him because it coincided with the clerk’s joy and with his own joy that toys had been brought. It seemed to Seryozha that today was a day on which everyone ought to be glad and happy.

“Do you know, papa has got the Alexander Nevsky?”

“Of course I know! People have already come to congratulate him.”

“Well, and is he glad?”

“How could one not be glad of the Tsar’s favour! It means he has deserved it,” said the porter sternly and seriously.

Seryozha grew thoughtful, peering into the porter’s face, which he had studied to the smallest detail, especially the chin hanging between the grey side-whiskers, which no one ever saw but Seryozha, who always looked at it from below.

“Well, and has your daughter been to see you lately?”

The porter’s daughter was a ballet dancer.

“When could she come on weekdays? They have their lessons too. And you have your lessons, sir; go along.”

When he came into the room, Seryozha, instead of sitting down to his lessons, told his teacher his guess that what had been brought must be a machine. “What do you think?” he asked.

But Vasily Lukich was thinking only that he had to prepare the grammar lesson for the teacher who was coming at two.

“No, only tell me, Vasily Lukich,” he asked suddenly, sitting at his work-table with the book in his hands, “what is higher than the Alexander Nevsky? You know papa has got the Alexander Nevsky?”

Vasily Lukich answered that the Vladimir was higher than the Alexander Nevsky.

“And higher?”

“And highest of all is the Andrei Pervozvanny.”

“And higher still than the Andrei?”

“I don’t know.”

“What, even you don’t know?” And Seryozha, leaning his elbows on his hands, sank into reflection.

His reflections were most complicated and varied. He imagined how his father would suddenly receive both the Vladimir and the Andrei, and how as a result he would be much kinder at his lesson today, and how he himself, when he was grown up, would receive all the orders, and whatever they invented higher than the Andrei. As soon as they invented it, he would deserve it. They would invent one higher still, and he would deserve it at once.

The time passed in such reflections, and when the teacher came, the lesson on adverbial modifiers of time and place and manner of action had not been prepared, and the teacher was not only displeased but grieved. The teacher’s grief touched Seryozha. He felt that he was not to blame for not having learned the lesson; but however hard he tried, he decidedly could not do it: while the teacher was explaining to him, he believed it and seemed to understand, but as soon as he was left alone he decidedly could not remember or understand that the short and so understandable word “suddenly” is an adverbial modifier of manner of action. But still he was sorry he had grieved the teacher, and he wanted to comfort him.

He chose a moment when the teacher was looking silently into the book.

“Mikhail Ivanych, when is your name-day?” he asked suddenly.

“You had better think about your work; name-days have no significance for a rational being. It is a day like any other, on which one must work.”

Seryozha looked attentively at the teacher, at his thin little beard, at his spectacles, which had slipped down below the mark on his nose, and fell into such thought that he heard nothing more of what the teacher was explaining to him. He understood that the teacher did not think what he was saying; he felt it from the tone in which it had been said. “But why have they all agreed to say it all in the same way, all the dullest and most useless things? Why does he push me away from him, why doesn’t he love me?” he asked himself sadly, and could not think of an answer.

## Chapter XXVII

After the teacher came the lesson with his father. While his father had not yet come, Seryozha sat at the table playing with a little knife and began to think. Among Seryozha’s favourite occupations was looking for his mother during his walks. He did not believe in death in general and in her death in particular, in spite of what Lidia Ivanovna had told him and his father had confirmed; and so, even after he had been told that she was dead, he looked for her during his walks. Every woman who was full-figured and graceful, with dark hair, was his mother. At the sight of such a woman a feeling of tenderness rose in his soul, such that he caught his breath and tears came into his eyes. And he would wait, any moment now, for her to come up to him and lift her veil. Her whole face would be seen; she would smile, she would embrace him, he would smell her scent, feel the tenderness of her hand and cry happily, as he had once lain at her feet one evening and she had tickled him, and he had laughed and bitten her white hand with its rings. Later, when he learned by chance from his nurse that his mother was not dead, and his father and Lidia Ivanovna explained to him that she was dead for him because she was not good (which he could not possibly believe, because he loved her), he looked for her and waited for her in just the same way. Today in the Summer Garden there had been a lady in a lilac veil, whom he had followed with a sinking heart, expecting that it was she, as she came towards them along the path. The lady had not reached them and had disappeared somewhere. Today, more strongly than ever, Seryozha felt a rush of love for her, and now, forgetting himself as he waited for his father, he had cut all round the edge of the table with his little knife, looking in front of him with shining eyes and thinking of her.

“Papa is coming!” Vasily Lukich roused him.

Seryozha jumped up, went to his father and, kissing his hand, looked at him attentively, searching for signs of joy at receiving the Alexander Nevsky.

“Did you have a nice walk?” said Alexei Alexandrovich, sitting down in his armchair, drawing the book of the Old Testament towards him and opening it. Although Alexei Alexandrovich had more than once told Seryozha that every Christian must know sacred history thoroughly, he himself often consulted the book over the Old Testament, and Seryozha had noticed it.

“Yes, it was great fun, papa,” said Seryozha, sitting sideways on his chair and rocking it, which was forbidden. “I saw Nadenka” (Nadenka was Lidia Ivanovna’s niece, who was being brought up in her house). “She told me you had been given a new star. Are you glad, papa?”

“In the first place, don’t rock, please,” said Alexei Alexandrovich. “And in the second, it is not the reward that is precious, but the work. And I should like you to understand that. If you work and study in order to receive a reward, the work will seem hard to you; but when you work” (said Alexei Alexandrovich, remembering how he had kept himself up by the awareness of duty in the dull work of that morning, which had consisted in signing a hundred and eighteen papers), “loving the work, you will find your reward in it.”

Seryozha’s eyes, shining with tenderness and gaiety, went dim and dropped under his father’s gaze. It was that same long-familiar tone which his father always took with him, and which Seryozha had already learned to fall in with. His father always talked to him, so Seryozha felt, as though he were addressing some boy he had imagined, one of the kind that are found in books, but not at all like Seryozha. And Seryozha always tried, with his father, to pretend to be that very boy out of a book.

“You understand that, I hope?” said his father.

“Yes, papa,” answered Seryozha, pretending to be the imaginary boy.

The lesson consisted of learning by heart some verses from the Gospel and repeating the beginnings of the Old Testament. Seryozha knew the verses from the Gospel fairly well, but at the moment he was saying them he became absorbed in looking at the bone of his father’s forehead, which curved so steeply at the temple that he got muddled and moved the end of one verse, at a word that was the same, to the beginning of another. It was obvious to Alexei Alexandrovich that he did not understand what he was saying, and this irritated him.

He frowned and began explaining what Seryozha had heard many times and could never remember, because he understood it too clearly: something like “suddenly” being an adverbial modifier of manner of action. Seryozha looked at his father with frightened eyes and thought of only one thing: whether or not his father would make him repeat what he had said, as sometimes happened. And this thought so frightened Seryozha that he no longer understood anything. But his father did not make him repeat it, and went on to the lesson from the Old Testament. Seryozha told the events themselves well, but when he had to answer questions about what certain events prefigured, he knew nothing, although he had already been punished over this lesson. And the place where he could say nothing at all, and fidgeted, and cut the table, and rocked on his chair, was where he had to talk about the patriarchs before the Flood. Of them he knew none except Enoch, who was taken up to heaven alive. Before, he had remembered the names, but now he had forgotten them completely, especially because Enoch was his favourite person in the whole Old Testament, and in his head there was attached to Enoch’s being taken up alive to heaven a whole long train of thought, to which he now gave himself up, gazing with fixed eyes at his father’s watch-chain and at a button of his waistcoat that was half undone.

In death, of which he was told so often, Seryozha did not believe at all. He did not believe that people he loved could die, and above all that he himself would die. That was completely impossible and incomprehensible to him. But he was told that everyone would die; he had even asked people he trusted, and they had confirmed it; his nurse said so too, though unwillingly. But Enoch had not died, so not everyone dies. “And why can’t everyone deserve it before God in the same way and be taken up alive to heaven?” thought Seryozha. The bad people, that is, those Seryozha did not love, they might die; but the good ones could all be like Enoch.

“Well, then, which are the patriarchs?”

“Enoch, Enos.”

“You have said that already. Bad, Seryozha, very bad. If you do not try to learn what is most necessary for a Christian,” said his father, getting up, “then what can interest you? I am displeased with you, and Pyotr Ignatyich” (this was the chief pedagogue) “is displeased with you… I must punish you.”

His father and the pedagogue were both displeased with Seryozha, and indeed he learned very badly. But it could by no means be said that he was an incapable boy. On the contrary, he was much more capable than the boys the pedagogue held up to Seryozha as examples. From his father’s point of view, he did not want to learn what he was taught. In reality, he could not learn it. He could not because there were demands in his soul more binding on him than those his father and the pedagogue made. These demands were in contradiction, and he was in open struggle with those who were bringing him up.

He was nine years old; he was a child; but he knew his own soul, it was precious to him, he guarded it as the eyelid guards the eye, and without the key of love he let no one into his soul. Those who were bringing him up complained that he did not want to learn, and yet his soul was overflowing with a thirst for knowledge. And he learned from Kapitonych, from his nurse, from Nadenka, from Vasily Lukich, but not from his teachers. The water his father and the pedagogue were waiting for to turn their wheels had long since seeped away and was working somewhere else.

His father punished Seryozha by not letting him go to see Nadenka, Lidia Ivanovna’s niece; but this punishment turned out to be lucky for Seryozha. Vasily Lukich was in a good humour and showed him how to make windmills. The whole evening passed in working at them and dreaming of how one could make a windmill to turn round on oneself: catch hold of the sails with one’s hands, or tie oneself on, and go round. Seryozha did not think of his mother all evening, but when he was in bed he suddenly remembered her and prayed in his own words that tomorrow, for his birthday, his mother would stop hiding and come to him.

“Vasily Lukich, do you know what I prayed for extra, not counting the rest?”

“To learn better?”

“No.”

“Toys?”

“No. You won’t guess. Something splendid, but it’s a secret! When it comes true I’ll tell you. Haven’t you guessed?”

“No, I shan’t guess. You tell me,” said Vasily Lukich, smiling, which was rare with him. “Well, lie down; I’m putting out the candle.”

“But without the candle I can see better what I see and what I prayed about. There, I nearly told the secret!” said Seryozha, laughing merrily.

When the candle had been taken away, Seryozha heard and felt his mother. She was standing over him and caressing him with a loving look. But then came windmills, the little knife, everything got mixed up, and he fell asleep.

## Chapter XXVIII

On arriving in Petersburg, Vronsky and Anna stayed at one of the best hotels. Vronsky separately, on the ground floor; Anna upstairs with the baby, the wet-nurse and her maid, in a large suite of four rooms.

On the very first day after their arrival Vronsky went to see his brother. There he found his mother, who had come from Moscow on business. His mother and his sister-in-law met him as usual; they asked him about his journey abroad, talked of common acquaintances, but did not say a word about his liaison with Anna. But his brother, coming to see Vronsky the next morning, asked him about her himself, and Alexei Vronsky told him straight out that he regarded his liaison with Madame Karenina as a marriage; that he hoped to arrange a divorce and then marry her, and until then considered her his wife just as much as any other wife, and asked him to tell their mother and his own wife so.

“If society does not approve of it, I don’t care,” said Vronsky, “but if my family want to be on family terms with me, they must be on the same terms with my wife.”

The elder brother, who had always respected the younger one’s judgements, did not quite know whether he was right or not until society decided the question; for his own part he had nothing against it, and he went with Alexei to see Anna.

In front of his brother, as in front of everyone, Vronsky said “you” to Anna and treated her as a close acquaintance; but it was understood that his brother knew their relations, and they talked of Anna’s going to Vronsky’s estate.

In spite of all his experience of society, Vronsky, because of the new position he was in, was under a strange delusion. It would seem that he ought to have understood that society was closed to him and Anna; but now some vague notions were born in his head that it had been so only in the old days, and that now, with rapid progress (he had without noticing it become a supporter of every kind of progress), the view of society had changed, and the question of whether they would be received in society was not yet settled. “Of course,” he thought, “court society will not receive her, but people close to us can and must understand it properly.”

One can sit for several hours on end with one’s legs tucked under in the same position if one knows that nothing prevents one from changing the position; but if a man knows that he must sit like that with his legs tucked under, he will get cramp, his legs will twitch and strain towards the place where he would like to stretch them. This is just what Vronsky felt with regard to society. Although in the depths of his soul he knew that society was closed to them, he tested whether society would not change now and receive them. But he very soon noticed that, though society was open to him personally, it was closed to Anna. As in the game of cat and mouse, the hands that were raised for him dropped at once before Anna.

One of the first ladies of Petersburg society whom Vronsky saw was his cousin Betsy.

“At last!” she greeted him joyfully. “And Anna? How glad I am! Where are you staying? I can imagine how horrible our Petersburg must be to you after your delightful journey; I can imagine your honeymoon in Rome. And the divorce? Have you done all that?”

Vronsky noticed that Betsy’s delight diminished when she learned that there had not yet been a divorce.

“People will throw stones at me, I know,” she said, “but I shall come to see Anna; yes, I shall certainly come. You won’t be staying here long?”

And indeed she came to see Anna that same day; but her tone was not at all what it had been before. She was evidently proud of her boldness and wished Anna to appreciate the loyalty of her friendship. She stayed no more than ten minutes, talking of society news, and as she left she said:

“You haven’t told me when the divorce will be. Suppose I have thrown my bonnet over the windmill, other people with their collars turned up will freeze you until you are married. And it is so simple now. Ça se fait. So you are leaving on Friday? A pity we shan’t see each other again.”

From Betsy’s tone Vronsky might have understood what he had to expect from society; but he made one more attempt, within his own family. He did not count on his mother. He knew that his mother, who had been so delighted with Anna at their first acquaintance, was now merciless towards her because she had been the cause of the ruin of her son’s career. But he set great hopes on Varya, his brother’s wife. It seemed to him that she would not throw a stone, and that with simplicity and decision she would go to Anna and receive her.

On the very next day after his arrival Vronsky went to see her and, finding her alone, stated his wish directly.

“You know, Alexei,” she said, after hearing him out, “how I love you and how ready I am to do anything for you; but I have kept silent because I knew I could not be of use to you and to Anna Arkadyevna,” she said, pronouncing “Anna Arkadyevna” with particular care. “Please don’t think that I condemn her. Never; perhaps in her place I would have done the same. I don’t and can’t go into the details,” she said, glancing timidly at his gloomy face. “But one must call things by their names. You want me to go and see her, receive her, and so rehabilitate her in society; but you must understand that I cannot do that. My daughters are growing up, and I must live in society for my husband’s sake. Well, I shall go and see Anna Arkadyevna; she will understand that I cannot invite her to my house, or that I must do it so that she does not meet the people who look at it differently; that would hurt her too. I cannot raise her up…”

“But I don’t consider that she has fallen any lower than hundreds of women whom you receive!” Vronsky interrupted her, more gloomily still, and got up in silence, understanding that his sister-in-law’s decision was not to be changed.

“Alexei! Don’t be angry with me. Please understand that it is not my fault,” Varya began, looking at him with a timid smile.

“I am not angry with you,” he said, just as gloomily, “but it hurts me doubly. It hurts me too that this breaks our friendship. Let us say it does not break it, but weakens it. You understand that for me too it cannot be otherwise.”

And with that he left her.

Vronsky understood that further attempts were vain, and that he must spend these few days in Petersburg as in a strange town, avoiding all relations with his former world, so as not to be exposed to the unpleasantnesses and insults that were so tormenting to him. One of the chief unpleasantnesses of his position in Petersburg was that Alexei Alexandrovich and his name seemed to be everywhere. It was impossible to begin talking about anything without the conversation turning to Alexei Alexandrovich; it was impossible to go anywhere without meeting him. So at least it seemed to Vronsky, as it seems to a man with a sore finger that, as if on purpose, he knocks that very sore finger against everything.

His stay in Petersburg seemed all the harder to Vronsky because all this time he saw in Anna some new mood that he could not understand. Now she seemed to be in love with him, now she became cold, irritable and impenetrable. She was tormented by something and was hiding something from him, and seemed not to notice the insults that poisoned his life and that for her, with her fine understanding, must have been still more tormenting.

## Chapter XXIX

One of the aims of the journey to Russia, for Anna, was to see her son. From the day she had left Italy, the thought of this meeting had never ceased to stir her. And the closer she came to Petersburg, the greater the joy and the significance of this meeting appeared to her. She did not even ask herself how this meeting was to be arranged. It seemed to her natural and simple to see her son when she was in the same town with him; but on her arrival in Petersburg her present position in society suddenly presented itself to her clearly, and she understood that it would be difficult to arrange a meeting.

She had already been two days in Petersburg. The thought of her son did not leave her for a minute, but she had not yet seen her son. To drive straight to the house, where she might meet Alexei Alexandrovich, she felt she had no right. She might not be let in, and be insulted. To write and enter into relations with her husband was agony even to think of: she could be at peace only when she was not thinking of her husband. To see her son out walking, finding out where and when he went out, was too little for her: she had been preparing for this meeting so long, she had so much to say to him, she longed so to embrace him, to kiss him. Seryozha’s old nurse could have helped her and told her what to do. But the nurse was no longer in Alexei Alexandrovich’s house. Two days passed in these hesitations and in searching for the nurse.

Having learned of Alexei Alexandrovich’s close relations with Countess Lidia Ivanovna, Anna decided on the third day to write her a letter, which cost her great effort, in which she deliberately said that permission to see her son must depend on her husband’s magnanimity. She knew that if the letter were shown to her husband, he, keeping up his part of magnanimity, would not refuse her.

The commissionaire who had taken the letter brought her the cruellest answer, and one she had not expected: that there would be no answer. She had never felt so humiliated as at the moment when, having called the commissionaire, she heard from him a detailed account of how he had waited and how he had then been told: “There will be no answer at all.” Anna felt humiliated, insulted, but she saw that from her point of view Countess Lidia Ivanovna was right. Her grief was all the stronger because it was solitary. She could not and would not share it with Vronsky. She knew that to him, although he was the chief cause of her unhappiness, the question of her seeing her son would seem a most unimportant thing. She knew that he would never be able to understand the full depth of her suffering; she knew that for his cold tone whenever it was mentioned she would hate him. And she feared this more than anything in the world, and so she hid from him everything that concerned her son.

Staying at home the whole day, she thought of means for a meeting with her son, and settled on the decision to write to her husband. She was already composing this letter when Lidia Ivanovna’s letter was brought to her. The countess’s silence had subdued and humbled her, but the letter, all that she read between its lines, so exasperated her, this malice seemed to her so outrageous in comparison with her passionate, rightful tenderness for her son, that she rebelled against the others and stopped blaming herself.

“This coldness is a pretence of feeling!” she said to herself. “All they want is to insult me and torment the child, and am I to submit to them? Never! She is worse than I. At least I don’t lie.” And then and there she decided that tomorrow, on Seryozha’s very birthday, she would go straight to her husband’s house, would bribe the servants, would deceive, but would at all costs see her son and destroy this monstrous deceit with which they had surrounded the unhappy child.

She went to a toy shop, bought a great many toys, and thought out a plan of action. She would arrive early in the morning, at eight o’clock, when Alexei Alexandrovich would surely not yet be up. She would have money in her hands, which she would give the porter and the footman so that they would let her in, and, without raising her veil, she would say that she had come from Seryozha’s godfather to congratulate him and had been told to put the toys by his bed. The only thing she had not prepared was the words she would say to her son. However much she thought about it, she could think of nothing.

The next day, at eight in the morning, Anna got out alone from a hired carriage and rang at the great entrance of her former home.

“Go and see what she wants. Some lady,” said Kapitonych, not yet dressed, in his overcoat and galoshes, looking out of the window at a lady in a veil, standing close to the door.

The porter’s assistant, a young fellow Anna did not know, had only just opened the door to her when she was already inside, and, taking a three-rouble note out of her muff, hurriedly thrust it into his hand.

“Seryozha… Sergei Alexeich,” she said, and was about to go on. Looking at the note, the porter’s assistant stopped her at the second glass door.

“Whom do you want?” he asked.

She did not hear his words and made no answer.

Noticing the stranger’s confusion, Kapitonych himself came out to her, let her through the doors and asked what she wished.

“From Prince Skorodumov, for Sergei Alexeich,” she said.

“He is not up yet,” said the porter, looking at her attentively.

Anna had never expected that the hall of the house where she had lived nine years, completely unchanged, would affect her so strongly. One after another, memories, joyful and tormenting, rose in her soul, and for an instant she forgot why she was there.

“Will you be pleased to wait?” said Kapitonych, taking off her fur coat.

Having taken off her coat, Kapitonych looked into her face, recognized her, and bowed low to her in silence.

“Please come in, your excellency,” he said to her.

She wanted to say something, but her voice refused to utter any sound; looking at the old man with guilty entreaty, she went with quick, light steps towards the stairs. Bending right forward, his galoshes catching on the steps, Kapitonych ran after her, trying to get ahead of her.

“The tutor is there, he may not be dressed. I’ll announce you.”

Anna went on up the familiar stairs, not understanding what the old man was saying.

“This way, to the left, please. Excuse its not being clean. He is in the old sitting-room now,” said the porter, panting. “Allow me to wait a moment, your excellency; I’ll look in,” he said, and, getting ahead of her, he opened the tall door a little and disappeared behind it. Anna stopped, waiting. “He has only just woken up,” said the porter, coming out of the door again.

And at the moment the porter was saying this, Anna heard the sound of a child yawning. From the sound of this yawn alone she knew her son, and saw him before her as if alive.

“Let me in, let me in, go away!” she said, and went in at the tall door. To the right of the door stood a bed, and on the bed sat, raised up, a little boy in nothing but an unbuttoned shirt, and, bending his little body over, stretching, he was finishing a yawn. At the moment his lips came together, they formed a blissfully sleepy smile, and with this smile he slowly and sweetly fell back again.

“Seryozha!” she whispered, going up to him noiselessly.

During her separation from him, and with the surge of love she had felt all this recent time, she had imagined him as a boy of four, as she had loved him most of all. Now he was not even as she had left him; he had gone still further from the four-year-old, had grown still more and become thinner. What is this! How thin his face is, how short his hair! How long his arms! How he has changed since she left him! But it was he, with the shape of his head, his lips, his soft little neck and his broad little shoulders.

“Seryozha!” she repeated, right at the child’s ear.

He raised himself on his elbow again, turned his tousled head this way and that, as though looking for something, and opened his eyes. Quietly and questioningly he looked for a few seconds at his mother standing motionless before him; then suddenly he smiled blissfully and, closing his sticky eyes again, fell, not backwards, but towards her, into her arms.

“Seryozha! My darling boy!” she said, gasping and putting her arms round his plump body.

“Mama!” he said, wriggling under her hands so as to touch her hands with different parts of his body.

Smiling sleepily, his eyes still shut, he moved his plump little hands from the head of the bed to her shoulders, leaned against her, surrounding her with that sweet sleepy smell and warmth which only children have, and began rubbing his face against her neck and shoulders.

“I knew,” he said, opening his eyes. “Today is my birthday. I knew you would come. I’ll get up now.”

And as he said it he was falling asleep.

Anna looked at him hungrily; she saw how he had grown and changed in her absence. She knew and did not know his bare legs, so big now, that had come out from under the blanket; she knew these thinner cheeks, these short-cut curls at the nape of his neck, where she had so often kissed him. She touched all this and could say nothing; tears were choking her.

“What are you crying about, mama?” he said, waking up completely. “Mama, what are you crying about?” he cried in a tearful voice.

“I? I won’t cry… I’m crying for joy. I haven’t seen you for so long. I won’t, I won’t,” she said, swallowing her tears and turning away. “Well, it’s time for you to dress now,” she added, recovering herself, after a pause; and, not letting go of his hand, she sat down by his bed on the chair where his clothes had been put ready.

“How do you dress without me? How…” she wanted to begin talking simply and gaily, but she could not, and turned away again.

“I don’t wash in cold water; papa said not to. And haven’t you seen Vasily Lukich? He’ll come. And you’re sitting on my clothes!” And Seryozha burst out laughing.

She looked at him and smiled.

“Mama, darling, sweetheart!” he cried, throwing himself on her again and hugging her. It was as though only now, seeing her smile, he clearly understood what had happened. “You don’t need that,” he said, taking off her hat. And, as though seeing her anew without her hat, he threw himself on her again to kiss her.

“But what did you think about me? You didn’t think I was dead?”

“I never believed it.”

“You didn’t believe it, my darling?”

“I knew, I knew!” he said his favourite phrase, and, seizing the hand that was caressing his hair, began pressing its palm to his mouth and kissing it.

## Chapter XXX

Meanwhile Vasily Lukich, who had not understood at first who this lady was, and had learned from the conversation that she was that very mother who had left her husband and whom he did not know, since he had come into the house after her time, was in doubt whether to go in or not, or to inform Alexei Alexandrovich. Considering at last that his duty consisted in getting Seryozha up at a fixed hour, and that he therefore had no business to sort out who was sitting there, his mother or anyone else, but must do his duty, he dressed, went to the door and opened it.

But the caresses of mother and son, the sounds of their voices and what they were saying, all this made him change his intention. He shook his head and, sighing, shut the door. “I’ll wait another ten minutes,” he said to himself, clearing his throat and wiping away his tears.

Among the servants of the house there was meanwhile great agitation. Everyone had learned that the mistress had come, and that Kapitonych had let her in, and that she was now in the nursery, while the master always went into the nursery himself after eight o’clock; and everyone understood that a meeting of husband and wife was impossible and must be prevented. Korney, the valet, going down to the porter’s lodge, asked who had let her in and how, and, learning that Kapitonych had received her and shown her up, he reprimanded the old man. The porter kept an obstinate silence, but when Korney told him he ought to be sacked for it, Kapitonych jumped up to him and, waving his hands in front of Korney’s face, began:

“Oh yes, you wouldn’t have let her in! I served her ten years and saw nothing but kindness, and you would go now and say: please, madam, get out! You understand policy very finely! So there! You’d better remember yourself, how you fleece the master and carry off raccoon coats!”

“Soldier!” said Korney contemptuously, and turned to the nurse, who was coming in. “Now you judge, Marya Yefimovna: he let her in and told no one,” Korney said to her. “Alexei Alexandrovich will be coming out directly and going to the nursery.”

“What goings-on, what goings-on!” said the nurse. “You, Korney Vasilyevich, keep him back somehow, the master, and I’ll run and get her away somehow. What goings-on!”

When the nurse went into the nursery, Seryozha was telling his mother how he and Nadenka had fallen while sliding down a hill and turned head over heels three times. She was listening to the sound of his voice, seeing his face and the play of its expression, feeling his hand, but not understanding what he was saying. She had to go, she had to leave him: that was all she thought and felt. She heard both Vasily Lukich’s steps coming up to the door and his coughing, and the steps of the nurse coming up; but she sat as if turned to stone, unable either to begin speaking or to get up.

“Mistress, my dear!” the nurse began, going up to Anna and kissing her hands and shoulders. “God has brought joy to our birthday boy. You haven’t changed one little bit.”

“Oh, nurse, dear, I didn’t know you were in the house,” said Anna, coming to herself for a moment.

“I don’t live here, I live with my daughter; I came to give my good wishes, Anna Arkadyevna, my dear!”

The nurse suddenly burst into tears and again began kissing her hand.

Seryozha, his eyes and smile beaming, holding his mother with one hand and his nurse with the other, was stamping on the carpet with his plump bare little feet. His beloved nurse’s tenderness to his mother delighted him.

“Mama! She often comes to see me, and when she comes…” he began, but stopped, noticing that the nurse had whispered something to his mother, and that his mother’s face had taken on a look of fright and something like shame, which did not suit his mother at all.

She went up to him.

“My darling!” she said.

She could not say goodbye, but the expression of her face said it, and he understood. “Darling, darling Kutik!” she said, using the name she had called him when he was little. “You won’t forget me? You…” but she could say no more.

How many words she thought of afterwards that she might have said to him! And now she could not, did not know how to say anything. But Seryozha understood everything she wanted to tell him. He understood that she was unhappy and loved him. He understood even what the nurse had said in a whisper. He had heard the words “always after eight o’clock”, and he understood that this was said of his father, and that his mother and father must not meet. That he understood; but one thing he could not understand: why fright and shame had come into her face?… She was not to blame, and yet she was afraid of him and ashamed of something. He wanted to ask a question that would clear up this doubt for him, but he did not dare: he saw that she was suffering, and he was sorry for her. He pressed himself against her in silence and said in a whisper:

“Don’t go yet. He won’t come soon.”

His mother held him away from her to understand whether he meant what he was saying, and in the frightened expression of his face she read that he was not only speaking of his father but was, as it were, asking her how he ought to think of his father.

“Seryozha, my darling,” she said, “love him; he is better and kinder than I, and I have wronged him. When you grow up, you will judge.”

“There’s no one better than you!…” he cried in despair through his tears, and, seizing her by the shoulders, began pressing her to him with all his strength, with arms trembling from the effort.

“My darling, my little one!” said Anna, and she cried as weakly, as childishly, as he was crying.

At that moment the door opened and Vasily Lukich came in. Steps were heard at the other door, and the nurse said in a frightened whisper:

“He’s coming,” and handed Anna her hat.

Seryozha sank down on the bed and sobbed, covering his face with his hands. Anna took those hands away, kissed his wet face once more, and went out of the door with quick steps. Alexei Alexandrovich was coming towards her. Seeing her, he stopped and bowed his head.

In spite of her having just said that he was better and kinder than she, in the quick glance she cast at him, taking in his whole figure with all its details, feelings of revulsion and anger towards him, and of jealousy over her son, seized her. With a quick movement she lowered her veil and, quickening her step, almost ran out of the room.

She had not even had time to take out the toys she had chosen the day before in the shop with such love and sadness, and so she brought them home again.

## Chapter XXXI

However strongly Anna had longed to see her son, however long she had thought about it and prepared for it, she had never expected the meeting to affect her so strongly. Returning to her lonely suite in the hotel, she could not for a long time understand why she was there. “Yes, it is all over, and I am alone again,” she said to herself, and, without taking off her hat, she sat down in an armchair by the fireplace. Fixing her motionless eyes on the bronze clock that stood on a table between the windows, she began to think.

The French maid she had brought from abroad came in to suggest that she should dress. She looked at her in surprise and said:

“Later.”

A footman offered coffee.

“Later,” she said.

The Italian wet-nurse, having dressed the little girl, came in with her and held her out to Anna. The plump, well-fed little girl, as always on seeing her mother, turned her bare little arms, creased as if tied with threads, palms downward, and, smiling with her toothless little mouth, began, like a fish with its fins, paddling with her hands, rustling them against the starched folds of her embroidered little skirt. It was impossible not to smile, not to kiss the little girl; impossible not to hold out a finger to her, which she seized, squealing and bouncing with her whole body; impossible not to offer her a lip, which she took into her little mouth by way of a kiss. And Anna did all this, and took her in her arms, and made her jump, and kissed her fresh little cheek and her bare little elbows; but at the sight of this child it was still clearer to her that what she felt for her was not even love compared with what she felt for Seryozha. Everything in this little girl was sweet, but somehow none of it took hold of her heart. On her first child, though it was by a man she did not love, all the strength of a love that found no satisfaction had been spent; the little girl had been born in the hardest conditions, and not a hundredth part of the care spent on the first had been spent on her. Besides, in the little girl everything was still expectation, while Seryozha was already almost a person, and a beloved person; thoughts and feelings were already struggling in him; he understood, he loved, he judged her, she thought, remembering his words and looks. And she was separated from him for ever, not only physically but spiritually, and that could not be put right.

She gave the little girl back to the wet-nurse, sent her away, and opened the locket in which there was a portrait of Seryozha when he was almost the same age as the little girl. She got up and, taking off her hat, took from a little table an album in which there were photographs of her son at other ages. She wanted to compare the photographs and began taking them out of the album. She took them all out. There remained one, the last and best photograph. He was sitting astride a chair in a white shirt, frowning with his eyes and smiling with his mouth. It was his most special, his best expression. With her small, deft hands, whose slender white fingers were moving today with particular tension, she caught at the corner of the photograph several times, but the photograph slipped away and she could not get it out. There was no paper-knife on the table, and, taking out the photograph next to it (it was a photograph of Vronsky taken in Rome, in a round hat and with long hair), she pushed out her son’s photograph with it. “Yes, here he is!” she said, glancing at Vronsky’s photograph, and suddenly remembered who was the cause of her present grief. She had not once remembered him all that morning. But now suddenly, seeing that manly, noble face, so familiar and dear to her, she felt an unexpected rush of love for him.

“But where is he? How can he leave me alone with my sufferings?” she suddenly thought with a feeling of reproach, forgetting that she herself had hidden from him everything that concerned her son. She sent to him to ask him to come to her at once; with a sinking heart, thinking out the words in which she would tell him everything, and the expressions of his love that would comfort her, she waited for him. The messenger came back with the answer that he had a visitor but would come at once, and had told him to ask whether she could receive him with Prince Yashvin, who had come to Petersburg. “He is not coming alone, and he has not seen me since dinner yesterday,” she thought; “he is not coming so that I can tell him everything, but with Yashvin.” And suddenly a strange thought came to her: what if he had stopped loving her?

And, going over the events of the last days, it seemed to her that she saw confirmation of this terrible thought in everything: in his having dined out yesterday, and in his having insisted that they should stay apart in Petersburg, and in his coming to her even now not alone, as though avoiding a meeting face to face.

“But he must tell me so. I need to know it. If I know it, then I know what I shall do,” she said to herself, unable to imagine the position she would be in once convinced of his indifference. She thought that he had stopped loving her, she felt herself close to despair, and as a result she felt particularly excited. She rang for her maid and went into her dressing-room. As she dressed, she took more trouble over her toilette than on all these days, as though, having stopped loving her, he might love her again because she wore the dress and the hair that suited her best.

She heard the bell before she was ready.

When she came out into the drawing-room, it was not he but Yashvin whose eyes met her. He was looking at the photographs of her son, which she had forgotten on the table, and was in no hurry to look at her.

“We have met,” she said, putting her small hand into the huge hand of Yashvin, who was embarrassed (which was so strange with his enormous height and coarse face). “We met last year, at the races. Give them to me,” she said, with a quick movement taking from Vronsky the photographs of her son, which he was looking at, and glancing at him significantly with shining eyes. “Were the races good this year? Instead of them I watched the races on the Corso in Rome. But then, you don’t like life abroad,” she said with a kind smile. “I know you, and I know all your tastes, though I have met you so little.”

“I am very sorry for that, because my tastes are mostly bad,” said Yashvin, biting his left moustache.

After talking for a while and noticing that Vronsky glanced at the clock, Yashvin asked her whether she would be staying long in Petersburg, and, unbending his enormous figure, took up his cap.

“Not long, I think,” she said with embarrassment, glancing at Vronsky.

“So we shan’t see each other again?” said Yashvin, getting up and turning to Vronsky. “Where are you dining?”

“Come and dine with me,” said Anna decidedly, as though angry with herself for her embarrassment, but blushing, as she always did when she showed her position before a new person. “The dinner here is not good, but at least you will see him. Of all his regiment there is no one Alexei loves as he loves you.”

“Delighted,” said Yashvin, with a smile from which Vronsky saw that he liked Anna very much.

Yashvin bowed and went out; Vronsky stayed behind.

“Are you going too?” she said to him.

“I am late already,” he answered. “Go on! I’ll catch you up in a moment,” he called to Yashvin.

She took him by the hand and, without taking her eyes off him, looked at him, searching her mind for something to say to keep him.

“Wait, there is something I must tell you,” and, taking his short hand, she pressed it to her neck. “Yes, it was all right, my inviting him to dinner?”

“You did very well,” he said with a calm smile, showing his even teeth and kissing her hand.

“Alexei, you have not changed towards me?” she said, pressing his hand with both of hers. “Alexei, I am worn out here. When are we leaving?”

“Soon, soon. You wouldn’t believe how hard our life here is for me too,” he said, and drew his hand away.

“Well, go, go!” she said, offended, and quickly walked away from him.
