## Book One

1. From my grandfather Verus: good character, and freedom from anger.

2. From what I heard and remember of my father: modesty and manliness.

3. From my mother: reverence for the gods, generosity, and holding back not only from doing wrong but even from thinking of it; and also simplicity in the way she lived, far from the habits of the rich.

4. From my great-grandfather: not to have gone to the public schools, but to have had good teachers at home, and to know that on such things one should spend freely.

5. From my tutor: not to become a fan of the Greens or the Blues at the races, nor of the light-shield or heavy-shield men at the games; to put up with hard work and need little; to do things with my own hands, and not to meddle in other people’s business; and to be slow to listen to slander.

6. From Diognetus: not to get excited about trifles; not to believe what wonder-workers and conjurers say about spells and driving out spirits and the like; not to keep fighting quails or be carried away by such things; to put up with plain speaking; to become at home in philosophy, and to hear first Bacchius, then Tandasis and Marcianus; to write dialogues as a boy; and to want the plank bed and the skin and whatever else belongs to the Greek training.

7. From Rusticus: the realisation that my character needed correcting and looking after; not to be led off into the ambitions of the sophists, nor to write treatises on theory, nor to give little sermons, nor to show off as the man who trains hard or does good deeds for display; to keep away from rhetoric and poetry and clever talk; not to walk about the house in formal dress, or do things of that kind; to write letters simply, like the one he himself wrote to my mother from Sinuessa.

To be easy to call back and easy to make peace with, as soon as those who have been angry with me or wronged me are willing to come back; to read carefully, and not be content with a rough idea of the whole, nor agree quickly with people who talk a lot; and to have come across the notes of Epictetus’s lectures, which he lent me from his own house.

8. From Apollonius: freedom, and a steadiness that takes no chances; to look to nothing else, not even for a moment, but reason; to be always the same, in sharp pain, in the loss of a child, in long illness; and to see clearly, in a living example, that the same man can be most intense and yet relaxed.

Not to be impatient when explaining things; to have seen a man who plainly thought his experience and his skill at teaching principles the least of his good qualities; and to have learned how to accept what seem to be favours from friends, without being made smaller by them, and without letting them pass unnoticed.

9. From Sextus: kindness; the example of a household run like a father’s; the idea of living in agreement with nature; dignity without pretence; looking after the interests of friends with care; patience with ordinary people and with those who form opinions without thinking.

The knack of getting on with everyone, so that his company was more pleasant than any flattery, and at the same time he was held in the greatest respect by the very people he was with; a way of finding and ordering, by a clear method, the principles needed for life.

Never to show any sign of anger or any other passion, but to be at once wholly free of passion and full of affection; to praise without fuss, and to know a great deal without showing it.

10. From Alexander the grammarian: not to find fault; not to pounce scornfully on people who use a foreign word or a bad construction or an odd-sounding phrase, but neatly to bring in the very expression that should have been used, by way of answer, or agreement, or joining in a discussion of the thing itself and not the word, or by some other graceful reminder of that kind.

11. From Fronto: to notice what envy, double-dealing and hypocrisy go with a tyrant’s power, and that on the whole the people we call patricians are somehow rather short on affection.

12. From Alexander the Platonist: not to say often, or without need, to anyone, or to write in a letter, “I am too busy”; and not to keep excusing myself in this way from the duties we owe to the people we live with, on the plea of urgent business.

13. From Catulus: not to brush aside a friend’s complaint, even if it happens to be unreasonable, but to try to put things back as they were; to speak warmly of one’s teachers, as is remembered of Domitius and Athenodotus; and to love one’s children truly.

14. From my brother Severus: love of family, love of truth, love of justice; through him to have come to know Thrasea, Helvidius, Cato, Dio, Brutus; to have formed the idea of a state with the same laws for all, run on equal rights and equal freedom of speech, and of a kingship that honours above all the freedom of those it rules.

From him also: a steady and constant regard for philosophy; doing good, giving generously, being hopeful, and trusting that one is loved by one’s friends; being open with those he found fault with; and that his friends never had to guess what he wanted or did not want: it was plain.

15. From Maximus: self-mastery, and not being swept about by anything; cheerfulness in every circumstance, and in illness too; a character well balanced, gentle and dignified; doing what had to be done without complaint.

That everyone believed that he meant what he said, and that whatever he did he did with no bad intent. Never surprised, never startled, never hurried or hesitant, never at a loss, never downcast, never forcing a smile, and never again angry or suspicious.

Doing good, being ready to forgive, being truthful; giving the impression of a man who had never been bent rather than one who had been straightened; that no one ever thought himself looked down on by him, nor would have dared to think himself better than he; and being pleasantly witty.

16. From my father: gentleness, and holding unshakably to decisions reached after careful thought; no vanity about what are thought to be honours; love of work and persistence; listening to anyone with something to offer for the common good; giving each person what he deserved, without fail; knowing from experience when to press and when to let go.

Putting an end to the affairs with boys; his sense of the common good; letting his friends off from always dining with him or always travelling with him, and being found just the same by those who had stayed behind on some business; careful and patient inquiry in council, never giving up the search early, content with first impressions; keeping his friends, never tiring of them and never infatuated; self-sufficient in everything, and cheerful.

Looking far ahead, and arranging the smallest things in advance without fuss; checking the cheering and all flattery directed at himself; always guarding what the empire needed, keeping careful accounts of public spending, and putting up with the blame for it; neither superstitious about the gods nor, with men, a crowd-pleaser or flatterer or seeker of popularity, but sober in everything and steady, never vulgar and never chasing novelty.

Using the comforts of life, which fortune gave him in plenty, without arrogance and without apology, so that he enjoyed them when they were there, simply, and did not miss them when they were not; that no one would have called him a sophist, or a smart talker, or a pedant, but a mature man, complete, above flattery, able to manage his own affairs and other people’s.

Besides this, honouring those who truly practise philosophy, without reproaching the others, nor yet being easily led by them; easy to be with, and gracious without overdoing it; looking after his own body in moderation, not as one in love with living, nor for vanity, nor yet neglecting it, but so that, by his own care, he hardly ever needed a doctor or medicines or ointments.

Above all, giving way without envy to those who had some special ability, in speaking, or in the history of laws or customs or other matters, and helping them eagerly so that each might win credit for his own gifts; doing everything according to the customs of our fathers, without making a show even of this, of keeping the customs.

Not restless or easily shifted, but staying with the same places and the same things; after his attacks of headache, at once fresh and vigorous for his usual work; having few secrets, very few and very rare, and those only about public affairs; prudent and measured in putting on shows, in public building, in handouts and the like, as a man who looks to what needs to be done, and not to the credit for doing it.

He did not bathe at odd hours; he did not love building; he did not fuss about food, or the weave and colour of his clothes, or the good looks of his slaves. His clothes came up from his country house at Lorium; most of his time at Lanuvium; the way he treated the tax-collector at Tusculum who apologised to him; and all his manner was like this.

Nothing harsh in him, nothing shameless, nothing violent, nothing that would make anyone say “he’s sweating over it”; but everything thought out in turn, as if at leisure, calmly, in order, firmly, consistently. What is remembered of Socrates would fit him: that he could both abstain from and enjoy the things that most people are too weak to abstain from and too self-indulgent in enjoying.

To be strong, to endure, and to stay sober in both is the mark of a man with a complete and unbeatable soul, as Maximus showed in his illness.

17. From the gods: to have had good grandparents, good parents, a good sister, good teachers, good people around me, kinsmen, friends, nearly all of them; and that I never slipped into offending any of them, though I had the kind of disposition that, given the chance, might have led me to do something of the sort; it was the gods’ kindness that no combination of circumstances arose that would have shown me up.

That I was not brought up any longer than I was with my grandfather’s mistress; that I kept my youth innocent, and did not become a man before my time, but even put it off.

That I was placed under a ruler and father who was to take all my vanity away from me, and to bring me to understand that it is possible to live in a palace without needing bodyguards, or embroidered robes, or torches, or statues of this sort, and the like pomp; but that one can bring oneself very close to the life of a private citizen, without for that being any meaner or slacker in the duties a ruler must do for the common good.

That I had a brother whose character could rouse me to take care of myself, and who at the same time delighted me with his respect and affection; that my children were not born dull or deformed in body; that I did not make more progress in rhetoric and poetry and the other studies, in which I might have been held fast if I had found myself getting on well.

That I was quick to give my tutors the positions they seemed to want, and did not put it off, hoping, since they were still young, to do it later. That I knew Apollonius, Rusticus, Maximus.

That I had clear and frequent pictures of what life in agreement with nature is like, so that, as far as the gods and the gifts and help and inspiration that come from them are concerned, nothing prevents me from living according to nature now; and if I still fall short of it, that is my own fault, and comes from not keeping to the reminders, almost the lessons, of the gods.

That my body has held out so long in such a life; that I never touched Benedicta or Theodotus, and that later, when I was caught up in love, I was cured; that, though I was often angry with Rusticus, I never did anything I would have regretted; that my mother, who was to die young, still spent her last years with me.

That whenever I wanted to help someone poor or in any other need, I was never told that I had no money to do it with; and that I myself never fell into such need as to take from another; that my wife is the woman she is, so obedient, so loving, so simple; that I found suitable teachers for my children.

That remedies were given me in dreams, among them against spitting blood and dizziness, as at Caieta; and that, when I set my heart on philosophy, I did not fall into the hands of some sophist, or sit down to writing books, or analysing syllogisms, or busying myself with the heavens. For all these things need “the help of the gods and of fortune”.

Written among the Quadi, on the river Gran.

## Book Two

1. Say to yourself at dawn: today I shall meet people who are meddling, ungrateful, insolent, treacherous, envious, unsociable. They are all like this because they do not know what is good and what is bad. But I have seen the nature of the good, that it is beautiful, and of the bad, that it is ugly, and the nature of the wrongdoer himself, that he is my kin: not of the same blood or seed, but sharing in the same mind and the same portion of the divine. So I cannot be harmed by any of them, for no one can drag me into what is ugly; and I cannot be angry with my kinsman or hate him. For we were born to work together, like feet, like hands, like eyelids, like the rows of upper and lower teeth. To work against one another, then, is against nature; and to be angry with someone and turn your back on him is working against him.

2. Whatever this is that I am, it is a little flesh, a little breath, and the ruling mind. Throw away your books; stop being distracted; it is not allowed. But, as if you were already dying, despise the flesh: blood and bones and a woven net of nerves, veins and arteries. Look too at the breath, what kind of thing it is: wind, and never the same, but every moment breathed out and sucked in again. The third, then, is the ruling mind. Think of it like this: you are old; do not let it be a slave any longer, do not let it be jerked about like a puppet by selfish impulses any longer, and do not let it go on grumbling at what is given you now, or dreading what is to come.

3. The works of the gods are full of providence. The works of fortune are not apart from nature, or from the weaving and interlacing of the things that providence governs. Everything flows from there. And there is also necessity, and what benefits the whole world, of which you are a part. For every part of nature, that is good which the nature of the whole brings, and which preserves it. And the world is preserved by changes, of the elements and of the things made from them alike. Let this be enough for you; let these be your principles. But throw away your thirst for books, so that you do not die grumbling, but truly at peace, and grateful to the gods from your heart.

4. Remember how long you have been putting this off, and how many times the gods have given you extra days and you have not used them. You must now at last understand what world you are a part of, and what governor of the world you have flowed from; and that a limit of time is set for you, and if you do not use it to clear the clouds from your mind, it will go, and you will go, and it will never come again.

5. Every hour, set your mind firmly, as a Roman and a man, on doing what is in front of you with precise and unaffected dignity, with affection, with freedom and with justice; and give yourself rest from all other thoughts. And you will give yourself rest if you do every act of your life as if it were your last, free of all carelessness, of every passionate turning away from the command of reason, of pretence, of self-love, and of discontent with what has been given you. You see how few things a person needs to master in order to live a life that flows well and is like the life of the gods; for the gods will ask nothing more of the one who keeps to these.

6. Go on, insult yourself, insult yourself, my soul; but you will not have the chance to honour yourself much longer. Each person has only one life, and yours is nearly over, and you do not respect yourself, but place your happiness in the souls of other people.

7. Are things that happen to you from outside distracting you? Then give yourself the time to learn something good, and stop wandering round in circles. But you must also beware of the other kind of wandering. For those people are also fools who have worn themselves out with activity in life, and have no target at which to aim every impulse and, in a word, every thought.

8. A person is seldom seen to be unhappy because he does not notice what is going on in someone else’s soul. But those who do not follow the movements of their own soul are bound to be unhappy.

9. Always keep these things in mind: what the nature of the whole is, and what my nature is, and how the one is related to the other; what kind of part it is of what kind of whole; and that no one can prevent you from always doing and saying what is in agreement with the nature of which you are a part.

10. Theophrastus, comparing wrongdoings as one might in a general way, says, like a true philosopher, that wrongs done from desire are worse than wrongs done from anger. For the angry man seems to turn away from reason with a certain pain and an inner tightening; but the man who does wrong from desire, overcome by pleasure, seems somehow more self-indulgent and softer in his wrongdoing. Rightly, then, and worthily of philosophy, he said that a wrong done with pleasure deserves more blame than one done with pain. And on the whole, the angry man is more like someone who has been wronged first and driven to anger by pain; while the other has set out to do wrong of his own accord, carried along by desire to do something.

11. Do everything, say everything, think everything, as one who may leave life at any moment. To depart from among men, if the gods exist, is nothing to fear; for they would not bring you into evil. And if they do not exist, or do not care about human affairs, what is it to me to live in a world empty of gods, or empty of providence?

But they do exist, and they do care about human affairs; and they have put it wholly in a man’s power not to fall into what is truly evil. And if there were anything evil in the rest, they would have seen to that too, so that it would be wholly in a man’s power not to fall into it. But how could something that does not make a man worse make a man’s life worse?

The nature of the whole would not have overlooked this, either through ignorance, or knowing but unable to guard against it or put it right; nor would it have made so great a mistake, through lack of power or lack of skill, as to let good things and bad things happen equally and at random to good people and bad.

But death and life, fame and obscurity, pain and pleasure, wealth and poverty, all these happen equally to good people and to bad, being neither fine nor shameful. So they are neither good nor bad.

12. How quickly everything vanishes: in the world, the bodies themselves; in time, the memory of them. What all the things of the senses are like, especially those that tempt us with pleasure, or frighten us with pain, or are trumpeted by vanity; how cheap they are, how contemptible, dirty, perishable and dead: this is for the power of the mind to consider. What are these people, whose opinions and voices give a good name? What is dying? And if one looks at it by itself, and with the analysis of thought takes apart the images attached to it, one will no longer think it anything other than a work of nature; and if anyone is afraid of a work of nature, he is a child. And yet it is not only a work of nature, but one that is good for nature. How a man touches God, and with what part of himself, and in what condition that part of him is when it does.

13. Nothing is more wretched than the man who goes round and round everything, and “searches the things beneath the earth”, as the poet says, and tries to guess what is in the souls of his neighbours, and does not see that it is enough to be with the divine spirit within himself alone, and to serve it truly. And the service of it is to keep it pure of passion, of carelessness, and of discontent with what comes from gods and men. For what comes from the gods deserves reverence for their goodness; and what comes from men deserves affection, because they are our kin; and sometimes, in a way, pity, because they do not know good from bad: a blindness no less than the one that takes away the power to tell white from black.

14. Even if you were going to live three thousand years, or thirty thousand, remember all the same that no one loses any other life than the one he is living, nor lives any other than the one he loses. So the longest and the shortest come to the same. For the present is equal for everyone, and so what is lost is equal too; and so what is lost turns out to be just a moment. For no one can lose either the past or the future: how could anyone take from him what he does not have?

So you must remember these two things. First, that all things from eternity are of the same kind and come round again in circles, and it makes no difference whether you see the same things for a hundred years, or two hundred, or for endless time. Second, that the one who lives longest and the one who will die soonest lose the same. For it is only the present that one can be deprived of, since that is all one has; and no one loses what he does not have.

15. That everything is judgement. What was said to Monimus the Cynic is clear; and clear too is the usefulness of the saying, if one takes the good of it, as far as it is true.

16. The human soul insults itself, first and most, when it becomes, as far as it can, an abscess, a kind of growth on the world. For to be angry at anything that happens is to break away from nature, which holds within it the natures of all other things. Next, when it turns away from a person, or even moves against him meaning to harm him, as the souls of angry people do. Third, it insults itself when it is overcome by pleasure or pain. Fourth, when it pretends, and does or says anything falsely and untruly. Fifth, when it lets any action or impulse of its own go at no target, but acts at random and without attention, when even the smallest things ought to be done with reference to the end. And the end of rational creatures is to follow the reason and the law of the most ancient city and commonwealth.

17. Of human life, the time is a point; the substance flowing; the perception dim; the whole body’s make-up quick to rot; the soul a whirl; fortune hard to read; fame without judgement. In a word: all that belongs to the body is a river, all that belongs to the soul a dream and smoke; life is a war and a stay in a foreign land; and fame after death is forgetting.

What, then, can guide us through? One thing, and one only: philosophy. And that means keeping the spirit within us unharmed and undamaged, above pleasures and pains, doing nothing at random, nothing falsely or with pretence, not needing anyone else to do or not do anything; and also accepting what happens and what is given as coming from there, wherever it is, from where it came itself; and above all waiting for death with a cheerful mind, as nothing but the setting free of the elements of which every living thing is made. And if there is nothing terrible for the elements themselves in each one constantly changing into another, why should anyone look askance at the change and dissolution of them all? It is according to nature; and nothing according to nature is bad.

Written at Carnuntum.
