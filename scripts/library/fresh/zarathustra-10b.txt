## The Awakening

## 1

After the song of the wanderer and shadow, the cave was suddenly full of noise and laughter; and since the assembled guests were all talking at once, and even the ass, with such encouragement, no longer kept quiet, a slight distaste and mockery towards his visitors came over Zarathustra, though he was glad of their cheerfulness. For it seemed to him a sign of recovery. So he slipped out into the open and spoke to his animals.

“Where has their distress gone now?” he said, and already he himself breathed more easily after his slight disgust. “With me, it seems, they have unlearned crying out in distress!

though, unfortunately, not yet crying out.” And Zarathustra held his ears, for just then the ass’s Ee-aw mingled strangely with the jubilant noise of these higher men.

“They are merry,” he began again, “and who knows? perhaps at their host’s expense; and if they have learned to laugh from me, it is still not my laughter that they have learned.

But what does it matter! They are old people: they recover in their own way, they laugh in their own way; my ears have put up with worse already, and did not grow cross.

This day is a victory: he is already giving way, he is fleeing, the spirit of gravity, my old arch-enemy! How well this day means to end, which began so badly and heavily!

And it wants to end. Already evening is coming: he rides over the sea, the good rider! How he sways, the blissful one, coming home, in his purple saddles!

The sky looks on clearly, the world lies deep: oh, all you strange ones who came to me, it is worth while living with me!”

So spoke Zarathustra. And again the shouting and laughter of the higher men came out of the cave: then he began anew.

“They are biting, my bait is working; their enemy too is giving way, the spirit of gravity. Already they are learning to laugh at themselves: do I hear right?

My men’s food is working, my sayings full of sap and strength: and truly, I did not feed them on vegetables that cause wind! But on warriors’ food, on conquerors’ food: I woke new desires.

There are new hopes in their arms and legs; their hearts are stretching. They are finding new words; soon their spirit will breathe high spirits.

Such food may not be for children, of course, nor for longing little women old and young. Their insides are persuaded differently; I am not their doctor and teacher.

Disgust is leaving these higher men: well then! that is my victory. In my kingdom they are growing safe; all stupid shame runs away; they are pouring themselves out.

They are pouring out their hearts, good hours are coming back to them, they are celebrating and chewing the cud: they are growing grateful.

That I take as the best sign: they are growing grateful. Not long now, and they will think up festivals and put up memorials to their old joys.

They are convalescents!” So Zarathustra spoke joyfully to his heart and looked out; but his animals pressed close to him and honoured his happiness and his silence.

## 2

But suddenly Zarathustra’s ear took fright: for the cave, which until then had been full of noise and laughter, all at once became deathly still; and his nose smelled a sweet-smelling smoke and incense, as of burning pine cones.

“What is happening? What are they doing?” he asked himself, and crept up to the entrance, so that he could watch his guests unnoticed. But, wonder upon wonder! what did he have to see with his own eyes!

“They have all become pious again, they are praying, they are mad!” he said, and was astonished beyond measure. And indeed! all these higher men, the two kings, the retired pope, the wicked sorcerer, the voluntary beggar, the wanderer and shadow, the old soothsayer, the conscientious one of the spirit and the ugliest man: they were all lying on their knees like children and believing old women, and worshipping the ass. And just then the ugliest man began to gurgle and snort, as if something unspeakable wanted to come out of him; but when he had really brought it to words, look, it was a pious, strange litany in praise of the worshipped and incensed ass. And this litany ran thus:

Amen! And praise and honour and wisdom and thanks and glory and strength be to our God, from everlasting to everlasting!

But the ass cried Ee-aw.

He carries our burden, he has taken the form of a servant, he is patient of heart and never says No; and whoever loves his God chastises him.

But the ass cried Ee-aw.

He does not speak: except that he always says Yes to the world he created: so he praises his world. It is his cunning that does not speak: so he is seldom in the wrong.

But the ass cried Ee-aw.

He goes through the world unnoticed. Grey is the colour of the body in which he wraps his virtue. If he has spirit, he hides it; but everyone believes in his long ears.

But the ass cried Ee-aw.

What hidden wisdom it is that he wears long ears and only ever says Yes and never No! Has he not created the world in his own image, that is, as stupid as possible?

But the ass cried Ee-aw.

You go straight ways and crooked ones; you care little what seems straight or crooked to us men. Beyond good and evil is your kingdom. It is your innocence not to know what innocence is.

But the ass cried Ee-aw.

Look how you push no one away from you, neither the beggars nor the kings. You suffer the little children to come to you, and when the wicked boys lure you, you say simply Ee-aw.

But the ass cried Ee-aw.

You love she-asses and fresh figs; you are no despiser of food. A thistle tickles your heart when you happen to be hungry. In that lies a god’s wisdom.

But the ass cried Ee-aw.

## The Ass Festival

## 1

But at this point in the litany Zarathustra could no longer control himself; he cried Ee-aw himself, louder still than the ass, and leapt into the middle of his guests, who had gone mad.

“But what are you doing there, you children of men?” he cried, pulling those who prayed up from the floor. “Woe if anyone other than Zarathustra saw you:

everyone would judge that with your new faith you were the worst blasphemers or the most foolish of all old women!

And you yourself, you old pope, how does it fit with you that you worship an ass here as God like this?”

“O Zarathustra,” answered the pope, “forgive me, but in matters of God I am even more enlightened than you. And that is only fair.

Better to worship God in this form than in no form at all! Think about this saying, my noble friend: you will quickly guess that there is wisdom in such a saying.

The one who said ‘God is a spirit’ took the biggest step and leap towards unbelief that has yet been taken on earth: such a word is not easily made good again on earth!

My old heart leaps and skips because there is still something on earth to worship. Forgive that, O Zarathustra, to an old pious pope’s heart!”

“And you,” said Zarathustra to the wanderer and shadow, “you call yourself and imagine yourself a free spirit? And you carry on such idolatry and priestcraft here?

Truly, you are behaving worse here than with your wicked brown girls, you wicked new believer!”

“Bad enough,” answered the wanderer and shadow, “you are right: but what can I do about it! The old god lives again, O Zarathustra, say what you will.

The ugliest man is to blame for it all: he has raised him up again. And if he says that he once killed him: with gods, death is always only a prejudice.”

“And you,” said Zarathustra, “you wicked old sorcerer, what have you done! Who will believe in you from now on, in this free age, if you believe in such divine asininities?

It was a stupidity, what you did; how could you, clever one, do such a stupid thing!”

“O Zarathustra,” answered the clever sorcerer, “you are right, it was a stupidity; and it has been hard enough for me too.”

“And you above all,” said Zarathustra to the conscientious one of the spirit, “consider, and put your finger to your nose! Does nothing here go against your conscience? Is your spirit not too clean for this praying and the fumes of these praying brothers?”

“There is something in it,” answered the conscientious one, and put his finger to his nose, “there is something in this spectacle that even does my conscience good.

Perhaps I may not believe in God: but it is certain that God seems most believable to me in this form.

God is said to be eternal, according to the testimony of the most pious: whoever has so much time takes his time. As slowly and as stupidly as possible: with that, such a one can get very far indeed.

And whoever has too much spirit might well fall in love with stupidity and folly itself. Think about yourself, O Zarathustra!

You yourself, truly! even you might well become an ass out of overflowing wisdom.

Does not a perfect sage like to go by the most crooked ways? The evidence shows it, O Zarathustra: your evidence!”

“And you yourself, finally,” said Zarathustra, and turned towards the ugliest man, who was still lying on the floor, raising his arm to the ass (for he was giving it wine to drink). “Speak, you unspeakable one, what have you done!

You seem transformed to me, your eye glows, the cloak of the sublime lies around your ugliness: what have you done?

Is it true, then, what they say, that you raised him up again? And what for? Was he not killed and done away with for good reason?

You yourself seem awakened to me: what have you done? What have you turned round? Why have you converted? Speak, you unspeakable one!”

“O Zarathustra,” answered the ugliest man, “you are a rogue!

Whether he still lives, or lives again, or is thoroughly dead: which of us two knows that best? I ask you.

But one thing I know; I learned it from you yourself once, O Zarathustra: whoever wants to kill most thoroughly, laughs.

‘One kills not with anger but with laughter’: so you once said. O Zarathustra, you hidden one, you destroyer without anger, you dangerous saint: you are a rogue!”

## 2

But then it happened that Zarathustra, astonished at nothing but such roguish answers, sprang back to the door of his cave and, turned towards all his guests, cried in a strong voice:

“O you buffoons, all of you, you clowns! Why do you disguise and hide yourselves from me!

How each of your hearts wriggled with delight and malice, because at last you had once again become like little children, that is, pious,

because at last you did again what children do, that is, prayed, folded your hands and said ‘dear God’!

But now leave this nursery, my own cave, where all childishness is at home today. Cool off out here your hot children’s high spirits and the noise of your hearts!

Of course: unless you become as little children, you shall not enter into that kingdom of heaven. (And Zarathustra pointed upward with his hands.)

But we do not want to enter into the kingdom of heaven at all: we have become men, so we want the kingdom of earth.”

## 3

And once more Zarathustra began to speak. “O my new friends,” he said, “you strange ones, you higher men, how well you please me now,

since you have become cheerful again! Truly, you have all blossomed: it seems to me that such flowers as you need new festivals,

a little brave nonsense, some divine service and ass festival, some old cheerful Zarathustra-fool, a roaring wind that blows your souls bright.

Do not forget this night and this ass festival, you higher men! You invented that with me; I take that as a good sign: only convalescents invent such things!

And if you celebrate it again, this ass festival, do it for love of yourselves, do it for love of me too! And in remembrance of me!”

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
