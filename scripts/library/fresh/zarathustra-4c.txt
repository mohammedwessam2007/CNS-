## On Immaculate Perception

When the moon rose yesterday, I imagined it wanted to give birth to a sun: so broad and pregnant it lay on the horizon.

But it was a liar to me with its pregnancy; and I would sooner believe in the man in the moon than in the woman.

Of course he is not much of a man either, this shy night-wanderer. Truly, he walks over the roofs with a bad conscience.

For he is lustful and jealous, the monk in the moon, lustful for the earth and for all the joys of lovers.

No, I do not like him, this tomcat on the roofs! All those who creep around half-closed windows disgust me!

Piously and silently he walks along on carpets of stars: but I do not like any soft-treading man’s feet on which not even a spur jingles.

Every honest man’s step speaks; but the cat steals away across the floor. Look, the moon comes along like a cat, and dishonestly.

This parable I give to you sentimental hypocrites, you “pure perceivers”! I call you: lustful ones!

You too love the earth and what is earthly: I have seen through you! But there is shame in your love, and a bad conscience: you are like the moon!

Your spirit has been persuaded to despise what is earthly, but not your bowels: and they are the strongest part of you!

And now your spirit is ashamed of doing what your bowels want, and goes by secret and lying paths to escape its own shame.

“For me the highest thing would be,” so your lying spirit says to itself, “to look at life without desire, and not like a dog with its tongue hanging out:

to be happy in looking, with a will that has died, without the grasp and greed of selfishness; cold and ash-grey all over the body, but with drunken moon-eyes!”

“For me the dearest thing would be,” so the seduced one seduces himself, “to love the earth as the moon loves it, and to touch its beauty with the eye alone.

And let this be called the immaculate perception of all things: that I want nothing from things, except to be allowed to lie before them like a mirror with a hundred eyes.”

Oh, you sentimental hypocrites, you lustful ones! You lack innocence in desire: and so now you slander desiring!

Truly, you do not love the earth as creators, as begetters, as those who delight in becoming!

Where is innocence? Where there is the will to beget. And whoever wants to create beyond himself has, for me, the purest will.

Where is beauty? Where I must will with my whole will; where I want to love and go under, so that an image does not remain merely an image.

Loving and going under: these have rhymed since eternity. Will to love: that means being willing to die as well. So I speak to you cowards!

But now your unmanned squinting wants to be called “contemplation”! And whatever lets itself be fingered by cowardly eyes is to be christened “beautiful”! Oh, you defilers of noble names!

But this shall be your curse, you immaculate ones, you pure perceivers, that you will never give birth, even if you lie broad and pregnant on the horizon!

Truly, you fill your mouths with noble words: and are we to believe that your hearts are overflowing, you liars?

But my words are small, despised, crooked words: I gladly pick up what falls under the table at your meals.

With them I can still tell hypocrites the truth! Yes, my fish bones, shells and prickly leaves shall tickle the noses of hypocrites!

There is always bad air around you and your meals: your lustful thoughts, your lies and secrets are in the air!

Dare first to believe yourselves: yourselves and your bowels! Whoever does not believe himself always lies.

You hung the mask of a god in front of yourselves, you “pure ones”: your horrible coiling worm crept into the mask of a god.

Truly, you deceive, you “contemplative ones”! Zarathustra too was once the fool of your divine skins; he did not guess the coils of snakes they were stuffed with.

I once imagined I saw a god’s soul playing in your games, you pure perceivers! I once imagined there was no better art than your arts!

Distance hid from me the snake-filth and the bad smell, and that a lizard’s cunning crept lustfully about here.

But I came near you: then the day came to me, and now it is coming to you; the moon’s love affair has come to an end!

Just look! There he stands, caught and pale, before the dawn!

For she is coming already, the glowing one: her love for the earth is coming! All sun-love is innocence and the creator’s desire!

Just look how impatiently she comes over the sea! Do you not feel the thirst and the hot breath of her love?

She wants to suck at the sea and drink its depth up to her height: and the desire of the sea rises with a thousand breasts.

It wants to be kissed and sucked by the sun’s thirst; it wants to become air and height and a footpath of light, and light itself!

Truly, like the sun I love life and all deep seas.

And this is what I call knowledge: everything deep shall rise up, to my height!

Thus spoke Zarathustra.

## On Scholars

As I lay asleep, a sheep ate at the ivy wreath on my head; it ate, and said as it did so: “Zarathustra is no longer a scholar.”

It said this, and went away, stiff and proud. A child told me about it.

I like to lie here, where the children play, by the broken wall, among thistles and red poppies.

To the children I am still a scholar, and to the thistles and red poppies too. They are innocent, even in their malice.

But to the sheep I am no longer one: so my lot wills it; blessed be it!

For this is the truth: I have moved out of the house of the scholars, and I slammed the door behind me too.

Too long my soul sat hungry at their table; I am not trained, as they are, for knowing as for cracking nuts.

I love freedom and the air over fresh earth; I would rather sleep on ox-hides than on their dignities and respectabilities.

I am too hot and scorched by my own thoughts: it often takes my breath away. Then I must go out into the open, away from all dusty rooms.

But they sit cool in the cool shade: in everything they want only to be spectators, and they take care not to sit where the sun burns on the steps.

Like those who stand in the street and gape at the people passing by: so they too wait and gape at thoughts that others have thought.

If you take hold of them with your hands, they give off dust like flour sacks, and without meaning to; but who would guess that their dust comes from grain, and from the yellow delight of summer fields?

When they act wise, their little sayings and truths make me shiver: there is often a smell about their wisdom, as if it came from the swamp; and truly, I have already heard the frog croaking out of it!

They are skilful, they have clever fingers: what does my simplicity want beside their complexity! Their fingers understand all threading and knotting and weaving: so they knit the stockings of the spirit!

They are good clockworks: only take care to wind them up properly! Then they show the hour without error, and make a modest noise as they do it.

They work like mills and stamping machines: just throw them your grain! They know how to grind corn small and make white dust out of it.

They watch each other’s fingers closely and do not trust one another an inch. Inventive in small cunning, they wait for those whose knowledge walks on lame feet: they wait like spiders.

I have always seen them prepare poison carefully; and they always put glass gloves on their fingers while they did it.

They know how to play with loaded dice too; and I found them playing so eagerly that they sweated.

We are strangers to one another, and their virtues go even more against my taste than their falsehoods and loaded dice.

And when I lived with them, I lived above them. They resented me for that.

They do not want to hear that anyone walks above their heads; and so they put wood and earth and rubbish between me and their heads.

So they muffled the sound of my steps: and until now I have been heard worst by the most learned.

They put all human faults and weaknesses between themselves and me: in their houses they call it a “false floor”.

But in spite of this I walk with my thoughts above their heads; and even if I wanted to walk on my own mistakes, I would still be above them and their heads.

For people are not equal: so justice speaks. And what I want, they may not want!

Thus spoke Zarathustra.

## On Poets

“Since I have come to know the body better,” said Zarathustra to one of his disciples, “the spirit is only, as it were, spirit to me; and all that is ‘imperishable’ is only a parable too.”

“I have heard you say that once before,” answered the disciple; “and then you added: ‘But the poets lie too much.’ Why did you say that the poets lie too much?”

“Why?” said Zarathustra. “You ask why? I am not one of those who may be asked for their why.

Is my experience from yesterday, then? It is long ago that I experienced the reasons for my opinions.

Would I not have to be a barrel of memory, if I wanted to have my reasons with me too?

It is already too much for me to keep my opinions themselves; and many a bird flies away.

And now and then I find a stray creature in my dovecote too, one that is strange to me, and that trembles when I lay my hand on it.

But what did Zarathustra once say to you? That the poets lie too much? But Zarathustra too is a poet.

Do you believe, then, that he spoke the truth here? Why do you believe it?”

The disciple answered: “I believe in Zarathustra.” But Zarathustra shook his head and smiled.

Faith does not make me blessed, he said, least of all faith in me.

But suppose someone said in all seriousness that the poets lie too much: he is right; we lie too much.

We also know too little and are bad learners: so we have to lie.

And which of us poets has not watered his wine? Many a poisonous hotchpotch has been made in our cellars; many an indescribable thing has been done there.

And because we know little, we are heartily fond of the poor in spirit, especially when they are young women!

And we are even greedy for the things that old women tell each other in the evenings. That is what we ourselves call the eternal feminine in us.

And as if there were a special secret way to knowledge, which is blocked to those who learn something: so we believe in the people and their “wisdom”.

But all poets believe this: that whoever lies in the grass or on lonely slopes and pricks up his ears will learn something of the things that are between heaven and earth.

And when tender feelings come to them, the poets always think that nature herself is in love with them:

and that she creeps to their ear to tell them secrets and loving flatteries: they boast of this and puff themselves up before all mortals!

Ah, there are so many things between heaven and earth that only the poets have dreamed of!

And especially above the heavens: for all gods are a poet’s parable, a poet’s trickery!

Truly, we are always drawn upward, that is, to the realm of the clouds: on these we set our motley bags of skin and then call them gods and Overmen.

For they are just light enough for these chairs, all these gods and Overmen!

Ah, how tired I am of all the inadequate that insists on being an event! Ah, how tired I am of the poets!

When Zarathustra spoke so, his disciple was angry with him, but he kept silent. And Zarathustra too was silent; and his eye had turned inward, as if it were looking into far distances. At last he sighed and drew breath.

I am of today and of before, he then said; but there is something in me that is of tomorrow and the day after tomorrow and of some day to come.

I grew tired of the poets, the old and the new: they are all superficial to me, and shallow seas.

They did not think deeply enough: that is why their feeling did not sink to the bottom.

A little lust and a little boredom: that has still been their best reflection.

All their harp-jingling is to me the breath and flitting of ghosts; what have they ever known of the fervour of sounds!

They are not clean enough for me either: they all muddy their waters, so that they may seem deep.

And they like to present themselves as reconcilers: but to me they remain go-betweens and mixers, half-and-halfs and unclean!

Ah, I cast my net into their seas, and wanted to catch good fish; but I always pulled up the head of some old god.

So the sea gave the hungry man a stone. And they themselves may well come from the sea.

Certainly, one finds pearls in them: all the more are they themselves like hard shellfish. And instead of a soul I have often found salty slime in them.

They have learned its vanity from the sea too: is not the sea the peacock of peacocks?

Even before the ugliest of all buffaloes it spreads out its tail; it never tires of its lace fan of silver and silk.

The buffalo looks on defiantly, close to the sand in his soul, closer still to the thicket, but closest of all to the swamp.

What are beauty and sea and peacock finery to him! This parable I tell the poets.

Truly, their spirit itself is the peacock of peacocks and a sea of vanity!

The poet’s spirit wants spectators, even if they are buffaloes!

But I grew tired of this spirit; and I see the time coming when it will grow tired of itself.

I have already seen the poets changed, and turning their gaze against themselves.

I have seen penitents of the spirit coming: they grew out of the poets.

Thus spoke Zarathustra.

## On Great Events

There is an island in the sea, not far from Zarathustra’s blessed isles, on which a volcano constantly smokes; of it the people say, and especially the old women among the people, that it has been set like a boulder before the gate of the underworld; and through the volcano itself the narrow path leads downward to this gate of the underworld.

Now at the time when Zarathustra was staying on the blessed isles, it happened that a ship cast anchor at the island where the smoking mountain stands; and its crew went ashore to shoot rabbits. But towards the hour of noon, when the captain and his men were together again, they suddenly saw a man coming towards them through the air, and a voice said clearly: “It is time! It is high time!” But when the figure was nearest to them (it flew past quickly like a shadow, in the direction of the volcano), they recognised with the greatest dismay that it was Zarathustra; for they had all seen him before, except the captain himself, and they loved him as the people love: that is, with love and awe in equal parts.

“Look there!” said the old helmsman. “There goes Zarathustra to hell!”

At the same time as these sailors landed on the fire island, the rumour went round that Zarathustra had disappeared; and when his friends were asked, they said he had gone on board a ship by night, without saying where he meant to travel.

So there was some unrest; and after three days the sailors’ story was added to this unrest, and now all the people said that the devil had taken Zarathustra. His disciples laughed at this talk, it is true; and one of them even said: “I would sooner believe that Zarathustra has taken the devil.” But at the bottom of their souls they were all full of worry and longing: so their joy was great when on the fifth day Zarathustra appeared among them.

And this is the story of Zarathustra’s conversation with the fire-dog.

The earth, he said, has a skin; and this skin has diseases. One of these diseases, for example, is called “man”.

And another of these diseases is called “fire-dog”: about him people have told themselves many lies, and let themselves be told many.

To get to the bottom of this secret I went over the sea; and I have seen the truth naked, truly! barefoot up to the neck.

I know now what the fire-dog is about; and likewise all the devils of eruption and overthrow, of whom it is not only old women who are afraid.

“Come out, fire-dog, out of your depth!” I cried, “and confess how deep this depth is! Where does it come from, what you are snorting up there?

You drink plentifully from the sea: your salty eloquence gives that away! Indeed, for a dog of the depths you take your food too much from the surface!

At most I take you for the ventriloquist of the earth: and whenever I have heard devils of overthrow and eruption speak, I have found them like you: salty, lying and shallow.

You know how to roar and to darken things with ashes! You are the best of braggarts, and you have learned well enough the art of making mud boil.

Wherever you are, there must always be mud nearby, and much that is spongy, hollow, squeezed in: it wants to be free.

‘Freedom’ is what you all like best to roar: but I have unlearned belief in ‘great events’ whenever there is much roaring and smoke around them.

And believe me, friend Hellish Racket! The greatest events are not our loudest but our stillest hours.

Not around the inventors of new noise, but around the inventors of new values does the world turn; it turns inaudibly.

And just admit it! Little had ever happened when your noise and smoke cleared away. What does it matter that a city became a mummy and a statue lies in the mud!

And I say this word too to those who overthrow statues. It is surely the greatest folly to throw salt into the sea and statues into the mud.

The statue lay in the mud of your contempt: but it is just its law that out of contempt its life and living beauty grow again!

With more divine features it rises now, and seductive in its suffering; and truly! it will yet thank you for overthrowing it, you overthrowers!

But this advice I give to kings and churches and everything that is weak with age and virtue: just let yourselves be overthrown! So that you may come back to life, and virtue come back to you!”

So I spoke before the fire-dog: then he interrupted me sulkily and asked: “Church? What is that?”

“Church?” I answered. “That is a kind of state, and the most deceitful kind. But be quiet, you hypocrite dog! You surely know your own kind best already!

Like yourself, the state is a hypocrite dog; like you it likes to speak with smoke and roaring, to make people believe, like you, that it speaks from the belly of things.

For the state wants at all costs to be the most important animal on earth; and people believe it, too.”

When I had said this, the fire-dog behaved as if mad with envy. “What?” he cried. “The most important animal on earth? And people believe it, too?” And so much steam and such horrible voices came out of his throat that I thought he would choke with anger and envy.

At last he grew quieter, and his panting eased; but as soon as he was quiet, I said, laughing:

“You are angry, fire-dog: so I am right about you!

And so that I may stay right, hear about another fire-dog: he really speaks from the heart of the earth.

His breath breathes gold and golden rain: so his heart wills it. What are ashes and smoke and hot slime to him now!

Laughter flutters out of him like a bright cloud; he is averse to your gurgling and spitting and the grumbling of your bowels!

But the gold and the laughter he takes from the heart of the earth: for, just so that you know it, the heart of the earth is of gold.”

When the fire-dog heard this, he could not bear to listen to me any longer. Ashamed, he drew in his tail, said in a small voice “Bow-wow!”, and crept down into his cave.

So Zarathustra told it. But his disciples hardly listened to him: so great was their eagerness to tell him about the sailors, the rabbits and the flying man.

“What am I to make of it!” said Zarathustra. “Am I a ghost, then?

But it must have been my shadow. You have surely heard something already of the wanderer and his shadow?

But this much is certain: I must keep it on a shorter leash; otherwise it will spoil my reputation yet.”

And once more Zarathustra shook his head and wondered. “What am I to make of it!” he said again.

“Why did the ghost cry: ‘It is time! It is high time!’

For what, then, is it high time?”

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
