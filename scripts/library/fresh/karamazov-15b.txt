## (d) The Mysterious Visitor

He had been in government service in our town for a long time and held a prominent position; he was a man respected by all, rich, famous for his charity; he had given a considerable sum to the almshouse and the orphanage and, besides, had done many good deeds in secret, without publicity, all of which came out after his death. He was about fifty, with an almost stern look, and spoke little; he had been married no more than ten years, to a wife who was still young, and had three small children by her. Well, the next evening I was sitting at home, when suddenly my door opens and this very gentleman comes in.

I must note that I was no longer living in my old lodgings by then; as soon as I sent in my papers I had moved to others, renting from an old woman, a civil servant’s widow, with her servant; and I had moved to these lodgings only because on the very day I came back from the duel I had sent Afanasy back to his company, for I was ashamed to look him in the eyes after what I had done to him: so inclined is an unprepared man of the world to be ashamed even of some of his most righteous deeds.

“I have been listening to you,” says the gentleman who has come in, “for several days in various houses, with great curiosity, and I wished at last to make your personal acquaintance, so as to talk with you in more detail. Can you do me, my dear sir, so great a service?” “I can,” I say, “with the greatest pleasure, and shall count it a special honour.” I say this to him, but I was almost frightened, so much did he strike me from the first moment. For though people listened to me and were curious, no one had yet approached me with such a serious and stern inner look. And this man had even come to my lodgings himself. He sat down. “I see,” he goes on, “a great strength of character in you, for you were not afraid to serve the truth in a matter where you risked bearing the general contempt of everyone for your truth.” “Perhaps you are praising me much too highly,” I say to him. “No, not too highly,” he answers me. “Believe me, to do such a thing is much harder than you think. It is really just that,” he goes on, “that struck me, and that is why I have come to you. Describe to me, if you are not repelled by my perhaps indecent curiosity, what exactly you felt at the moment when you decided to ask forgiveness at the duel, if you remember. Don’t think my question frivolous; on the contrary, in asking it I have a secret purpose of my own, which I shall probably explain to you later, if it pleases God to bring us closer.”

All the time he was saying this I looked him straight in the face, and suddenly I felt the strongest trust in him, and besides that an extraordinary curiosity on my part too, for I felt that there was some special secret of his own in his soul.

“You ask what exactly I felt at the moment when I asked my adversary’s forgiveness,” I answer him, “but I had better tell you from the very beginning what I have not told others yet,” and I told him everything that had happened between me and Afanasy, and how I had bowed down to the ground before him. “From this you can see for yourself,” I concluded, “that during the duel it was already easier for me, for I had begun at home, and once I had set out on that road, all the rest went not only without difficulty but even joyfully and gaily.”

He heard me out and looked at me so kindly: “All this,” he says, “is extremely interesting; I shall come to you again and again.” And from then on he began coming to me almost every evening. And we would have become great friends if he had told me about himself too. But about himself he said hardly a word; he kept asking me about myself. In spite of that I came to love him very much and trusted him completely with all my feelings, for I thought: what are his secrets to me? I can see without them that he is a righteous man. Besides, he was so serious a man and so much older than I, and yet he came to me, a youth, and did not disdain me. And I learned much that was useful from him, for he was a man of high intelligence. “That life is paradise,” he says to me suddenly, “I have been thinking about that for a long time,” and suddenly he added: “It is all I think about.” He looks at me and smiles. “I am more convinced of it than you are,” he says; “later you will learn why.” I listen to this and think to myself: “He surely wants to reveal something to me.” “Paradise,” he says, “lies hidden in each of us; here it is hidden in me now, and, if I want, it will come for me tomorrow in reality, and for the rest of my life.” I look at him: he speaks with tender feeling and looks at me mysteriously, as if questioning me. “And as for every man being guilty for all and for everything, besides his own sins,” he continues, “you judged that quite rightly, and it is wonderful how you were able all at once to grasp the thought so fully. And it is truly so: when people understand this thought, the kingdom of heaven will come for them, no longer in dreams but in reality.” “But when,” I exclaimed to him with sorrow, “will that come true, and will it ever come true? Is it not only a dream?” “There, you don’t believe,” he says. “You preach and you don’t believe yourself. Know then that this dream, as you call it, will come true without doubt; believe that; but not now, for every action has its law. It is a matter of the soul, a psychological matter. To remake the world anew, people must themselves turn psychologically onto another road. Until you have really become a brother to everyone, brotherhood will not come. People will never, by any science or any advantage, manage to share out their property and their rights without offence. Everything will be too little for each, and they will all murmur, envy and destroy one another. You ask when it will come true. It will come true, but first the period of human isolation must come to an end.” “What isolation?” I ask him. “Why, the isolation that reigns everywhere now, and especially in our age; but it has not yet run its full course, and its time has not yet come. For everyone now strives to set his own person apart as far as possible, wants to experience the fullness of life in himself alone; and yet all his efforts lead, instead of to the fullness of life, only to complete suicide, for instead of the full definition of their being they fall into complete isolation. For in our age all have split into units; each withdraws into his hole; each keeps away from the other, hides himself and hides what he has, and ends by pushing himself away from people and pushing people away from himself. He piles up riches in isolation and thinks: how strong I am now and how secure; and the madman does not know that the more he piles up, the deeper he sinks into a suicidal impotence. For he has grown used to relying on himself alone and has split off from the whole as a unit; he has taught his soul not to believe in the help of others, in people and in mankind, and only trembles lest his money and the rights he has acquired be lost. Everywhere now the mind of man begins, mockingly, not to understand that the true security of the person lies not in his own isolated effort but in the common wholeness of mankind. But it will surely come to pass that this terrible isolation too will come to an end, and all will understand at once how unnaturally they have separated from one another. Such will be the spirit of the time, and they will wonder that they sat so long in darkness and did not see the light. And then the sign of the Son of Man will appear in the heavens… But until then one must still guard the banner, and now and then a man must, if only alone, suddenly set an example and draw his soul out of isolation to the great work of brotherly communion, even if in the guise of a holy fool. So that the great idea does not die…”

In such fiery and rapturous conversations our evenings passed one after another. I even gave up society and began appearing much more rarely as a guest, besides which the fashion for me began to pass. I say this not in reproach, for they went on loving me and treating me cheerfully; but that fashion is no small queen in the world must, all the same, be admitted. My mysterious visitor I came at last to regard with admiration, for besides delighting in his mind I began to feel that he was nursing some plan within him and preparing for a great, perhaps heroic deed. Perhaps he liked it too that I did not outwardly show curiosity about his secret, nor question him about it either directly or by hints. But I noticed at last that he himself seemed to be beginning to long to reveal something to me. At least this became very evident about a month after he began visiting me. “Do you know,” he asked me once, “that the town is very curious about us both and wonders that I come to you so often? But let them, for soon everything will be explained.” Sometimes an extraordinary agitation would suddenly come over him, and almost always in such cases he would get up and go. Sometimes he would look at me for a long time, piercingly as it were, and I would think: “He is going to say something now,” but he would suddenly break off and begin to talk of something familiar and ordinary. He began to complain often of headaches too. And then once, quite unexpectedly, after he had been talking long and fervently, I saw him suddenly turn pale; his face was quite distorted, and he stared at me point-blank.

“What is the matter with you?” I say. “Aren’t you feeling unwell?”

He had, in fact, been complaining of a headache.

“I… do you know… I… killed a man.”

He said it and smiled, and he was as white as chalk. “Why is he smiling?” the thought suddenly pierced my heart before I had grasped anything. I turned pale myself.

“What are you saying?” I cry to him.

“You see,” he answers me, still with a pale smile, “how dearly it has cost me to say the first word. Now I have said it, and I seem to have set out on the road. I shall go on.”

For a long time I did not believe him, and I did not believe him all at once, but only after he had come to me for three days and told me everything in detail. I thought him mad, but ended by being convinced at last, plainly, with very great sorrow and amazement. He had committed a great and terrible crime fourteen years before, against a rich lady, young and beautiful, a widowed landowner who kept a house of her own in our town for her visits. Feeling a great love for her, he had declared his love and begun to urge her to marry him. But she had already given her heart to another, a distinguished military man of high rank, who was away on campaign at the time and whom she was expecting back soon. She refused his offer and asked him not to visit her. Having stopped visiting, he, knowing the arrangement of her house, made his way into it one night from the garden over the roof, with extraordinary audacity, risking discovery. But, as very often happens, crimes committed with extraordinary audacity succeed more often than others. Entering the attic of the house by a dormer window, he went down to her into the living rooms by the little attic stairs, knowing that the door at the bottom of the stairs, through the servants’ carelessness, was not always locked. He counted on this carelessness this time too, and was right. Having made his way into the living rooms, he went in the dark into her bedroom, where an icon lamp was burning. And, as if on purpose, both her maids had quietly gone out without leave to a name-day party in the same street. The rest of the servants, men and women, were asleep in the servants’ quarters and the kitchen on the ground floor. At the sight of the sleeping woman passion flared up in him, and then a vengeful, jealous fury seized his heart, and, beside himself, as if drunk, he went up and plunged a knife straight into her heart, so that she did not even cry out. Then, with a hellish and most criminal calculation, he arranged things so that the servants would be suspected: he did not scruple to take her purse; with keys he took from under her pillow he opened her chest of drawers and took some things from it, exactly as an ignorant servant would have done, that is, he left the securities and took only the money, took a few of the larger gold things, and passed over little things ten times more precious. He took something else besides, as a keepsake, but of that later. Having done this terrible deed, he left the way he had come. Neither on the next day, when the alarm was raised, nor ever afterwards in his whole life, did it enter anyone’s head to suspect the real criminal! And no one knew of his love for her, for he had always been of a silent and uncommunicative character and had no friend to whom he confided his soul. He was regarded simply as an acquaintance of the murdered woman, and not even a very close one, for in the last two weeks he had not visited her. Suspicion fell at once on her serf Pyotr, and all the circumstances came together to confirm the suspicion, for this servant knew, and the dead woman had not concealed it, that she meant to send him for a soldier, as part of the recruits due from her peasants, since he was a single man and of bad conduct besides. He had been heard threatening in a tavern, drunk and furious, to kill her. Two days before her death he had run away and was living somewhere in the town in unknown places. The day after the murder he was found on the road at the edge of the town, dead drunk, with a knife in his pocket, and his right palm, for some reason, stained with blood. He insisted that the blood was from his nose, but no one believed him. The maids confessed that they had been at the party and that the front door had stayed unlocked until they came back. And there were many more signs of the same kind besides, on account of which the innocent servant was seized. He was arrested and the trial began, but exactly a week later the prisoner fell ill with a fever and died in the hospital without regaining consciousness. With that the case ended; it was left to the will of God, and everyone, the judges, the authorities and the whole of society, remained convinced that the crime had been committed by none other than the dead servant. And after that the punishment began.

The mysterious visitor, now my friend, told me that at first he did not suffer pangs of conscience at all. He suffered for a long time, but not from that, only from regret that he had killed the woman he loved, that she was no more, that in killing her he had killed his love, while the fire of passion was still in his blood. But of the innocent blood shed, of the murder of a human being, he hardly thought then at all. The thought that his victim might have become another man’s wife seemed impossible to him, and so for a long time he was convinced in his conscience that he could not have acted otherwise. At first the servant’s arrest weighed on him somewhat, but the prisoner’s quick illness and then his death set his mind at rest, for the man had died, to all appearances (so he reasoned then), not of the arrest or of fright but of a chill caught precisely in the days when he was on the run, when, dead drunk, he had lain a whole night on the damp ground. The stolen things and money troubled him little, for (he reasoned in the same way) the theft had been done not for gain but to turn suspicion elsewhere. The sum stolen was insignificant, and he soon gave all of it, and even much more, to the almshouse being founded in our town. He did this on purpose to set his conscience at rest about the theft, and, remarkably, for a time, and even a long time, he really was at rest; he told me so himself. He threw himself then into great activity in his service; he asked for a troublesome and difficult commission himself, which occupied him for about two years, and, being of a strong character, he almost forgot what had happened; and when he did remember it, he tried not to think of it at all. He threw himself into charity too; he set up and gave much in our town, and made his name in the capitals as well, being elected a member of charitable societies in Moscow and Petersburg. But all the same he began at last to brood, with a torment beyond his strength. Then a beautiful and sensible girl took his fancy, and he soon married her, dreaming that by marriage he would drive away his solitary anguish, and that, entering on a new road and zealously doing his duty by his wife and children, he would get away from his old memories altogether. But exactly the opposite of that expectation happened. Even in the first month of the marriage a constant thought began to trouble him: “Here my wife loves me; but what if she knew?” When she became pregnant with their first child and told him so, he was suddenly troubled: “I am giving life, and I have taken life.” Children came: “How dare I love them, teach them and bring them up? How shall I speak to them of virtue? I have shed blood.” The children were growing up beautifully; he wanted to caress them: “But I cannot look at their innocent, bright faces; I am unworthy of it.” At last the blood of his murdered victim began to haunt him menacingly and bitterly, her ruined young life, the blood crying out for vengeance. He began to have terrible dreams. But, being firm of heart, he bore the torment for a long time: “I shall redeem everything by this secret torment of mine.” But that hope too was in vain: the further it went, the stronger the suffering became. In society they came to respect him for his charitable work, though everyone was afraid of his stern and gloomy character; but the more they respected him, the more unbearable it became for him. He confessed to me that he had thought of killing himself. But instead another dream began to haunt him, a dream he thought at first impossible and mad, but which at last fastened itself to his heart so that it could not be torn away. This was his dream: to rise up, go out before the people and announce to everyone that he had killed a human being. For about three years he went about with this dream; it haunted him in various forms. At last he believed with all his heart that by declaring his crime he would heal his soul without doubt and be at peace once for all. But having believed it, he felt horror in his heart, for how was he to do it? And suddenly this incident of my duel happened. “Looking at you, I have now made up my mind.” I look at him.

“And can it be,” I exclaimed to him, throwing up my hands, “that so small an incident could give birth to such a resolve in you?”

“My resolve has been three years being born,” he answers me, “and your incident only gave it a push. Looking at you, I reproached myself and envied you,” he said to me, even with sternness.

“But they won’t believe you,” I observed to him. “Fourteen years have passed.”

“I have proofs, great ones. I shall present them.”

And I wept then and kissed him.

“Decide one thing for me, one thing!” he said to me (as though everything now depended on me). “My wife, my children! My wife may die of grief, and my children, though they will not lose their rank and property, will be a convict’s children, and for ever. And the memory, the memory of me I shall leave in their hearts!”

I am silent.

“And to part with them, to leave them for ever? For it is for ever, for ever!”

I sit, whispering a prayer to myself in silence. At last I got up; I was frightened.

“Well?” he looks at me.

“Go,” I say, “and declare it to people. Everything will pass; only the truth will remain. The children will understand, when they grow up, how much magnanimity there was in your great resolve.”

He left me then as though he had really made up his mind. Yet for more than two weeks after that he came to me every evening without a break, still preparing himself, still unable to bring himself to it. He wore my heart out. Now he would come in firm and say with tender feeling:

“I know that paradise will come for me; it will come the moment I declare it. For fourteen years I have been in hell. I want to suffer. I shall take my suffering and begin to live. You can go through the world on a lie, but you cannot come back. Now I dare not love not only my neighbour but even my own children. Lord, perhaps the children will understand what my suffering has cost me, and will not condemn me! God is not in might but in truth.”

“Everyone will understand your great deed,” I say to him. “If not now, they will understand later, for you have served the truth, the higher truth, not of this earth…”

And he would leave me as if comforted, and the next day he would suddenly come again, spiteful and pale, and say mockingly:

“Every time I come in, you look at me with such curiosity: ‘So he hasn’t declared it again?’ Wait, don’t despise me too much. It isn’t as easy to do as you think. Perhaps I won’t do it at all. You won’t go and inform on me then, eh?”

And I, so far from looking at him with foolish curiosity, was afraid even to glance at him. I was worn out to the point of illness, and my soul was full of tears. I even lost my sleep at night.

“I have just come from my wife,” he goes on. “Do you understand what a wife is? When I was leaving, the children called out to me: ‘Goodbye, papa, come back soon and read Children’s Reading with us.’ No, you don’t understand this! Another man’s trouble doesn’t teach you sense.”

His eyes flashed, his lips twitched. Suddenly he struck the table with his fist so that the things on it jumped. Such a gentle man; it was the first time it had happened to him.

“But is it necessary?” he cried. “Is it needed? No one was convicted, no one was sent to penal servitude because of me; the servant died of illness. And for the blood I shed I have been punished with torments. And they won’t believe me at all; they won’t believe any of my proofs. Must I declare it, must I? For the blood I shed I am ready to go on suffering all my life, only so as not to strike down my wife and children. Will it be just to ruin them along with me? Aren’t we mistaken? Where is the truth here? And will people know this truth, value it, respect it?”

“Lord!” I think to myself. “He is thinking of people’s respect at such a moment!” And I felt so sorry for him then that I think I would have shared his lot myself if only to ease it for him. I see that he is as if in a frenzy. I was horrified, understanding now not with my mind alone but with my living soul what such a resolve costs.

“Decide my fate!” he cried again.

“Go and declare it,” I whispered to him. My voice failed me, but I whispered it firmly. Then I took the Gospel from the table, the Russian translation, and showed him John, chapter twelve, verse twenty-four:

“Verily, verily, I say unto you, Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit.” I had read that verse just before he came.

He read it.

“True,” he says, but smiled bitterly. “Yes, in these books,” he says after a pause, “you come across horrors. It’s easy to shove them under people’s noses. And who wrote them? Can it have been men?”

“The Holy Spirit wrote them,” I say.

“It’s easy for you to chatter,” he smiled again, but almost with hatred now. I took the book again, opened it at another place and showed him Hebrews, chapter ten, verse thirty-one. He read: “It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God.”

He read it and flung the book aside. He was trembling all over.

“A fearful verse,” he says. “No denying it, you have picked one.” He got up from his chair. “Well,” he says, “goodbye; perhaps I won’t come again… we shall meet in paradise. So for fourteen years I have already ‘fallen into the hands of the living God’; that is what these fourteen years are called, it seems. Tomorrow I shall ask those hands to let me go…”

I wanted to embrace and kiss him, but I did not dare: his face was so twisted and his look was so heavy. He went out. “Lord,” I thought, “where has the man gone?” I threw myself on my knees then before the icon and wept for him to the most holy Mother of God, the swift intercessor and helper. Half an hour passed as I stood in tears at prayer, and it was already late at night, about twelve. Suddenly I see the door open, and he comes in again. I was amazed.

“Where have you been?” I ask him.

“I,” he says, “I think I forgot something… my handkerchief, I think… Well, even if I didn’t forget anything, let me sit down…”

He sat down on a chair. I stand over him. “Sit down too,” he says. I sat down. We sat for about two minutes; he looked at me intently and suddenly smiled (I remembered that); then he got up, embraced me tightly and kissed me…

“Remember,” he says, “how I came to you the second time. Do you hear, remember it!”

It was the first time he had called me “thou”. And he went out. “Tomorrow,” I thought.

And so it came to pass. I did not know that evening that the next day happened to be his birthday. I had not gone out anywhere in the last days, and so I could not have learned it from anyone. On that day every year there was a great gathering at his house; the whole town came. They came now too. And then, after dinner, he comes out into the middle of the room with a paper in his hands, a formal report to his superiors. And since his superiors were right there, he read the paper aloud then and there to all who were gathered, and in it was a full description of the whole crime in every detail. “I cast myself out from among men as a monster; God has visited me,” he concluded the paper. “I want to suffer!” Then and there he brought out and laid on the table everything by which he thought to prove his crime and which he had kept for fourteen years: the murdered woman’s gold things, which he had taken to turn suspicion from himself; her locket and cross, taken from her neck (in the locket, a portrait of her betrothed); her notebook; and lastly two letters: her betrothed’s letter to her announcing his speedy arrival, and her answer to that letter, which she had begun and not finished and left on the table to send to the post the next day. He had taken both letters with him. Why? Why had he kept them afterwards for fourteen years instead of destroying them as evidence? And what happened? Everyone was amazed and horrified, and no one wanted to believe it, though everyone listened with extraordinary curiosity, but as to a sick man; and a few days later it was decided and settled in every house that the unhappy man had gone out of his mind. The authorities and the court could not but take the matter up, but they too held back: though the things and letters presented did make them think, it was decided there too that even if these documents proved genuine, a final conviction could not be pronounced on the strength of these documents alone. Besides, he might have had all the things from her herself, as her acquaintance and in trust. I heard, however, that the genuineness of the things was later verified through many acquaintances and relatives of the murdered woman and that there was no doubt about it. But again this case was not destined to be concluded. About five days later everyone learned that the sufferer had fallen ill and that his life was feared for. What illness he fell ill with I cannot explain; they said it was a disorder of the heart; but it became known that a council of doctors, at his wife’s insistence, had examined his mental state too and concluded that there was already madness. I gave nothing away, though people rushed to question me; but when I wanted to visit him, I was kept away for a long time, chiefly by his wife. “It was you,” she tells me, “who upset him. He was gloomy before, and in the last year everyone noticed an extraordinary agitation in him and strange behaviour, and then you ruined him; it was you who talked him into it; he never left your rooms for a whole month.” And what happened? Not only his wife, but everyone in the town fell upon me and blamed me: “It’s all your doing,” they said. I keep silent, and in my soul I am glad, for I have beheld the unmistakable mercy of God to a man who rose up against himself and punished himself. And I could not believe in his madness. At last they let me in to him too; he had demanded it insistently himself, to take leave of me. I went in and saw at once that not only his days but his hours were numbered. He was weak and yellow, his hands trembling, he was gasping for breath, but he looked tender and joyful.

“It is accomplished!” he said to me. “I have long been longing to see you; why didn’t you come?”

I did not tell him that I had not been allowed in to him.

“God has had pity on me and is calling me to himself. I know I am dying, but I feel joy and peace for the first time after so many years. I felt paradise in my soul at once, as soon as I had done what had to be done. Now I dare to love my children and kiss them. They don’t believe me, and no one has believed me, neither my wife nor my judges; and my children will never believe it. I see God’s mercy to my children in that. I shall die, and my name will be unstained for them. And now I feel God near; my heart rejoices as in paradise… I have done my duty…”

He cannot speak; he gasps for breath; he presses my hand warmly and looks at me with fire. But we did not talk long; his wife kept looking in on us. But he did manage to whisper to me:

“And do you remember how I came back to you that second time, at midnight? And told you to remember it? Do you know why I came in? I came to kill you!”

I simply shuddered.

“I went out from you then into the darkness, wandered about the streets and fought with myself. And suddenly I hated you so that my heart could hardly bear it. ‘Now,’ I thought, ‘he alone has bound me, and he is my judge; I can no longer refuse tomorrow’s punishment, for he knows everything.’ And not that I was afraid you would inform (there was not a thought of that), but I thought: ‘How shall I look at him if I don’t declare myself?’ And even if you had been at the ends of the earth, but alive, it would have been all the same: the thought was unbearable that you were alive and knew everything and were judging me. I hated you as if you were the cause of everything and to blame for everything. I came back to you then; I remember there was a dagger lying on your table. I sat down and asked you to sit down, and for a whole minute I was thinking. If I had killed you, I should have been ruined for that murder all the same, even if I had not declared my former crime. But I did not think of that at all, and did not want to think of it at that moment. I only hated you and wanted with all my strength to take revenge on you for everything. But my Lord overcame the devil in my heart. Know, though, that you were never nearer to death.”

A week later he died. The whole town followed his coffin to the grave. The archpriest gave a heartfelt address. They mourned the terrible illness that had cut short his days. But the whole town rose against me when they had buried him, and they even stopped receiving me. True, some, few at first, and then more and more, began to believe in the truth of his confession, and began visiting me a great deal and questioning me with great curiosity and joy: for man loves the fall of the righteous and his disgrace. But I fell silent and soon left the town altogether, and five months later I was found worthy by the Lord God to set out on the firm and goodly path, blessing the unseen finger that had so plainly shown me this path. And the much-suffering servant of God Mikhail I remember in my prayers every day to this day.
