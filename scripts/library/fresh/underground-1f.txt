## VIII

“Ha, ha, ha! But when you come down to it, wanting doesn’t even exist, if you like!” you interrupt me, laughing. “Science has already managed to dissect man so thoroughly that even now we know that wanting and so-called free will are nothing but...”

Wait, gentlemen, I was going to begin like that myself. I confess I even got frightened. I was just about to shout that, after all, the devil only knows what wanting depends on, and that perhaps we should thank God for that; but then I remembered science and... sat down. And that is when you started talking. For really, well, if one day they truly find the formula for all our wants and caprices, that is, what they depend on, by what laws exactly they arise, how exactly they spread, where they tend in such and such a case, and so on and so on, that is, a real mathematical formula, then man will perhaps at once stop wanting; indeed, he will very likely stop for certain. What pleasure is there in wanting by a table? More than that: he will at once turn from a man into an organ stop or something of the kind; for what is a man without desires, without will and without wants, if not a stop on the cylinder of a barrel organ? What do you think? Let us work out the probabilities: can it happen or not?

“Hm...” you decide, “our wants are mostly mistaken because of a mistaken view of our advantages. The reason we sometimes want pure nonsense is that, in our stupidity, we see in this nonsense the easiest way to reach some advantage we had assumed in advance. Well, and when all this has been explained and calculated on paper (which is quite possible, since it is vile and senseless to believe in advance that man will never discover certain laws of nature), then of course there will be no so-called desires any more. For if wanting ever comes completely to terms with reason, then we shall reason instead of wanting, precisely because it is impossible, for example, while keeping one’s reason, to want nonsense and so knowingly go against reason and wish oneself harm... And since all wants and reasonings really can be calculated, because one day the laws of our so-called free will are bound to be discovered, then, joking aside, something like a table really may be arranged, so that we really shall want according to this table. For if one day they calculate and prove to me, for example, that if I thumbed my nose at so-and-so, it was precisely because I could not help thumbing my nose at him, and that I had to do it with exactly that finger, then what will be left in me that is free, especially if I am an educated man who has finished a course of studies somewhere? Why, then I could calculate my whole life thirty years ahead; in a word, if this is arranged, there will be nothing left for us to do; we shall have to accept it anyway. And in general we must tirelessly repeat to ourselves that at such and such a moment and in such and such circumstances nature does not ask our permission; that we must accept her as she is and not as we imagine her; and if we really are heading towards a table and a calendar, and... well, even towards a test tube, then what can we do, we must accept the test tube too! Otherwise it will accept itself, without you...”

Yes, sir, but this is exactly where I get stuck! Gentlemen, excuse me for philosophising; it is forty years of the underground! Allow me to let my fancy run. You see, gentlemen: reason is a fine thing, that is beyond dispute; but reason is only reason and satisfies only the reasoning part of man, while wanting is the expression of the whole of life, that is, the whole of human life, reason and all the head-scratching included. And although our life, expressed this way, often turns out to be a rather trashy business, it is still life, and not just the extraction of square roots. I, for example, quite naturally want to live in order to satisfy my whole capacity for living, and not in order to satisfy only my capacity for reasoning, that is, some one-twentieth part of my whole capacity for living. What does reason know? Reason knows only what it has managed to learn (and some things it perhaps will never learn; that is no comfort, but why not say it?), while human nature acts as a whole, with everything that is in it, consciously and unconsciously, and even if it lies, it lives.

I suspect, gentlemen, that you are looking at me with pity; you repeat to me that an enlightened and developed man, in a word, the kind of man the future man will be, cannot knowingly want something that is to his disadvantage; that this is mathematics. I entirely agree: it really is mathematics. But I repeat to you for the hundredth time, there is one case, only one, when man may deliberately, consciously wish for himself something even harmful, stupid, even the stupidest thing: namely, in order to have the right to wish for himself even the stupidest thing, and not to be bound by the duty to wish only for what is clever. For this stupidest thing, this caprice of ours, may really, gentlemen, be more advantageous for people like us than anything else on earth, especially in certain cases. And in particular it may be more advantageous than all advantages even when it does us obvious harm and contradicts the soundest conclusions of our reason about advantage, because in any case it preserves for us what is most important and most precious, that is, our personality and our individuality. Some people do indeed claim that this is what is most precious of all to man. Wanting can, of course, agree with reason if it wants to, especially if one does not abuse this but uses it in moderation; that is useful and even sometimes praiseworthy. But very often, and even mostly, wanting completely and stubbornly disagrees with reason, and... and... and do you know that this too is useful, and even sometimes very praiseworthy?

Gentlemen, let us suppose that man is not stupid. (And indeed one cannot possibly say that of him, if only because, if he is stupid, then who is clever?) But even if he is not stupid, he is still monstrously ungrateful! Phenomenally ungrateful. I even think that the best definition of man is: a creature that goes on two legs and is ungrateful. But that is not all; that is not yet his chief failing. His chief failing is his constant bad behaviour, constant from the Flood to the Schleswig-Holstein period of human destiny. Bad behaviour, and therefore bad sense; for it has long been known that bad sense comes from nothing but bad behaviour. Just try casting a glance at the history of mankind; well, what will you see? Is it majestic? Majestic, perhaps; the Colossus of Rhodes alone, for example, is worth something! Not for nothing does Mr Anaevsky testify that some say it is the work of human hands, while others claim it was created by nature herself. Is it colourful? Colourful, perhaps; just sorting out the dress uniforms, military and civilian, of all ages and all peoples is worth something, and with the everyday uniforms thrown in you could break your leg; no historian could keep his feet. Is it monotonous? Well, monotonous too, perhaps: they fight and fight, they fight now, they fought before and they fought after; you must agree that this is even too monotonous. In a word, you can say anything about world history, anything that might enter the most disordered imagination. There is only one thing you cannot say: that it is sensible. You would choke on the first word.

And here is the kind of thing you come across every minute: there are always people appearing in life who are so well-behaved and so sensible, such sages and lovers of the human race, who make it their aim all their lives to behave as well and as sensibly as possible, to be a light, so to speak, to their neighbours, precisely in order to prove to them that it really is possible to live in the world both well-behaved and sensible. And what happens? It is well known that many of these lovers of mankind, sooner or later, towards the end of their lives, betrayed themselves by producing some scandalous anecdote, sometimes even of the most indecent kind. Now I ask you: what can you expect of man, a creature endowed with such strange qualities? Shower him with every earthly blessing, drown him in happiness right over his head, so that only bubbles rise to the surface of happiness, as on water; give him such economic prosperity that there is nothing left for him to do but sleep, eat gingerbread and busy himself with keeping world history going; and even then, out of sheer ingratitude, out of sheer spite, man will play you some nasty trick. He will even risk his gingerbread and deliberately want the most ruinous rubbish, the most uneconomical nonsense, solely in order to mix into all this positive good sense his own ruinous, fantastic element. It is precisely his fantastic dreams, his most vulgar stupidity, that he will want to keep, solely in order to confirm to himself (as if that were so very necessary) that people are still people and not piano keys, on which the laws of nature themselves may be playing with their own hands, but which they threaten to play so thoroughly that nobody will be able to want anything not in the calendar.

And that is not all: even if he really did turn out to be a piano key, even if this were proved to him by the natural sciences and mathematically, he would still not come to his senses, but would deliberately do something contrary, out of sheer ingratitude, simply to have his own way. And if he finds he has no means, he will invent destruction and chaos, invent all kinds of suffering, and have his own way after all! He will send a curse out over the world, and since only man can curse (that is his privilege, the chief thing that distinguishes him from the other animals), he may achieve his goal by the curse alone, that is, really convince himself that he is a man and not a piano key! If you say that all this too can be calculated by a table, chaos and darkness and curse, so that the mere possibility of calculating it in advance will stop everything and reason will win, then man will deliberately go mad for the occasion, so as to be rid of reason and have his own way! I believe in this, I answer for it, because the whole business of man, it seems, really does consist in nothing but man proving to himself every minute that he is a man and not an organ stop! Proving it even at the cost of his own skin; proving it even by living like a caveman. And after this, how can one help sinning and rejoicing that this has not happened yet, and that wanting still depends, for the time being, on the devil knows what...

You shout at me (if you still deign to shout at me) that nobody is taking away my will here; that all they are trying to do is somehow arrange things so that my will, of its own free will, coincides with my normal interests, with the laws of nature and with arithmetic.

Oh, gentlemen, what kind of will of my own will there be, when it comes down to tables and arithmetic, when there is nothing in use but two times two makes four? Two times two will make four without my will. As if that were what one’s own will is!
