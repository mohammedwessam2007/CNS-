## PART II · CHAPTER I

He lay like that for a very long time. Sometimes he seemed to wake up, and at those moments he noticed that it had long been night, but it did not occur to him to get up. At last he noticed that it was already light as day. He was lying on his back on the sofa, still stunned from his recent trance. Terrible, desperate screams came sharply to him from the street, which, however, he heard every night under his window at about three o’clock. It was they that had woken him now. “Ah! There are the drunks coming out of the taverns,” he thought. “It’s three o’clock,” and suddenly he jumped up as if someone had torn him from the sofa. “What! Three o’clock already!” He sat on the sofa, and then he remembered everything! Suddenly, in one moment, he remembered everything!

For the first moment he thought he would go mad. A terrible cold gripped him; but the cold came also from the fever, which had started in him long before, while he slept. Now suddenly such a shivering fit struck him that his teeth almost jumped out, and everything in him shook. He opened the door and began to listen: in the house everything was fast asleep. In amazement he looked at himself and at everything in the room around him, and did not understand: how could he have come in yesterday and not fastened the door on the hook, and thrown himself on the sofa, not only without undressing, but even with his hat on: it had rolled off and lay right there on the floor, near the pillow. “If anyone had come in, what would he have thought? That I was drunk, but...” He rushed to the window. There was enough light, and he quickly began to examine himself, all over, from head to foot, all his clothes: were there any traces? But that would not do: shaking with the chill, he began to take everything off and examine it all again. He turned everything over, to the last thread and scrap, and, not trusting himself, repeated the examination three times. But there was nothing, it seemed, no traces; only in the place where the bottoms of his trousers were frayed and hung in a fringe, there were thick traces of dried blood on that fringe. He seized his big folding knife and cut off the fringe. There seemed to be nothing else. Suddenly he remembered that the purse and the things he had taken out of the old woman’s trunk were all still in his pockets! He had not even thought until now of taking them out and hiding them! He had not remembered them even now, as he examined his clothes! What was this? In an instant he rushed to take them out and throw them on the table. When he had taken out everything, even turned his pockets inside out to make sure nothing was left, he carried the whole pile into the corner. There, in the very corner, low down, the wallpaper, which had come away from the wall, was torn in one place: at once he began stuffing everything into this hole, under the paper: “It’s gone in! Everything out of sight, and the purse too!” he thought joyfully, half rising and staring dully at the corner, at the hole, which bulged out still more. Suddenly he shuddered all over with horror: “My God,” he whispered in despair, “what is the matter with me? Is that hidden? Is that how one hides things?”

It is true that he had not reckoned on things; he had thought there would be only money, and so he had not prepared a place beforehand. “But now, now what am I glad about?” he thought. “Is that how one hides things? Truly my reason is leaving me!” In exhaustion he sat down on the sofa, and at once an unbearable chill shook him again. Mechanically he pulled over him his old student’s winter coat, which lay beside him on a chair, warm but almost in rags by now, and sleep and delirium seized him again at once. He sank into oblivion.

Not more than five minutes later he jumped up again and at once, in a frenzy, rushed to his clothes again. “How could I fall asleep again, when nothing has been done! Just so, just so: I still haven’t taken off the loop under the arm! I forgot, forgot such a thing! Such a piece of evidence!” He tore off the loop and quickly began tearing it into pieces, stuffing them under the pillow among the linen. “Pieces of torn linen will not rouse suspicion in any case; it seems so, it seems so!” he repeated, standing in the middle of the room, and with attention strained to the point of pain he began looking all around again, on the floor and everywhere, to see whether he had forgotten anything else. The certainty that everything, even memory, even simple reasoning, was leaving him began to torment him unbearably. “What, can it be beginning already, can this be the punishment coming already? There, there, just so!” Indeed, the bits of fringe he had cut off his trousers were lying right on the floor, in the middle of the room, for the first comer to see! “But what is the matter with me!” he cried again, as if lost.

Then a strange thought came into his head: that perhaps all his clothes were covered in blood, that perhaps there were many stains, but he simply did not see them, did not notice them, because his reasoning was weakened, shattered... his mind darkened... Suddenly he remembered that there had been blood on the purse too. “Bah! Then there must be blood in the pocket too, because I put the purse in my pocket still wet!” In an instant he turned the pocket inside out, and, just so, there were traces on the lining of the pocket, stains! “So my reason has not quite left me yet, so I still have some reasoning and memory, since I realised it and guessed it myself!” he thought triumphantly, drawing a deep and joyful breath with his whole chest. “It is simply the weakness of fever, delirium for a moment,” and he tore out the whole lining of the left trouser pocket. At that moment a ray of sunshine lit up his left boot: on the sock, which peeped out of the boot, there seemed to be marks. He threw off the boot: “Marks indeed! The whole tip of the sock is soaked in blood”; he must have stepped carelessly in that pool then... “But what am I to do with this now? Where am I to put this sock, the fringe, the pocket?”

He gathered it all up in his hand and stood in the middle of the room. “In the stove? But they will start rummaging in the stove first of all. Burn it? But burn it with what? I haven’t even any matches. No, better to go out somewhere and throw it all away. Yes! Better throw it away!” he repeated, sitting down on the sofa again, “and at once, this minute, without delay!..” But instead his head sank on the pillow again; again an unbearable chill froze him; again he pulled the coat over himself. And for a long time, several hours, he kept imagining in fits and starts that “now, at once, without putting it off, I ought to go somewhere and throw it all away, to get it out of sight, quickly, quickly!” Several times he made a dash to get up from the sofa, tried to stand, but by now he could not. What finally woke him was a loud knocking at the door.

“Open up, are you alive or not? He’s always snoring!” shouted Nastasya, banging on the door with her fist. “Snoring the whole day long like a dog! A dog is what he is! Open up, will you. It’s past ten.”

“Maybe he isn’t in!” said a man’s voice.

“Bah! That is the caretaker’s voice... What does he want?”

He jumped up and sat on the sofa. His heart was beating so hard that it even hurt.

“And who put the hook on, then?” objected Nastasya. “Look at him, he’s started locking himself in! Is he afraid he’ll be carried off? Open up, you blockhead, wake up!”

“What do they want? Why the caretaker? Everything is known. Resist or open? Let it all go...”

He half rose, leaned forward and took off the hook.

His whole room was so small that he could take off the hook without getting up from the bed.

Just so: there stood the caretaker and Nastasya.

Nastasya looked him over somehow strangely. He glanced at the caretaker with a defiant and desperate look. The man silently held out to him a grey paper folded in two and sealed with bottle wax.

“A summons, from the police office,” he said, handing him the paper.

“From what office?..”

“The police, that is, they’re calling you, to the office. You know what office.”

“To the police!.. What for?..”

“How should I know? They want you, so go.” He looked at him attentively, looked around and turned to go.

“He seems to have fallen properly ill, hasn’t he?” remarked Nastasya, who had not taken her eyes off him. The caretaker too turned his head for a moment. “He’s been in a fever since yesterday,” she added.

He did not answer and held the paper in his hands without opening it.

“Don’t get up, then,” Nastasya went on, moved to pity and seeing that he was lowering his feet from the sofa. “If you’re ill, don’t go: it won’t burn down. What have you got in your hands?”

He looked: in his right hand he had the cut-off pieces of fringe, the sock and the scraps of the torn-out pocket. He had slept with them like that. Afterwards, thinking about it, he remembered that even half waking in his fever he had gripped all this tight in his hand and fallen asleep again like that.

“Look what rags he has collected, and he sleeps with them as if they were treasure...” And Nastasya went off into her sickly, nervous laugh. In an instant he thrust everything under his coat and fixed his eyes on her intently. Though he could understand very little clearly at that moment, he felt that a man would not be treated like that when they came to arrest him. “But... the police?”

“Would you have some tea? Do you want some? I’ll bring it; there’s some left...”

“No... I’ll go: I’ll go right now,” he muttered, getting to his feet.

“You won’t even get down the stairs, I bet?”

“I’ll go...”

“As you like.”

She went out after the caretaker. At once he rushed to the light to examine the sock and the fringe: “There are stains, but not very noticeable; it is all dirty, rubbed and faded already. Anyone who doesn’t know beforehand won’t make anything out. So Nastasya could not have noticed anything from a distance, thank God!” Then, trembling, he opened the summons and began to read; he read for a long time and at last he understood. It was an ordinary summons from the district police to appear that very day, at half past nine, at the office of the district superintendent.

“But when has this ever happened? I have no business of my own with the police! And why just today?” he thought in tormenting bewilderment. “Lord, let it be over quickly!” He was about to throw himself on his knees to pray, but he even laughed himself, not at the prayer but at himself. He hastily began to dress. “If I am lost, I am lost; it’s all the same! Put on the sock!” he suddenly thought. “It will get still more rubbed in the dust, and the traces will disappear.” But no sooner had he put it on than he pulled it off again with disgust and horror. He pulled it off, but, realising that he had no other, he took it and put it on again, and again he laughed. “All this is conventional, all relative, all this is merely forms,” he thought fleetingly, with only the very edge of his mind, while he himself was trembling all over. “There, I have put it on! I ended by putting it on after all!” The laughter, however, was at once replaced by despair. “No, it is beyond my strength...” he thought. His legs were trembling. “From fear,” he muttered to himself. His head was spinning and aching from the fever. “It’s a trick! They want to lure me by a trick and then suddenly trip me up on everything,” he went on to himself as he went out onto the stairs. “The bad thing is that I am almost delirious... I may blurt out some stupid lie...”

On the stairs he remembered that he was leaving all the things just like that, in the hole in the wallpaper: “and perhaps they will search on purpose while I am out,” he remembered, and stopped. But such despair and, if one may say so, such a cynicism of ruin suddenly took hold of him that he waved his hand and went on.

“If only it would be quick!..”

In the street the heat was unbearable again; not a drop of rain all these days. Again dust, bricks and lime, again the stench from the shops and taverns, again drunks every minute, Finnish pedlars and half-broken-down cabs. The sun flashed brightly in his eyes, so that it hurt to look and his head spun completely: the usual sensation of a man in a fever who suddenly comes out into the street on a bright, sunny day.

When he reached the turning into yesterday’s street, he glanced down it with tormenting anxiety, at that house... and at once looked away.

“If they ask, perhaps I’ll tell,” he thought, as he came near the police office.

The office was about a quarter of a verst from his lodging. It had only just moved to new premises, in a new building, on the fourth floor. He had been in the old premises once, briefly, but a very long time ago. Going in under the gateway, he saw on the right a staircase, down which a peasant was coming with a book in his hands: “a caretaker, then; so this is where the office is,” and he began to climb at random. He did not want to ask anyone anything.

“I’ll go in, fall on my knees and tell everything...” he thought, as he came up to the fourth floor.

The staircase was narrow, steep and all covered in slops. All the kitchens of all the flats on all four floors opened onto this staircase and stood open like that almost all day. Because of that it was terribly stuffy. Caretakers with books under their arms, messengers and various people of both sexes, visitors, went up and down. The door into the office itself was also wide open. He went in and stopped in the entrance room. Some peasants were standing there waiting. Here too the stuffiness was extreme, and besides there was a sickening smell of fresh paint, not yet dry, made with rancid oil, in the newly painted rooms. After waiting a little, he decided to move on into the next room. The rooms were all tiny and low. A terrible impatience drew him on and on. No one noticed him. In the second room some clerks were sitting and writing, dressed perhaps a little better than he was, all strange-looking people. He turned to one of them.

“What do you want?”

He showed the summons from the office.

“You are a student?” the man asked, glancing at the summons.

“Yes, a former student.”

The clerk looked him over, though without any curiosity. He was a particularly dishevelled man with a fixed idea in his look.

“There is nothing to be learned from this one, because it is all the same to him,” thought Raskolnikov.

“Go in there, to the chief clerk,” said the clerk, and jabbed his finger forward, pointing to the very last room.

He went into that room (the fourth in order), cramped and packed full of people, rather more cleanly dressed than in the other rooms. Among the visitors were two ladies. One, in mourning, poorly dressed, was sitting at the table opposite the chief clerk and writing something at his dictation. The other lady, very stout and purple-red, with blotches, a striking woman, and somehow very gorgeously dressed, with a brooch on her bosom the size of a saucer, stood to one side waiting for something. Raskolnikov thrust his summons at the chief clerk. He glanced at it in passing, said, “Wait,” and went on attending to the lady in mourning.

He breathed more freely. “It can’t be that!” Little by little he began to take heart; he urged himself with all his strength to take heart and collect himself.

“Some stupidity, some tiny carelessness, and I can give myself away completely! Hm... a pity there is no air here,” he added, “it is stifling... My head is spinning even more... and my mind too...”

He felt a terrible disorder in his whole being. He was afraid he would not be able to control himself. He tried to fasten on something and think about anything at all, something completely unrelated, but he could not manage it at all. The chief clerk, however, interested him greatly: he kept wanting to guess something from his face, to see through him. He was a very young man, about twenty-two, with a dark and mobile face that looked older than his years, dressed in the fashion and like a dandy, with his hair parted at the back, combed and pomaded, with a great many rings on his white fingers, scrubbed with brushes, and gold chains on his waistcoat. With a foreigner who was there he even exchanged a few words in French, and very creditably.

“Luiza Ivanovna, you should sit down,” he said in passing to the gorgeously dressed purple-red lady, who was still standing as if not daring to sit down herself, though there was a chair beside her.

“Ich danke,” she said, and quietly, with a rustle of silk, sank onto the chair. Her pale-blue dress with white lace trimming spread around the chair like a balloon and took up almost half the room. There was a waft of perfume. But the lady was evidently timid about taking up half the room and smelling so strongly of perfume, though she smiled in a way that was cowardly and brazen at once, but with evident uneasiness.

The lady in mourning finished at last and began to get up. Suddenly, with some noise, very dashingly and with a special swing of the shoulders at every step, an officer came in, threw his cap with its cockade on the table and sat down in an armchair. The gorgeous lady positively jumped up from her seat when she saw him, and began curtseying with a kind of special rapture; but the officer paid not the slightest attention to her, and she no longer dared sit down in his presence. He was a lieutenant, the assistant district superintendent, with reddish moustaches sticking out horizontally on both sides, and with extremely small features, which expressed nothing in particular, however, except a certain insolence. He looked sideways at Raskolnikov, and partly with indignation: his clothes were really too wretched, and, in spite of all his humiliation, his bearing still did not match his clothes; Raskolnikov, carelessly, looked at him too straight and too long, so that he even took offence.

“What do you want?” he shouted, probably surprised that such a ragamuffin had no thought of shrinking away from his lightning glance.

“I was summoned... by a summons...” Raskolnikov answered somehow.

“It’s the case of recovering money from him, from the student,” the chief clerk hurried to say, tearing himself away from his papers. “Here!” And he tossed Raskolnikov a notebook, pointing to a place in it. “Read it!”

“Money? What money?” thought Raskolnikov. “But... then it certainly isn’t that!” And he started with joy. He suddenly felt terribly, inexpressibly light. Everything fell from his shoulders.

“And what time were you told to come, my dear sir?” shouted the lieutenant, more and more offended at heaven knows what. “You are told nine, and now it’s past eleven!”

“It was only brought to me a quarter of an hour ago,” Raskolnikov answered loudly, over his shoulder, he too suddenly and unexpectedly angry, and even finding a certain pleasure in it. “It is enough that I have come at all, ill with a fever.”

“Kindly do not shout!”

“I am not shouting, I am speaking very evenly; it is you who are shouting at me; and I am a student and I will not allow anyone to shout at me.”

The assistant flared up so much that at first he could not even utter a word, and only some spray flew from his lips. He jumped up from his seat.

“Kindly be si-i-ilent! You are in a government office. No r-r-rudeness, sir!”

“And you are in a government office too,” cried Raskolnikov, “and besides shouting, you are smoking a cigarette, so you are showing disrespect to all of us.” Having said this, Raskolnikov felt an inexpressible pleasure.

The chief clerk looked at them with a smile. The hot-tempered lieutenant was evidently taken aback.

“That is none of your business, sir!” he shouted at last, somehow unnaturally loudly. “But kindly give the declaration that is required of you. Show him, Alexander Grigoryevich. There are complaints against you! You don’t pay your debts! What a fine falcon has flown in!”

But Raskolnikov was no longer listening, and greedily seized the paper, looking for the answer to the riddle as fast as he could. He read it once, twice, and did not understand.

“What is this?” he asked the chief clerk.

“It is a demand for money from you on a promissory note, a recovery. You must either pay with all costs, fines and so on, or give a written declaration of when you can pay, and at the same time an undertaking not to leave the capital until you have paid, and not to sell or conceal your property. And the creditor is free to sell your property and to proceed against you according to the law.”

“But I... don’t owe anyone anything!”

“That is not our business. Here we have received for recovery an overdue and legally protested promissory note for a hundred and fifteen roubles, issued by you to the widow of a collegiate assessor, Zarnitsyna, nine months ago, and passed by the widow Zarnitsyna in payment to Court Councillor Chebarov, and so we summon you to make a declaration.”

“But she is my landlady!”

“Well, what if she is your landlady?”

The chief clerk looked at him with a condescending smile of pity and at the same time of a certain triumph, as at a novice who is just beginning to come under fire: “Well, how do you feel now?” But what, what did he care now about a promissory note, about recovery! Was it worth the slightest anxiety now, or even the slightest attention! He stood, read, listened, answered, even asked questions himself, but all mechanically. The triumph of self-preservation, deliverance from the danger that had been crushing him: that was what filled his whole being at that moment, without foresight, without analysis, without future guessing and riddling, without doubts and without questions. It was a minute of complete, immediate, purely animal joy. But at that very minute something like thunder and lightning broke out in the office. The lieutenant, still shaken by the disrespect, all aflame, and evidently wishing to restore his injured pride, fell with all his thunderbolts on the unhappy “gorgeous lady”, who had been looking at him with the stupidest of smiles ever since he came in.

“And you, you so-and-so,” he suddenly shouted at the top of his voice (the lady in mourning had already gone out), “what happened at your place last night? Eh? Disgrace again, you’re making a row for the whole street to hear. Fighting and drunkenness again. You’re dreaming of the house of correction! Didn’t I tell you, didn’t I warn you ten times that I wouldn’t let it pass the eleventh time? And here you are again, again, you so-and-so!”

The paper even fell out of Raskolnikov’s hands, and he stared wildly at the gorgeous lady who was being dressed down so unceremoniously; but soon he grasped what it was about, and at once the whole story began to please him very much indeed. He listened with pleasure, so much that he wanted to laugh, laugh, laugh... All his nerves were jumping.

“Ilya Petrovich!” the chief clerk began solicitously, but stopped to bide his time, because there was no holding back the boiling lieutenant except by the arms, as he knew from his own experience.

As for the gorgeous lady, at first she positively trembled at the thunder and lightning; but, strange to say, the more numerous and stronger the abuse became, the more amiable her look grew, the more charming the smile she turned on the formidable lieutenant. She shuffled on the spot and curtseyed incessantly, waiting impatiently to be allowed at last to put in a word of her own, and her chance came.

“No noise and no fight vas at my place, Herr Kapitän,” she suddenly rattled off, as if a bag of peas had been spilt, with a strong German accent, though in brisk Russian, “and no, no scandal, but zey come drunk, and I vill tell it all, Herr Kapitän, and I am not guilty... I have a noble house, Herr Kapitän, and noble manners, Herr Kapitän, and I alvays, alvays myself vanted no scandal. But zey come quite drunk and zen ask for three bottles more, and zen one lift up his feet and begin to play ze piano viz his foot, and zat is not at all nice in a noble house, and he ganz break ze piano, and zere is no, no manners at all here, and I said so. And he take ze bottle and begin to push everyone from behind viz ze bottle. And zen I quick call ze caretaker and Karl come, and he take Karl and hit him in ze eye, and Henriette he hit in ze eye too, and me he hit on ze cheek five times. And zat is so indelicate in a noble house, Herr Kapitän, and I scream. And he open ze window on ze canal and stand in ze window and squeal like a little pig; and zat is a shame. And how can one squeal in ze window on ze street like a little pig; and zat is a shame. Fui-fui-fui! And Karl pull him from behind by his coat from ze window, and zen, it is true, Herr Kapitän, he tear his coat. And zen he shout zat man muss pay him fifteen roubles fine. And I myself, Herr Kapitän, pay him five roubles for his coat. And zat is not a noble guest, Herr Kapitän, and he make every kind of scandal! I, he said, vill print a big satire about you, because I can write everything about you in all ze newspapers.”

“One of the writing fraternity, then?”

“Yes, Herr Kapitän, and vat a not noble guest it is, Herr Kapitän, ven in a noble house...”

“Now, now, now! Enough! I have told you, told you, I told you...”

“Ilya Petrovich!” the chief clerk said again significantly. The lieutenant glanced at him quickly; the chief clerk nodded his head slightly.

“...So here is my last word to you, most respected Laviza Ivanovna, and it is for the last time,” the lieutenant went on. “If there is a scandal in your noble house even one more time, I will haul you up before the court myself, as they say in high style. Do you hear? So a literary man, a writer, took five roubles in a ‘noble house’ for his coat-tails? There they are, your writers!” And he cast a contemptuous look at Raskolnikov. “The day before yesterday there was a scene in a tavern too: one of them dined and then wouldn’t pay; ‘I’ll describe you in a satire for that,’ he says. On a steamer last week another one called the respectable family of a civil councillor, his wife and daughter, the vilest names. The other day one was thrown out of a confectioner’s by the scruff of his neck. That’s what they are like, writers, literary men, students, heralds... pah! And you, be off with you! I’ll look in on you myself... then look out! Do you hear?”

Luiza Ivanovna, with hasty amiability, began curtseying in all directions, and, curtseying, backed to the door; but in the doorway she bumped backwards into a fine-looking officer with an open, fresh face and superb, very thick fair side-whiskers. This was Nikodim Fomich himself, the district superintendent. Luiza Ivanovna hastened to curtsey almost to the floor and, with quick little steps, hopping, flew out of the office.

“Thunder again, thunder and lightning again, a waterspout, a hurricane!” Nikodim Fomich said amiably and in a friendly way to Ilya Petrovich. “Your heart has been stirred up again, you’ve boiled over again! I heard you from the stairs.”

“Oh, vot!” Ilya Petrovich said with gentlemanly nonchalance (and not even ‘what’, but somehow ‘Oh, va-at!’), moving to another table with some papers and picturesquely jerking his shoulders at every step, wherever the step went, there went the shoulder. “Here, sir, if you please: the gentleman is a writer, I mean a student, a former one, that is; he doesn’t pay his debts, he has given bills, he won’t clear out of his lodging, there are constant complaints against him, and he was pleased to take offence because I lit a cigarette in his presence! They behave like s-s-scoundrels themselves, and here, sir, look at him: there he is now in all his most attractive glory, sir!”

“Poverty is no vice, my friend, but there it is! We know, he’s gunpowder, he couldn’t stand an insult. You must have taken offence at something against him, and you couldn’t hold yourself back,” Nikodim Fomich went on, turning amiably to Raskolnikov. “But you were wrong there: he is the mo-o-ost no-o-oble of men, I tell you, but gunpowder, gunpowder! He flares up, boils over, burns out, and that’s all! It’s all over! And all that’s left is a heart of gold! In the regiment they used to call him ‘Lieutenant Gunpowder’...”

“And what a r-r-regiment it was!” exclaimed Ilya Petrovich, very pleased to be tickled so pleasantly, but still sulking.

Raskolnikov suddenly wanted to say something extraordinarily pleasant to them all.

“But really, Captain,” he began very casually, turning suddenly to Nikodim Fomich, “consider my position too... I am even ready to ask his pardon, if I have been at all disrespectful on my side. I am a poor and sick student, weighed down” (he actually said “weighed down”) “by poverty. I am a former student because I cannot keep myself now, but I shall be getting money... I have a mother and sister in X. province. They will send me some, and I... shall pay. My landlady is a good woman, but she is so angry that I have lost my lessons and haven’t paid for four months that she doesn’t even send up my dinner... And I don’t understand at all what this bill is! Now she is demanding payment from me on this promissory note; how am I to pay her, judge for yourselves!..”

“But that is not our business...” the chief clerk was about to remark again...

“Allow me, allow me, I entirely agree with you, but allow me to explain too,” Raskolnikov took it up again, addressing not the chief clerk but still Nikodim Fomich, but trying with all his might to address Ilya Petrovich too, though the latter stubbornly pretended to be rummaging in his papers and contemptuously paid no attention to him. “Allow me on my side to explain that I have been living with her for about three years now, ever since I came from the provinces, and before... before... but why should I not confess in my turn: from the very beginning I gave a promise that I would marry her daughter, a verbal promise, quite freely given... She was a girl... in fact I even liked her... though I was not in love... in a word, youth, that is, I mean to say that my landlady gave me a lot of credit then, and I led a sort of life... I was very frivolous...”

“Nobody is asking you for such intimacies, my dear sir, and there is no time either,” Ilya Petrovich interrupted rudely and triumphantly; but Raskolnikov stopped him hotly, though it had suddenly become extremely hard for him to speak.

“But allow me, allow me to tell it all, in part... how it happened and... in my turn... although it is unnecessary, I agree with you, to tell it... but a year ago this girl died of typhus, and I stayed on as a lodger, as before, and my landlady, when she moved to her present flat, said to me... and said it in a friendly way... that she trusted me completely and all that... but would I not give her this promissory note for a hundred and fifteen roubles, all that she reckoned I owed her? Allow me: she said precisely that, as soon as I gave her this paper, she would give me credit again as much as I liked, and that never, never, for her part (those were her own words), would she make use of this paper until I paid myself... And now, when I have lost my lessons and have nothing to eat, she puts it in for recovery... What am I to say now?”

“All these touching details, my dear sir, are no concern of ours,” Ilya Petrovich cut him short insolently. “You must give a declaration and an undertaking, and whether you were pleased to be in love and all those tragic passages are no business of ours at all.”

“Well, now you are... being cruel...” muttered Nikodim Fomich, sitting down at the table and beginning to sign papers too. He felt somehow ashamed.

“Write, then,” the chief clerk said to Raskolnikov.

“Write what?” he asked, somehow especially rudely.

“I will dictate it to you.”

It seemed to Raskolnikov that the chief clerk had become more offhand and more contemptuous with him after his confession; but, strange to say, he himself suddenly did not care in the least about anyone’s opinion, and this change happened somehow in a moment, in a single minute. If he had wanted to think a little, he would certainly have been surprised at how he could have talked to them like that a minute before, and even forced his feelings on them. And where had those feelings come from? On the contrary, now, if the room had suddenly been filled not with police officers but with his very best friends, even then, it seems, he would not have found a single human word for them, so empty had his heart suddenly become. A gloomy sensation of tormenting, endless solitude and estrangement suddenly made itself consciously felt in his soul. It was not the baseness of his heartfelt outpourings before Ilya Petrovich, nor the baseness of the lieutenant’s triumph over him, that had suddenly turned his heart over like this. Oh, what did he care now about his own baseness, about all these vanities, lieutenants, German women, recoveries, offices and so on, and so on! If he had been sentenced to be burned at that moment, even then he would not have stirred, and would hardly even have listened attentively to the sentence. Something was happening to him that was completely unknown to him, new, sudden, and that had never been before. It was not that he understood it, but he felt clearly, with the whole force of feeling, that not only with sentimental effusions, as just now, but with anything at all, he could no longer turn to these people in the police office, and that even if they had all been his own brothers and sisters and not police lieutenants, even then there would be no reason whatever for him to turn to them, in any circumstance of life; he had never until that moment experienced such a strange and terrible sensation. And what was most tormenting of all: it was more a sensation than an awareness, than an idea; an immediate sensation, the most tormenting of all the sensations he had lived through in his life until then.

The chief clerk began dictating to him the form of declaration usual in such cases, that is, I cannot pay, I promise to pay on such and such a date (some time), I will not leave the town, I will not sell or give away my property, and so on.

“But you can’t write, the pen is falling out of your hand,” the chief clerk remarked, looking at Raskolnikov with curiosity. “Are you ill?”

“Yes... my head is spinning... go on!”

“That’s all! Sign it.”

The chief clerk took back the paper and turned to the others.

Raskolnikov gave back the pen, but instead of getting up and going away, he put both elbows on the table and squeezed his head in his hands. It was as if a nail were being driven into the crown of his head. A strange thought suddenly came to him: to get up now, go to Nikodim Fomich and tell him everything about yesterday, everything to the last detail, then go with them to his lodging and show them the things in the corner, in the hole. The urge was so strong that he had already risen from his seat to carry it out. “Shouldn’t I think it over for a minute at least?” flashed through his head. “No, better without thinking, and get it off my shoulders!” But suddenly he stopped as if rooted to the spot: Nikodim Fomich was speaking hotly to Ilya Petrovich, and the words reached him:

“It can’t be, they’ll both be released! In the first place, everything contradicts it; judge for yourself: why would they call the caretaker if it was their doing? To inform on themselves? Or as a trick? No, that would be too cunning! And finally, the student Pestryakov was seen right at the gate by both caretakers and a tradeswoman at the very moment he went in: he was walking with three friends and parted from them right at the gate, and asked the caretakers about lodgings, while his friends were still there. Now, would a man ask about lodgings if he had come with such an intention? And as for Koch, before going to the old woman he sat for half an hour downstairs at the silversmith’s, and went up from him to the old woman at exactly a quarter to eight. Now consider...”

“But wait, how did they come to contradict themselves like that: they assert themselves that they knocked and that the door was locked, and three minutes later, when they came with the caretaker, it turns out that the door was open?”

“That’s just the point: the murderer must have been sitting in there and had bolted the door; and they would certainly have caught him there if Koch hadn’t been a fool and gone for the caretaker himself. It was precisely in that interval that he managed to get down the stairs and slip past them somehow. Koch crosses himself with both hands: ‘If I had stayed there,’ he says, ‘he would have jumped out and killed me with the axe.’ He wants to have a thanksgiving service said, he-he!..”

“And nobody saw the murderer?”

“Why, how could anybody see him there? The house is a Noah’s ark,” remarked the chief clerk, who had been listening from his place.

“The thing is clear, the thing is clear!” Nikodim Fomich repeated hotly.

“No, the thing is very unclear,” Ilya Petrovich clinched it.

Raskolnikov picked up his hat and went towards the door, but he did not reach the door...

When he came to, he saw that he was sitting on a chair, that some man was supporting him on the right, that on the left stood another man with a yellow glass filled with yellow water, and that Nikodim Fomich was standing in front of him and looking at him intently; he got up from the chair.

“What is this, are you ill?” Nikodim Fomich asked rather sharply.

“Even when he was signing, he could hardly move the pen,” remarked the chief clerk, sitting down in his place and turning to his papers again.

“And have you been ill long?” Ilya Petrovich shouted from his place, also sorting papers. He too, of course, had been looking at the sick man while he was in his faint, but he had moved away at once when he came to.

“Since yesterday...” Raskolnikov muttered in reply.

“And did you go out yesterday?”

“I did.”

“Ill?”

“Ill.”

“At what time?”

“After seven in the evening.”

“And where, may I ask?”

“Along the street.”

“Short and clear.”

Raskolnikov answered sharply, abruptly, as white as a handkerchief, and without lowering his black, inflamed eyes before Ilya Petrovich’s look.

“He can hardly stand on his feet, and you...” Nikodim Fomich was about to remark.

“No mat-ter!” Ilya Petrovich said somehow peculiarly. Nikodim Fomich was about to add something more, but, glancing at the chief clerk, who was also looking at him very intently, he fell silent. Everyone suddenly fell silent. It was strange.

“Well, sir, very good, sir,” Ilya Petrovich concluded. “We are not detaining you.”

Raskolnikov went out. He could still hear how, after he had gone, a lively conversation began, in which the questioning voice of Nikodim Fomich sounded most clearly of all... In the street he came fully to himself.

“A search, a search, a search right away!” he repeated to himself, hurrying to get home. “The robbers! They suspect!” The fear of before seized him again, all over, from head to foot.
