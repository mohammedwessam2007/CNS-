## Book Eight: Mitya · Chapter I · Kuzma Samsonov

Meanwhile Dmitri Fyodorovich, to whom Grushenka, flying off into a new life, had “bidden” Alyosha pass on her last greeting, and whom she had told to remember for ever her little hour of love, was at that moment, knowing nothing of what had happened to her, also in terrible confusion and bustle. For the last two days he had been in such an unimaginable state that he really might have fallen ill with brain fever, as he said himself afterwards. Alyosha had not been able to find him the morning before, and his brother Ivan had not been able to arrange to meet him at the tavern that same day. The people who owned the little flat where he lodged had hidden his tracks on his orders. And during those two days he had literally been rushing about in all directions, “struggling with his fate and saving himself”, as he put it afterwards, and had even flown out of town for a few hours on a certain urgent business, although he was terribly afraid to go away and leave Grushenka even for a minute without an eye on her. All this came out later in the most detailed and documented form, but for now we will set down, in terms of fact, only what is most necessary from the story of those two terrible days in his life that preceded the dreadful catastrophe which broke so suddenly over his fate.

Grushenka, though she did love him truly and sincerely for her little hour, it is true, also at the same time tormented him at times really cruelly and mercilessly. The main thing was that he could make out nothing of her intentions; nor was there any possibility of coaxing it out of her by kindness or by force: she would never have given in, but would only have grown angry and turned away from him altogether; he understood that clearly then. He suspected then, quite rightly, that she herself was in some kind of struggle, in some extraordinary indecision, that she was making up her mind to something and still could not make it up; and so, not without reason, he supposed with a sinking heart that at moments she must simply hate him and his passion. Perhaps it was so; but what exactly Grushenka was pining over he still did not understand. For him the whole question that tormented him came down to two terms only: “Either he, Mitya, or Fyodor Pavlovich.” Here, by the way, one firm fact must be noted: he was entirely convinced that Fyodor Pavlovich would certainly offer Grushenka lawful marriage (if he had not already), and did not believe for a minute that the old voluptuary hoped to get off with only three thousand. Mitya concluded this, knowing Grushenka and her character. That was why it could seem to him at times that all Grushenka’s torment and all her indecision arose simply from her not knowing which of them to choose and which would be more profitable to her. As for the speedy return of the “officer”, that is, of the fateful man in Grushenka’s life whose arrival she was awaiting with such agitation and fear, strange to say, in those days he did not even think of thinking about it. True, in the very last days Grushenka had kept very silent with him about it. Yet he knew perfectly well from her own lips about the letter she had received a month before from her former seducer; he knew in part the contents of the letter too. Then, in a spiteful moment, Grushenka had shown him the letter; but, to her surprise, he attached almost no value to it. And it would be very hard to explain why: perhaps simply because, crushed himself by all the ugliness and horror of his struggle with his own father for this woman, he could not imagine anything more terrible and dangerous for himself, at least at that time. As for a suitor who suddenly sprang up from somewhere after five years’ disappearance, he simply did not believe in him, and especially not that he would come soon. Besides, in that very first letter from the “officer”, which had been shown to Mitenka, the arrival of this new rival was spoken of very vaguely: the letter was very cloudy, very high-flown and filled only with sentiment. It should be noted that on that occasion Grushenka hid from him the last lines of the letter, in which the return was spoken of somewhat more definitely. Besides, Mitenka remembered afterwards that at that moment he had caught on Grushenka’s own face a kind of involuntary and proud contempt for this missive from Siberia. After that Grushenka told Mitenka nothing more about any further communications with this new rival. And so, little by little, he even forgot about the officer altogether. He thought only that whatever might come of it, and however things turned out, the final clash with Fyodor Pavlovich that was approaching was too near and must be settled before everything else. With a sinking soul he waited every minute for Grushenka’s decision and kept believing that it would come suddenly, as if by inspiration. Suddenly she would say to him: “Take me, I am yours for ever,” and everything would be over: he would seize her and carry her off to the ends of the earth at once. Oh, he would carry her off at once, as far, as far as possible, if not to the ends of the earth, then somewhere to the ends of Russia; he would marry her there and settle with her incognito, so that no one would know about them at all, neither here nor there nor anywhere. Then, oh, then a completely new life would begin at once! Of this other, renewed and now “virtuous” life (“virtuous without fail, without fail”) he dreamed every minute, frantically. He thirsted for this resurrection and renewal. The vile pit in which he had got stuck by his own will weighed on him too heavily, and he, like very many in such cases, believed most of all in a change of place: if only not these people, if only not these circumstances, if only he could fly away from this cursed place, and everything would be reborn and go on in a new way! That was what he believed in and what he longed for.

But this was only in the case of the first, happy solution of the question. There was another solution too; another, but now terrible, outcome presented itself. Suddenly she would say to him: “Go away; I have just settled things with Fyodor Pavlovich and am marrying him, and I don’t need you.” And then… but then… Mitya, however, did not know what would happen then; he did not know until the very last hour; in this one must acquit him. He had no definite intentions; no crime was premeditated. He only watched, spied and tormented himself, but still prepared himself only for the first, happy outcome of his fate. He even drove away every other thought. But here a quite different torment began; a quite new and extraneous, but also fateful and insoluble, circumstance arose.

Namely: in case she said to him, “I am yours, take me away,” how was he to take her away? Where were his means, his money? Just at this time all his income from Fyodor Pavlovich’s handouts, which had never been interrupted through so many years, had run dry. Of course Grushenka had money, but in this respect a terrible pride suddenly showed itself in Mitya: he wanted to take her away himself and begin a new life with her on his own means, not on hers; he could not even imagine taking her money from her, and suffered from the thought to the point of tormented disgust. I do not enlarge on this fact here or analyse it, but only note it: such was the cast of his soul at that moment. All this may have arisen indirectly, and as if even unconsciously, from the secret torments of his conscience over Katerina Ivanovna’s money, which he had appropriated like a thief: “I am a scoundrel to the one, and I shall at once turn out a scoundrel to the other again,” he thought then, as he confessed himself afterwards; “and if Grushenka finds out, she won’t want such a scoundrel herself.” And so, where was he to get the means, where was he to get this fateful money? Otherwise everything would be lost and nothing would come off, “and solely because the money ran short, oh, shame!”

I run ahead: the point is that perhaps he did know where to get this money; he knew, perhaps, even where it lay. I will say nothing more about it this time, for everything will be explained later; but this was where his chief trouble lay, and I will state it, though vaguely: in order to take these means lying somewhere, in order to have the right to take them, he must first return the three thousand to Katerina Ivanovna; otherwise, “I am a pickpocket, I am a scoundrel, and I don’t want to begin a new life as a scoundrel,” Mitya decided; and so he decided to turn the whole world upside down, if necessary, but without fail to give these three thousand back to Katerina Ivanovna at all costs and before everything else. The final process of this decision took place in him, so to speak, in the very last hours of his life, namely after his last meeting with Alyosha, two days before, in the evening, on the road, after Grushenka had insulted Katerina Ivanovna and Mitya, hearing the story of it from Alyosha, had confessed that he was a scoundrel and told him to tell Katerina Ivanovna so, “if it could ease her in any way”. That same night, having parted from his brother, he felt in his frenzy that it would be better even “to kill and rob someone, but pay back the debt to Katya”. “Better that I should be a murderer and a thief before the man I killed and robbed, and before all people, and go to Siberia, than that Katya should have the right to say that I betrayed her and stole money from her and ran off with Grushenka on her money to begin a virtuous life! That I cannot do!” So Mitya declared, grinding his teeth, and at times he really could imagine that he would end with brain fever. But meanwhile he struggled…

Strange to say: it would seem that with such a decision nothing but despair was left to him, for where was he suddenly to get such money, and a beggar like him at that? And yet to the end, all that time, he went on hoping that he would get these three thousand, that they would come, would fly to him somehow of themselves, even from the sky. But that is just how it is with those who, like Dmitri Fyodorovich, all their lives know only how to spend and squander money that came to them by inheritance for nothing, and have no idea how money is earned. The most fantastic whirlwind rose in his head as soon as he had parted from Alyosha two days before, and confused all his thoughts. And so it came about that he began with the wildest of enterprises. Indeed, perhaps it is precisely in such situations that to such people the most impossible and fantastic enterprises present themselves first as the most possible. He suddenly decided to go to the merchant Samsonov, Grushenka’s protector, and propose a certain “plan” to him, and get from him on this “plan” the whole sum he needed at one stroke; of the commercial side of his plan he had not the least doubt; he doubted only how Samsonov himself would look on his escapade, if he chose to look at it not only from the commercial side. Though Mitya knew this merchant by sight, he was not acquainted with him and had never even once spoken to him. But for some reason a conviction had formed in him, long since, that this old debauchee, who now had one foot in the grave, might not at all object at this moment if Grushenka were to arrange her life honestly somehow and marry “a reliable man”. And that not only would he not object, but he wished it himself, and, given the chance, would help it on himself. Whether from some rumours or from some words of Grushenka’s, he had also concluded that the old man might prefer him to Fyodor Pavlovich for Grushenka. Perhaps to many readers of our story this reckoning on such help, and the intention of taking his bride, so to speak, from the hands of her protector, will seem much too coarse and unscrupulous on Dmitri Fyodorovich’s part. I can only remark that to Mitya Grushenka’s past seemed already finally past. He looked at that past with infinite compassion and decided with all the fire of his passion that as soon as Grushenka told him she loved him and would marry him, a completely new Grushenka would begin at once, and with her a completely new Dmitri Fyodorovich, without any vices now, only with virtues: they would both forgive each other and begin their life in a completely new way. As for Kuzma Samsonov, he regarded him, in this former, collapsed past of Grushenka’s, as a fateful man in her life, whom, however, she had never loved, and who, this was the main thing, had also “passed”, had come to an end, so that he no longer existed at all now. Besides, Mitya could not even regard him as a man now, for it was known to everyone in the town that he was only a sick ruin, who kept up relations with Grushenka, so to speak, only as a father, and not at all on the old footing, and that it had been so for a long time, for almost a year now. In any case there was a great deal of simple-heartedness in this on Mitya’s part, for with all his vices he was a very simple-hearted man. In consequence of this simple-heartedness he was, among other things, seriously convinced that old Kuzma, preparing to depart into the next world, felt a sincere repentance for his past with Grushenka, and that she had now no protector and friend more devoted than this already harmless old man.

On the very next day after his conversation with Alyosha in the field, after which Mitya hardly slept all night, he appeared at Samsonov’s house at about ten in the morning and asked to be announced. The house was old, gloomy, very spacious, of two storeys, with outbuildings and a lodge. On the ground floor lived Samsonov’s two married sons with their families, his aged sister and one unmarried daughter. In the lodge lived his two clerks, one of whom also had a large family. Both the children and the clerks were crowded into their quarters, but the upper floor of the house was occupied by the old man alone, and he would not let even his daughter live there with him, though she looked after him and, at fixed hours and at his unfixed summons, had to run up to him from below every time in spite of her long-standing breathlessness. This “upstairs” consisted of a great many large state rooms, furnished in the old merchant style, with long, dreary rows of clumsy mahogany armchairs and chairs along the walls, with crystal chandeliers in covers and gloomy mirrors between the windows. All these rooms stood quite empty and uninhabited, because the sick old man huddled in one room only, his remote little bedroom, where he was waited on by an old servant woman with her hair tied in a kerchief and by a “lad” who sat on the bench in the hall. Because of his swollen legs the old man could hardly walk at all any more and only rarely got up from his leather armchair, and the old woman, holding him under the arms, would walk him once or twice round the room. He was stern and taciturn even with this old woman. When the arrival of “the captain” was announced to him, he at once ordered that he be refused. But Mitya insisted and had himself announced again. Kuzma Kuzmich questioned the lad in detail: what did he look like, was he drunk? Was he making a row? And received the answer that “he is sober, but won’t go away.” The old man again ordered him refused. Then Mitya, who had foreseen all this and had brought paper and pencil with him on purpose for the occasion, wrote one line clearly on a scrap of paper: “On a most urgent matter closely concerning Agrafena Alexandrovna,” and sent it to the old man. After thinking a little, the old man told the lad to show the visitor into the hall, and sent the old woman downstairs with an order to his younger son to come up to him at once. This younger son, a man over six feet tall and of enormous strength, who shaved his face and dressed in the German fashion (Samsonov himself went about in a kaftan and with a beard), appeared at once and without a word. They all trembled before their father. The father summoned this fine fellow not exactly from fear of the captain (he was of a very far from timid character), but just in case, more so as to have a witness. Accompanied by his son, who took his arm, and the lad, he sailed at last into the hall. One must suppose that he felt a certain rather strong curiosity. This hall, in which Mitya was waiting, was a huge, gloomy room that crushed the soul with dreariness, two storeys high, with a gallery, with walls painted “in imitation marble” and three enormous crystal chandeliers in covers. Mitya was sitting on a little chair by the entrance door, waiting for his fate in nervous impatience. When the old man appeared at the opposite entrance, some seventy feet from Mitya’s chair, Mitya suddenly jumped up and went to meet him with his firm, military, yard-long strides. Mitya was decently dressed, in a buttoned frock coat, with a round hat in his hands and black gloves, exactly as he had been three days before at the monastery, at the elder’s, at the family meeting with Fyodor Pavlovich and his brothers. The old man waited for him standing, gravely and sternly, and Mitya felt at once that while he was approaching, the old man had taken him all in. Mitya was struck too by Kuzma Kuzmich’s face, which had swollen extremely of late: his lower lip, thick enough before, now looked like a kind of hanging pancake. Gravely and silently he bowed to his visitor, pointed him to an armchair by the sofa, and himself, slowly, leaning on his son’s arm and groaning painfully, began to settle himself on the sofa opposite Mitya, so that Mitya, seeing his painful efforts, at once felt in his heart remorse and a delicate shame at his present insignificance before so important a person whom he had troubled.

“What do you want of me, sir?” said the old man, having settled at last, slowly, distinctly, sternly but politely.

Mitya started, was about to jump up, but sat down again. Then he at once began to speak loudly, rapidly, nervously, with gestures and in a decided frenzy. It was plain that the man had reached the limit, was ruined and was looking for a last way out, and if that failed, then he might as well go and drown himself. Old Samsonov probably understood all this in an instant, though his face remained unchanged and cold as an idol’s.

“The most honourable Kuzma Kuzmich has probably heard more than once of my quarrels with my father, Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov, who robbed me of my inheritance from my own mother… since the whole town is already chattering about it… for here everyone chatters about what they shouldn’t… And besides, it may have reached you from Grushenka… excuse me: from Agrafena Alexandrovna… from the highly respected Agrafena Alexandrovna, whom I so deeply honour…” So Mitya began, and broke off at the very first word. But we will not give his whole speech word for word, but only a summary. The point, as it were, was this: he, Mitya, three months before, had expressly consulted (he said precisely “expressly”, and not “on purpose”) a lawyer in the provincial capital, “a famous lawyer, Kuzma Kuzmich, Pavel Pavlovich Korneplodov; you have probably heard of him? A vast forehead, almost a statesman’s mind… he knows you too… spoke of you in the highest terms…” Mitya broke off a second time. But his breaks did not stop him; he leapt over them at once and rushed on further and further. This same Korneplodov, it seemed, having questioned him in detail and examined the documents Mitya could show him (about the documents Mitya expressed himself vaguely and in particular haste at this point), had given the opinion that as regards the village of Chermashnya, which should, as it were, have belonged to him, Mitya, through his mother, a suit could indeed be brought and the old reprobate staggered by it… “because not all doors are locked, and justice knows where to squeeze through.” In a word, one could hope even, it seemed, for some six thousand more from Fyodor Pavlovich, seven even, since Chermashnya was worth no less than twenty-five thousand, that is, surely twenty-eight, “thirty, thirty, Kuzma Kuzmich, and, imagine, I haven’t got even seventeen out of that cruel man!…” So I, Mitya, had dropped the matter then, for I don’t know how to deal with the law; and on coming here I was struck dumb by a counter-claim (here Mitya got confused again and again leapt over it abruptly): so wouldn’t you, most honourable Kuzma Kuzmich, like to take over all my rights against this monster, and give me only three thousand yourself?… You can’t lose in any case; I swear it on my honour, my honour; quite the contrary, you may make six or seven thousand instead of three… And the main thing is to finish it “today even”. “I’d sign it over to you at a notary’s, or however it is done… In a word, I am ready for anything; I will hand over all the documents you require, sign everything… and we could draw up the paper at once, and if it were possible, if only it were possible, this very morning… You would give me those three thousand… for who is a capitalist to compare with you in this little town?… and by that you would save me from… in a word, you would save my poor head for a most noble deed, a most lofty deed, one may say… for I cherish the noblest feelings for a certain person whom you know only too well and over whom you watch like a father. I would not have come otherwise, if it were not like a father. And, if you like, three heads have knocked together here, for fate is a monster, Kuzma Kuzmich! Realism, Kuzma Kuzmich, realism! And since you have long had to be left out of it, there remain two heads, as I put it, clumsily perhaps, but I am not a literary man. That is, one head is mine and the other is that monster’s. So choose: either me or the monster. Everything is in your hands now: three destinies and two lots… Forgive me, I have got muddled, but you understand… I see by your honourable eyes that you have understood… And if you haven’t, then into the water today, so there!”

Mitya broke off his absurd speech with this “so there”, and, jumping up from his seat, waited for an answer to his foolish proposal. With the last phrase he had suddenly and hopelessly felt that it had all burst, and above all that he had reeled off a terrible heap of nonsense. “Strange: while I was coming here everything seemed fine, and now it is nonsense!” suddenly flashed through his hopeless head. All the time he was talking the old man sat motionless, following him with an icy expression in his eyes. Having kept him waiting a minute, however, Kuzma Kuzmich pronounced at last in the most decided and comfortless tone:

“Excuse me, sir, we don’t go in for such deals.”

Mitya suddenly felt his legs giving way under him.

“What am I to do now, Kuzma Kuzmich?” he muttered with a pale smile. “I am done for now, don’t you think?”

“Excuse me, sir…”

Mitya still stood and still stared motionless, point-blank, and suddenly noticed that something had moved in the old man’s face. He started.

“You see, sir, such deals are not in our line,” the old man said slowly. “There’d be lawsuits, lawyers, a regular misery! But if you like, there is a man here; you apply to him…”

“My God, who is it?… You are bringing me back to life, Kuzma Kuzmich,” Mitya suddenly began to babble.

“He isn’t from these parts, this man, and he isn’t here now either. He is of peasant stock; he deals in timber; his nickname is Lyagavy, the Setter. He has been bargaining with Fyodor Pavlovich for a year now over that wood of yours at Chermashnya, but they can’t agree on the price; perhaps you have heard. Now he has just come again and is staying with the priest at Ilyinskoye, twelve versts or so from Volovya station, in the village of Ilyinskoye. He wrote here to me too about this very matter, that is, about the wood; he asked my advice. Fyodor Pavlovich wants to go and see him himself. So if you were to get in before Fyodor Pavlovich and propose to Lyagavy just what you have told me, perhaps he might…”

“A brilliant idea!” Mitya broke in rapturously. “He is the very man; it is just right for him! He is bargaining, they are asking too much of him, and here is a deed to the very property, ha-ha-ha!” And Mitya suddenly burst out laughing with his short wooden laugh, quite unexpectedly, so that even Samsonov’s head gave a jerk.

“How can I thank you, Kuzma Kuzmich?” Mitya was boiling over.

“Not at all, sir,” Samsonov bowed his head.

“But you don’t know, you have saved me; oh, a presentiment drew me to you… So, to this priest!”

“No need for thanks, sir.”

“I hurry, I fly. I have taxed your health. I shall never forget it; it is a Russian who tells you so, Kuzma Kuzmich, a R-Russian!”

“Ve-ery well.”

Mitya was about to seize the old man’s hand and shake it, but something spiteful flashed in the old man’s eyes. Mitya drew back his hand, but at once reproached himself for his suspiciousness. “He is tired…” flashed through his mind.

“For her! For her, Kuzma Kuzmich! You understand that it is for her!” he suddenly bellowed through the whole hall, bowed, turned sharply and, with the same quick, yard-long strides, without looking back, made for the door. He was trembling with rapture. “Everything was being lost, and now my guardian angel has saved me,” rushed through his mind. “And if such a man of business as this old man (a most honourable old man, and what a bearing!) has pointed out this way, then… then of course the way is won. I must fly at once. I’ll be back before night, I’ll be back at night, but the thing is won. Can the old man have been laughing at me?” So Mitya exclaimed as he strode to his lodgings; and of course it could not present itself to his mind otherwise: that is, either it was sound advice (from such a man of business), given with knowledge of the matter, with knowledge of this Lyagavy (a strange name!), or… or the old man had been laughing at him! Alas, the latter thought was the only right one. Afterwards, long afterwards, when the whole catastrophe had already happened, old Samsonov himself confessed, laughing, that he had made a fool of “the captain” then. He was a spiteful, cold and mocking man, with morbid antipathies besides. Whether it was the captain’s rapturous look, or the foolish conviction of this “spendthrift and wastrel” that he, Samsonov, could fall for such wild nonsense as his “plan”, or a jealous feeling about Grushenka, in whose name “this scapegrace” had come to him with some wild nonsense for money, I do not know what exactly moved the old man then; but at the moment when Mitya stood before him, feeling his legs giving way, and exclaiming senselessly that he was done for, at that moment the old man looked at him with infinite spite and thought of making a fool of him. When Mitya had gone, Kuzma Kuzmich, pale with spite, turned to his son and told him to see to it that this ragamuffin never set foot there again, not to let him into the yard even, or else…

He did not finish what he threatened, but even his son, who had often seen him in a rage, trembled with fear. For a whole hour afterwards the old man was even shaking all over with spite, and by evening he fell ill and sent for the “doctor”.

## Chapter II · Lyagavy

And so he had to “gallop”, and still he had not a kopeck for horses; that is, he had two twenty-kopeck pieces, and that was all, all that was left of so many years of former prosperity! But he had at home an old silver watch, which had long since stopped going. He seized it and took it to a Jewish watchmaker who had his little shop in the market. The man gave him six roubles for it. “I didn’t even expect that!” cried the delighted Mitya (he still went on being delighted); he seized his six roubles and ran home. At home he made up the sum by borrowing three roubles from his landlords, who gave them to him with pleasure, though they were giving him their last money, so much did they love him. Mitya in his rapturous state told them there and then that his fate was being decided, and told them, in a terrible hurry of course, almost the whole of his “plan” that he had just put to Samsonov, then Samsonov’s decision, his future hopes, and so on, and so on. His landlords had been let into many of his secrets before this, and that was why they looked on him as one of their own, not at all a proud gentleman. Having thus put together nine roubles, Mitya sent for post-horses to Volovya station. But in this way the fact was remembered and noted that “on the eve of a certain event, at midday, Mitya had not a kopeck, and that, to get money, he sold his watch and borrowed three roubles from his landlords, and all before witnesses.”

I note this fact in advance; it will be explained later why I do so.

Galloping to Volovya station, Mitya, though he was radiant with a joyful presentiment that at last he would finish and untangle “all these affairs”, was nevertheless trembling with fear too: what would become of Grushenka now in his absence? What if this very day she made up her mind at last to go to Fyodor Pavlovich? That was why he had left without telling her and had told his landlords on no account to reveal where he had gone if anyone came from anywhere to ask for him. “Without fail, without fail, I must be back this evening,” he kept repeating, jolting in the cart, “and perhaps drag this Lyagavy here too… to execute the deed…” So Mitya dreamed with a sinking soul; but alas, his dreams were not at all destined to be fulfilled according to his “plan”.

In the first place, he was late, having set off from Volovya station by the country road. The country road turned out to be not twelve versts but eighteen. In the second place, he did not find the Ilyinskoye priest at home; he had gone off to a neighbouring village. By the time Mitya had tracked him down there, having driven to the neighbouring village with the same horses, now worn out, it was almost night. The priest, a timid and kindly-looking little man, explained to him at once that this Lyagavy, though he had stayed with him at first, was now at Sukhoi Posyolok, spending the night in the forester’s cottage there, because he was bargaining for timber there too. At Mitya’s insistent entreaties to take him to Lyagavy at once and “thereby, so to speak, save him”, the priest, though he hesitated at first, agreed to show him the way to Sukhoi Posyolok, evidently feeling curious; but, unluckily, he advised going “on foot”, since it was only a verst or so “and a little more”. Mitya of course agreed and strode off with his yard-long strides, so that the poor priest almost had to run after him. He was not yet old and a very cautious little man. Mitya at once began talking to him too about his plans, hotly, nervously demanded advice about Lyagavy, and talked all the way. The priest listened attentively but advised little. To Mitya’s questions he answered evasively: “I don’t know, oh, I don’t know; how should I know?” and so on. When Mitya began to speak of his quarrels with his father over the inheritance, the priest was even frightened, because he stood in some relation of dependence on Fyodor Pavlovich. He asked with surprise, however, why he called this trading peasant Gorstkin “Lyagavy”, and obligingly explained to Mitya that though he really was Lyagavy, he was not Lyagavy, because he took terrible offence at the name, and that one must be sure to call him Gorstkin, “or you will get nowhere with him; he won’t even listen,” the priest concluded. Mitya was somewhat and hastily surprised and explained that Samsonov himself had called him so. On hearing of this circumstance the priest at once dropped the subject, though he would have done well to explain to Dmitri Fyodorovich there and then his own guess: that if Samsonov himself had sent him to this peasant as to “Lyagavy”, had he not done so for some reason as a joke, and was there not something wrong about it? But Mitya had no time to stop “over such trifles”. He hurried, he strode on, and only on reaching Sukhoi Posyolok did he realise that they had walked not one verst, nor one and a half, but surely three; this vexed him, but he put up with it. They went into the cottage. The forester, an acquaintance of the priest, lived in one half of the cottage, and in the other, the clean half, across the passage, Gorstkin had installed himself. They went into this clean half and lit a tallow candle. The room was heated terribly hot. On the pine table stood a samovar that had gone out, with a tray of cups, an empty bottle of rum, a not quite empty quart bottle of vodka and the remains of a wheaten loaf. The newcomer himself lay stretched out on the bench, with his coat bundled under his head instead of a pillow, snoring heavily. Mitya stood perplexed. “Of course I must wake him: my business is too important; I hurried so; I am in a hurry to get back today,” Mitya began to fret; but the priest and the forester stood silent, not giving their opinion. Mitya went up and began to wake him himself; he set about it energetically, but the sleeper did not wake. “He is drunk,” Mitya decided, “but what am I to do, Lord, what am I to do!” And suddenly, in terrible impatience, he began pulling the sleeper by the arms and legs, rocking him by the head, lifting him up and sitting him on the bench; and still, after very prolonged efforts, all he achieved was that the man began to mumble absurdly and to swear, roundly though indistinctly.

“No, you had better wait,” the priest said at last, “for he is plainly in no condition.”

“He has been drinking all day,” said the forester.

“God!” Mitya kept crying. “If only you knew how necessary it is for me and what despair I am in now!”

“No, you had better wait till morning,” the priest repeated.

“Till morning? Have mercy, it’s impossible!” And in despair he was about to rush at the drunkard again to wake him, but at once gave it up, understanding how useless his efforts were. The priest was silent; the sleepy forester was gloomy.

“What terrible tragedies realism contrives with people!” Mitya said in complete despair. The sweat was pouring down his face. Taking advantage of the moment, the priest very reasonably explained that even if they did manage to wake the sleeper, being drunk he would still be incapable of any conversation, “and your business is important, so you had better leave it till morning…” Mitya spread out his hands and agreed.

“I’ll stay here with the candle, father, and catch the moment. When he wakes, then I’ll begin… I’ll pay you for the candle,” he turned to the forester, “and for the lodging too; you will remember Dmitri Karamazov. Only I don’t know what to do with you now, father: where will you sleep?”

“No, I’ll go home, sir. I’ll ride on his mare,” he pointed to the forester. “And so goodbye, sir; I wish you every success.”

So it was settled. The priest set off on the mare, glad to have got away at last, but still shaking his head in perplexity and wondering whether he ought not to inform his benefactor Fyodor Pavlovich of this curious incident tomorrow in good time, “or else, who knows, he’ll find out, be angry, and stop his favours.” The forester scratched himself and went in silence to his own half of the cottage, and Mitya sat down on the bench to catch the moment, as he put it. A deep anguish lay over his soul like a heavy fog. A deep, terrible anguish! He sat and thought, but could think nothing through. The candle was burning down; a cricket began to chirp; in the overheated room it was becoming unbearably stuffy. Suddenly he pictured the garden, the passage behind the garden; in his father’s house a door is mysteriously opening, and Grushenka is running in through the door… He jumped up from the bench.

“A tragedy!” he said, grinding his teeth, went mechanically up to the sleeper and began to look at his face. He was a lean peasant, still not old, with a very long face, light-brown curls and a long, thin, reddish beard, in a cotton shirt and a black waistcoat, from the pocket of which peeped the chain of a silver watch. Mitya examined this physiognomy with terrible hatred, and for some reason he particularly hated the fact that the man had curls. The main thing that was unbearably humiliating was that here he was, Mitya, standing over him with his urgent business, having sacrificed so much, given up so much, utterly worn out, while this parasite, “on whom my whole fate depends now, snores as if nothing had happened, as if he came from another planet.” “Oh, the irony of fate!” Mitya exclaimed, and suddenly, losing his head completely, rushed again to wake the drunken peasant. He tried to wake him with a kind of fury, tugged at him, pushed him, even beat him; but after struggling for five minutes and again achieving nothing, he went back in helpless despair to his bench and sat down.

“Stupid, stupid!” Mitya exclaimed. “And… how dishonourable it all is!” he suddenly added for some reason. His head began to ache terribly. “Shall I give it up? Go away altogether?” flashed through his mind. “No, till morning. I’ll stay on purpose, on purpose! Why did I come, then? And there is nothing to go away in; how could I get away from here now? Oh, the senselessness of it!”

His head, however, ached more and more. He sat motionless and no longer remembered how he began to doze, and suddenly he fell asleep sitting up. It seems he slept for two hours or more. He came to himself from an unbearable headache, unbearable enough to make him scream. His temples were throbbing, the crown of his head ached; coming to, he could not for a long time recover himself completely and grasp what had happened to him. At last he realised that the overheated room was full of fumes from the stove, and that he might have died. And the drunken peasant still lay snoring; the candle had guttered and was about to go out. Mitya shouted and rushed, staggering, across the passage into the forester’s room. The forester soon woke, but on hearing that there were fumes in the other room, though he went to see to it, he took the fact with strange indifference, which surprised Mitya and offended him.

“But he is dead, he is dead, and then… what then?” Mitya cried in a frenzy before him.

They opened the doors, opened the window, opened the flue; Mitya dragged a bucket of water in from the passage, first wetted his own head, and then, finding some rag, dipped it in the water and put it on Lyagavy’s head. The forester went on treating the whole event even somehow contemptuously, and, having opened the window, said gloomily, “It’ll do like this,” and went back to sleep, leaving Mitya a lighted iron lantern. Mitya fussed over the fume-stupefied drunkard for half an hour, wetting his head again and again, and seriously meant not to sleep all night; but, worn out, he sat down somehow for a minute to get his breath, and instantly closed his eyes; then at once, unconsciously, he stretched out on the bench and fell asleep like the dead.

He woke terribly late. It was already about nine in the morning. The sun was shining brightly through the two little windows of the cottage. The curly-haired peasant of the night before was sitting on the bench, already dressed in his coat. Before him stood a new samovar and a new quart bottle. The old one of the night before had been finished, and the new one was more than half empty. Mitya jumped up and in an instant realised that the cursed peasant was drunk again, deeply and irretrievably drunk. He stared at him for a minute, his eyes starting out. And the peasant looked at him silently and slyly, with a kind of insulting calm, even with a kind of contemptuous haughtiness, as it seemed to Mitya. He rushed to him.

“Allow me, you see… I… you have probably heard from the forester here in the other room: I am Lieutenant Dmitri Karamazov, son of old Karamazov, whose wood you are bargaining for…”

“That’s a lie!” the peasant suddenly rapped out, firmly and calmly.

“What do you mean, a lie? You know Fyodor Pavlovich?”

“I don’t know any Fyodor Pavlovich of yours,” the peasant said, somehow moving his tongue heavily.

“The wood, the wood; you are bargaining with him for it; wake up, come to your senses. Father Pavel of Ilyinskoye brought me here… You wrote to Samsonov, and he sent me to you…” Mitya was gasping.

“That’s a l-lie!” Lyagavy rapped out again.

Mitya’s legs went cold.

“Have mercy, this is no joke! Perhaps you are tipsy. But you can speak, after all, understand… otherwise… otherwise I understand nothing!”

“You’re a painter!”

“Have mercy, I am Karamazov, Dmitri Karamazov; I have a proposal for you… a profitable proposal… a very profitable one… precisely about the wood.”

The peasant was stroking his beard importantly.

“No, you took on a contract and turned out a scoundrel. You’re a scoundrel!”

“I assure you, you are mistaken!” Mitya wrung his hands in despair. The peasant kept stroking his beard and suddenly screwed up his eyes slyly.

“No, you show me this: you show me a law that allows people to play dirty tricks, do you hear! You’re a scoundrel, do you understand?”

Mitya stepped back gloomily, and suddenly it was as if “something struck him on the forehead”, as he put it himself afterwards. In an instant a kind of illumination came over his mind: “a light was kindled, and I understood everything.” He stood dumbfounded, wondering how he, an intelligent man after all, could have fallen for such foolishness, rushed headlong into such an adventure and kept it up for almost a whole day and night, fussing over this Lyagavy, wetting his head… “Well, the man is drunk, drunk as a lord, and he’ll go on drinking for another week; what is there to wait for? And what if Samsonov sent me here on purpose? And what if she… Oh God, what have I done!…”

The peasant sat looking at him and chuckling. Had it been any other time, Mitya might have killed this fool in his rage; but now he himself had grown as weak as a child. He went quietly to the bench, took his overcoat, put it on in silence and went out of the room. In the forester’s half he found no one; there was nobody there. He took fifty kopecks in small change out of his pocket and put it on the table, for the night’s lodging, for the candle and for the trouble. Coming out of the cottage, he saw that all round there was only forest and nothing more. He went at random, not even remembering which way to turn from the cottage, to the right or to the left; last night, hurrying here with the priest, he had not noticed the road. There was no thought of revenge on anyone in his soul, not even on Samsonov. He strode along the narrow forest track senselessly, at a loss, with a “lost idea”, and not caring at all where he was going. A child he met could have knocked him down, so weak had he suddenly become in soul and body. Somehow, however, he made his way out of the forest: suddenly reaped, bare fields stretched before him, boundless. “What despair, what death all round!” he repeated, striding on and on.

He was saved by travellers: a driver was taking an old merchant along the country road. When they came abreast, Mitya asked the way, and it turned out that they too were going to Volovya. They struck a bargain and took Mitya along. About three hours later they arrived. At Volovya station Mitya at once ordered post-horses to the town, and suddenly realised that he was impossibly hungry. While the horses were being harnessed they cooked him some fried eggs. He ate them all in a moment, ate the whole big hunk of bread, ate some sausage they found and drank three glasses of vodka. Fortified, he cheered up, and his soul grew bright again. He flew along the road, urged the driver on, and suddenly made a new and now “infallible” plan for getting “this cursed money” that very day before evening. “And to think, only to think, that a man’s fate is being ruined for the sake of these miserable three thousand!” he exclaimed contemptuously. “I shall settle it today!” And had it not been for the incessant thought of Grushenka, and whether something had happened to her, he might perhaps have become quite cheerful again. But the thought of her pierced his soul every minute like a sharp knife. At last they arrived, and Mitya at once ran to Grushenka.
