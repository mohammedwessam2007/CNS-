## CHAPTER VI

“...I don’t believe it! I can’t believe it!” repeated the perplexed Razumikhin, trying with all his might to refute Raskolnikov’s arguments. They were already approaching Bakaleev’s rooms, where Pulkheria Alexandrovna and Dunya had long been waiting for them. Razumikhin kept stopping on the way in the heat of the conversation, troubled and agitated by the mere fact that they had for the first time spoken of this openly.

“Don’t believe it!” Raskolnikov answered with a cold and careless smile. “You, as usual, noticed nothing, but I was weighing every word.”

“You are suspicious, that’s why you were weighing... Hm... it’s true, I agree, Porfiry’s tone was rather strange, and especially that scoundrel Zamyotov!.. You’re right, there was something in him; but why? Why?”

“He thought things over during the night.”

“But on the contrary, on the contrary! If they had that brainless idea, they would have tried with all their might to hide it and conceal their cards, so as to catch you later... But as it is, it’s insolent and careless!”

“If they had facts, that is, real facts, or even any suspicions with some foundation, then they really would have tried to hide their game in the hope of winning still more (though they would have searched the place long ago!). But they have no fact, not a single one; it’s all a mirage, everything cuts both ways, one flighty idea; so they are trying to throw me with insolence. And perhaps he was angry himself that there are no facts, and burst out in vexation. Or perhaps he has some intention... He seems to be an intelligent man... Perhaps he wanted to frighten me with the fact that he knows... There’s a psychology of its own here, brother... But it’s loathsome to explain all this. Drop it!”

“And insulting, insulting! I understand you! But... since we have now spoken openly (and it’s splendid that we have spoken openly at last, I’m glad!), I’ll confess to you straight out now that I noticed it in them long ago, this idea, all this time, only in the tiniest form, of course, a creeping form, but why even a creeping one! How dare they? Where, where do these roots of theirs lie hidden? If you knew how furious I was! Think of it: because a poor student, disfigured by poverty and hypochondria, on the eve of a cruel illness with delirium which had perhaps already begun in him (note that!), suspicious, proud, knowing his own worth, who had not seen anyone for six months in his corner, in rags and boots without soles, stands in front of some local police officers and puts up with their insults; and there is an unexpected debt under his nose, an overdue bill held by Court Councillor Chebarov, the stink of paint, thirty degrees Réaumur, stale air, a crowd of people, the story of the murder of a person he had visited the day before, and all this on an empty stomach! How could a fainting fit not happen! And on that, on that they base everything! The devil take it! I understand it’s annoying, but in your place, Rodka, I would laugh in all their faces, or better: I would spi-it in all their mugs, and good and thick, and hand out a couple of dozen slaps in all directions, cleverly, as they always should be given, and leave it at that. Spit on it! Cheer up! It’s shameful!”

“He put that well, though,” thought Raskolnikov.

“Spit on it? And tomorrow another interrogation!” he said bitterly. “Am I really to start explaining myself to them? It’s bad enough that yesterday in the tavern I lowered myself to Zamyotov...”

“The devil take it! I’ll go to Porfiry myself! And I’ll squeeze him, as a relation; let him lay it all out for me down to the roots! And as for Zamyotov...”

“He’s guessed at last!” thought Raskolnikov.

“Wait!” shouted Razumikhin, suddenly seizing him by the shoulder. “Wait! You’ve got it wrong! I’ve thought it over: you’ve got it wrong! What sort of trap was that? You say the question about the workmen was a trap? Work it out: well, if you had done it, could you have let slip that you saw the flat being painted... and the workmen? On the contrary: you saw nothing, even if you had seen it! Who confesses against himself?”

“If I had done that thing, I would certainly have said that I saw both the workmen and the flat,” Raskolnikov went on answering, reluctantly and with visible disgust.

“But why speak against yourself?”

“Because only peasants, or the most inexperienced novices, deny everything flatly and all at once under questioning. A man who is in the least developed and experienced will certainly try, as far as possible, to admit all the external facts that cannot be got rid of; only he looks for other causes for them, slips in some feature of his own, special and unexpected, which gives them quite another meaning and shows them in another light. Porfiry might have been counting precisely on my answering like that, and on my certainly saying that I had seen them, to sound plausible, and slipping in something by way of explanation...”

“But then he would have told you at once that two days before there couldn’t have been any workmen there, and that therefore you were there precisely on the day of the murder, between seven and eight. He would have tripped you up on nothing!”

“But that is just what he was counting on, that I wouldn’t have time to think, and would hurry precisely to answer more plausibly, and would forget that two days before there could have been no workmen.”

“But how could anyone forget that?”

“Nothing easier! It is on just such utterly trivial things that clever people are most easily tripped up. The cleverer a man is, the less he suspects that he will be tripped up on something simple. The cleverest man must be tripped up precisely on the simplest thing. Porfiry is not nearly so stupid as you think...”

“Then he’s a scoundrel after that!”

Raskolnikov could not help laughing. But at that very moment his own animation and eagerness seemed strange to him, with which he had spoken this last explanation, whereas he had kept up the whole preceding conversation with sullen disgust, obviously for a purpose, out of necessity.

“I am acquiring a taste for it on some points!” he thought to himself.

But almost at the same moment he suddenly became somehow uneasy, as if an unexpected and alarming thought had struck him. His uneasiness grew. They had already reached the entrance to Bakaleev’s rooms.

“Go in alone,” Raskolnikov said suddenly. “I’ll be back in a moment.”

“Where are you going? We’ve arrived!”

“I must, I must; business... I’ll come in half an hour... Tell them.”

“As you like, but I’m coming with you!”

“What, do you want to torment me too!” he cried with such bitter irritation, with such despair in his look, that Razumikhin’s hands dropped. For some time he stood on the steps and watched gloomily as the other strode quickly in the direction of his own lane. At last, clenching his teeth and his fists, and swearing on the spot that this very day he would squeeze Porfiry dry like a lemon, he went upstairs to calm Pulkheria Alexandrovna, who was already alarmed by their long absence.

When Raskolnikov reached his house, his temples were damp with sweat and he was breathing heavily. He went quickly up the stairs, entered his unlocked room and at once fastened the hook. Then, frightened and frantic, he rushed to the corner, to that same hole in the wallpaper where the things had lain that time, thrust his hand into it, and for several minutes carefully felt all over the hole, going through every nook and every fold of the paper. Finding nothing, he stood up and drew a deep breath. As he was approaching Bakaleev’s steps just now, he had suddenly imagined that some object, some little chain, a stud, or even the paper they had been wrapped in, with a mark in the old woman’s hand, might somehow have slipped out then and got lost in some little crack, and then suddenly come out before him as unexpected and irrefutable evidence.

He stood as if lost in thought, and a strange, humiliated, half-senseless smile wandered on his lips. At last he took his cap and went quietly out of the room. His thoughts were tangled. Thoughtfully he went down to the gateway.

“Why, here he is himself!” shouted a loud voice; he raised his head.

The caretaker was standing at the door of his little room and pointing straight at him for the benefit of a short man who looked like a tradesman, dressed in something like a long coat, with a waistcoat, and from a distance looking very much like a peasant woman. His head, in a greasy cap, hung down, and his whole body seemed hunched. His flabby, wrinkled face showed him to be over fifty; his small, puffy little eyes looked sullen, stern and displeased.

“What is it?” asked Raskolnikov, going up to the caretaker.

The tradesman gave him a sidelong look from under his brows and examined him intently and attentively, without hurrying; then he slowly turned round and, without saying a word, went out of the gate of the house into the street.

“But what is it!” cried Raskolnikov.

“Why, someone was asking whether a student lives here, gave your name, who you lodge with. You came down just then, I pointed you out, and off he went. Just look at that.”

The caretaker was also somewhat perplexed, though not very much, and after thinking a tiny bit more he turned and crawled back into his little room.

Raskolnikov rushed after the tradesman and at once saw him walking along the other side of the street with the same even, unhurried step, his eyes fixed on the ground, as if thinking something over. He soon caught up with him, but for a while walked behind him; at last he drew level with him and looked into his face from the side. The man noticed him at once, gave him a quick look, but lowered his eyes again, and so they walked for about a minute, side by side, without saying a word.

“You were asking for me... at the caretaker’s?” Raskolnikov said at last, but somehow very quietly.

The tradesman gave no answer and did not even look at him. Again they were silent.

“But why do you... come asking... and say nothing... what is the meaning of this?” Raskolnikov’s voice kept breaking, and the words somehow would not come out clearly.

This time the tradesman raised his eyes and looked at Raskolnikov with an ominous, gloomy look.

“Murderer!” he said suddenly, in a quiet but clear and distinct voice...

Raskolnikov walked beside him. His legs suddenly grew terribly weak, a chill ran down his back, and his heart seemed to stop for an instant; then suddenly it began to pound, as if it had slipped off a hook. So they walked about a hundred paces, side by side, and again in complete silence.

The tradesman did not look at him.

“But what do you... what... who is a murderer?” Raskolnikov muttered, barely audibly.

“You are a murderer,” the man said, still more distinctly and impressively, and as if with a smile of some hateful triumph, and again he looked straight into Raskolnikov’s pale face and his deadened eyes. They had both reached a crossroads by then. The tradesman turned into the street on the left and walked on without looking back. Raskolnikov stayed where he was and for a long time looked after him. He saw how the man, after some fifty paces, turned and looked back at him, still standing motionless on the same spot. It was impossible to make it out, but it seemed to Raskolnikov that this time too the man smiled his coldly hateful and triumphant smile.

With a quiet, weakened step, with trembling knees, and as if terribly chilled, Raskolnikov went back and climbed up to his little room. He took off his cap and put it on the table, and for ten minutes stood beside it, motionless. Then he lay down helplessly on the sofa and stretched out on it painfully, with a faint groan; his eyes were closed. So he lay for about half an hour.

He was not thinking about anything. There were, it is true, some thoughts or scraps of thoughts, some images, without order or connection: faces of people he had seen as a child or met somewhere only once and would never have remembered; the bell tower of the V— church; a billiard table in a certain tavern and some officer by the billiard table, the smell of cigars in some basement tobacco shop, a drinking house, a back staircase, quite dark, all slopped with dishwater and strewn with eggshells, and from somewhere comes the Sunday ringing of bells... The objects changed and whirled like a whirlwind. Some of them he even liked, and he clung to them, but they faded out, and in general something was pressing on him inside, but not very much. At times it was even pleasant... A slight chill would not go away, and that too was almost pleasant to feel.

He heard Razumikhin’s hurried footsteps and his voice, closed his eyes and pretended to be asleep. Razumikhin opened the door and for some time stood on the threshold, as if considering. Then he stepped quietly into the room and carefully came up to the sofa. Nastasya’s whisper was heard:

“Don’t disturb him; let him have his sleep out; he can eat after.”

“Quite right,” answered Razumikhin.

Both went out carefully and shut the door. Another half hour passed. Raskolnikov opened his eyes and flung himself onto his back again, clasping his hands behind his head...

“Who is he? Who is this man who has come up out of the ground? Where was he and what did he see? He saw everything, there is no doubt of that. Where was he standing then, and where was he watching from? Why is he only now coming up out of the floor? And how could he have seen, is it possible?.. Hm...” Raskolnikov went on, growing cold and shuddering, “and the jewel case that Nikolai found behind the door: was that possible too? Evidence? Overlook the hundred-thousandth little line, and there you have evidence the size of an Egyptian pyramid! A fly was flying about, it saw! Is such a thing possible?”

And with loathing he suddenly felt how weak he had grown, physically weak.

“I ought to have known this,” he thought with a bitter smile, “and how dared I, knowing myself, foreseeing myself, take an axe and bloody myself! I was bound to know beforehand... Eh! but I did know beforehand!..” he whispered in despair.

At times he stopped motionless before some thought:

“No, those people are not made like this; the real master, to whom everything is permitted, storms Toulon, makes a massacre in Paris, forgets an army in Egypt, spends half a million men on the Moscow campaign and gets off with a pun in Vilna; and after his death they put up idols to him; and so everything is permitted. No, such people, it seems, are made not of flesh but of bronze!”

One sudden irrelevant thought almost made him laugh:

“Napoleon, the pyramids, Waterloo, and a skinny, nasty little registrar’s widow, a little old moneylender, with a red trunk under her bed: well, how is even Porfiry Petrovich to digest that!.. How are they to digest it!.. Aesthetics will get in the way: would Napoleon, they’ll say, crawl under the bed of a ‘little old woman’! Eh, rubbish!..”

At moments he felt that he was as if delirious: he fell into a feverishly exalted mood.

“The old woman is nonsense!” he thought hotly and impetuously. “The old woman, perhaps, was a mistake; she is not the point! The old woman was only an illness... I wanted to step over as quickly as possible... I didn’t kill a human being, I killed a principle! I killed the principle all right, but I didn’t step over, I stayed on this side... All I managed to do was kill. And I didn’t manage even that, it turns out... A principle? Why was that little fool Razumikhin abusing the socialists earlier? A hard-working and commercial people; they busy themselves with ‘the general happiness’... No, life is given to me once, and there will never be any more of it; I don’t want to wait for ‘universal happiness’. I want to live myself, or else better not to live at all. Well? I only didn’t want to walk past a hungry mother, clutching my rouble in my pocket, waiting for ‘universal happiness’. ‘I am carrying my little brick for universal happiness,’ they say, ‘and so I feel peace of heart.’ Ha-ha! Then why did you let me slip by? I live only once, after all, I want to too... Eh, I am an aesthetic louse, and nothing more,” he added suddenly, laughing like a madman. “Yes, I really am a louse,” he went on, clinging to the thought with malicious glee, rummaging in it, playing with it and amusing himself with it, “and if only because, first, I am now reasoning about being a louse; because, second, for a whole month I troubled all-merciful Providence, calling it to witness that I was undertaking this not for my own flesh and lust, but had in view a magnificent and agreeable goal, ha-ha! Because, third, I resolved to observe all possible justice in carrying it out, weight and measure and arithmetic: out of all the lice I chose the most utterly useless one and, having killed her, resolved to take from her exactly as much as I needed for the first step, no more and no less (and the rest, then, would simply go to the monastery, by her will, ha-ha!)... Because, because I am finally a louse,” he added, grinding his teeth, “because I myself am perhaps still viler and nastier than the louse I killed, and I had a presentiment beforehand that I would say this to myself after I had killed her! Can anything compare with such horror! Oh, the vulgarity! Oh, the baseness!.. Oh, how I understand ‘the prophet’, with his sabre, on his horse. Allah commands, and obey, ‘trembling’ creature! He is right, right, ‘the prophet’, when he sets up a fi-i-ine battery somewhere across the street and blasts away at the righteous and the guilty, without even condescending to explain! Obey, trembling creature, and do not wish, because it is not your business!.. Oh, never, never will I forgive the old woman!”

His hair was soaked with sweat, his quivering lips were parched, his fixed gaze was turned to the ceiling.

“Mother, sister, how I loved them! Why do I hate them now? Yes, I hate them, physically hate them, I can’t bear them near me... Earlier I went up and kissed Mother, I remember... To embrace her and think that if she knew, then... should I tell her then? It would be like me... Hm! she must be the same as I am,” he added, thinking with an effort, as if struggling with the delirium that was taking hold of him. “Oh, how I hate the old woman now! I think I would kill her again if she came to life! Poor Lizaveta! Why did she have to turn up there!.. Strange, though, why I hardly think of her at all, as if I hadn’t killed her?.. Lizaveta! Sonya! Poor, gentle ones, with gentle eyes... Dear ones!.. Why don’t they weep? Why don’t they moan?.. They give everything... they look gently and quietly... Sonya, Sonya! Quiet Sonya!..”

He lost consciousness; it seemed strange to him that he did not remember how he could have come to be in the street. It was already late evening. The twilight was thickening, the full moon shone brighter and brighter; but the air was somehow especially stifling. People were walking through the streets in crowds; craftsmen and busy people were going home, others were strolling; there was a smell of lime, dust, stagnant water. Raskolnikov walked along sad and preoccupied: he remembered very well that he had come out of the house with some intention, that he had to do something and hurry, but what exactly he had forgotten. Suddenly he stopped and saw that on the other side of the street, on the pavement, a man was standing and waving to him. He went towards him across the street, but suddenly the man turned and walked away as if nothing had happened, his head lowered, not turning round and giving no sign that he had called him. “But come, did he call me?” thought Raskolnikov, yet he began to catch up with him. Ten paces short of him he suddenly recognised him and was frightened: it was the tradesman from earlier, in the same long coat and just as hunched. Raskolnikov followed at a distance; his heart was pounding; they turned into a lane; the man still did not turn round. “Does he know that I am following him?” thought Raskolnikov. The tradesman went in at the gate of a large house. Raskolnikov quickly came up to the gate and began to watch: wouldn’t he look round and call him? Indeed, having gone through the whole archway and already coming out into the courtyard, the man suddenly turned round and again seemed to wave to him. Raskolnikov at once went through the archway, but the tradesman was no longer in the courtyard. So he must have gone straight in here, up the first staircase. Raskolnikov rushed after him. Indeed, two flights higher someone’s measured, unhurried footsteps could still be heard. Strange, the staircase seemed familiar! There is the window on the first floor; the moonlight passed sadly and mysteriously through the panes; and here is the second floor. Ba! This is the very flat in which the workmen were painting... How was it he hadn’t recognised it at once? The steps of the man going ahead died away: “so he has stopped, or hidden himself somewhere.” Here is the third floor; should he go on? And what a silence there, it is even frightening... But he went on. The noise of his own footsteps frightened and disturbed him. God, how dark it is! The tradesman must be lurking somewhere here in a corner. Ah! the flat stands wide open onto the stairs; he thought for a moment and went in. The hall was very dark and empty, not a soul, as if everything had been carried out; quietly, on tiptoe, he went into the sitting room: the whole room was flooded brightly with moonlight; everything was as before: the chairs, the mirror, the yellow sofa and the little framed pictures. An enormous, round, copper-red moon looked straight in at the windows. “It is because of the moon that it is so silent,” thought Raskolnikov, “it must be setting a riddle now.” He stood and waited, waited a long time, and the quieter the moon was, the harder his heart pounded, until it even began to hurt. And still silence. Suddenly there was a momentary dry crack, as if a splinter of kindling had been snapped, and everything was still again. A fly that had woken up suddenly flew against the glass and buzzed plaintively. At that very moment, in the corner between the small cupboard and the window, he made out what seemed to be a woman’s cloak hanging on the wall. “Why is there a cloak here?” he thought. “It wasn’t here before...” He went up to it quietly and guessed that someone seemed to be hiding behind the cloak. Carefully he drew the cloak aside with his hand and saw that a chair stood there, and on the chair in the corner sat the old woman, all hunched up, her head bent so that he could not possibly make out her face, but it was she. He stood over her: “She’s afraid!” he thought, quietly freed the axe from the loop and struck the old woman on the crown of the head, once and again. But strangely: she did not even stir from the blows, as if she were made of wood. He was frightened, bent closer and began to examine her; but she bent her head still lower. Then he bent right down to the floor and looked up into her face from below, looked, and went numb: the old woman was sitting and laughing, simply shaking with quiet, inaudible laughter, holding herself in with all her strength so that he should not hear her. Suddenly it seemed to him that the bedroom door had opened a tiny crack and that in there too someone seemed to be laughing and whispering. Fury overcame him: with all his strength he began beating the old woman on the head, but with every blow of the axe the laughter and whispering from the bedroom rang out louder and more audibly, and the old woman was simply heaving all over with laughter. He rushed to run away, but the whole hall was already full of people, the doors onto the stairs stood wide open, and on the landing, on the stairs and all the way down: people everywhere, head to head, all looking, but all keeping still and waiting, silent... His heart contracted, his legs would not move, they had grown into the floor... He wanted to cry out and woke up.

He drew breath heavily; but strangely, the dream seemed to be still going on: his door stood wide open, and on the threshold stood a man completely unknown to him, examining him intently.

Raskolnikov had not yet fully opened his eyes and instantly closed them again. He lay on his back and did not stir. “Is the dream going on or not?” he thought, and very slightly, imperceptibly, raised his lashes again to look: the stranger was standing in the same place and went on gazing at him. Suddenly he stepped carefully over the threshold, cautiously shut the door behind him, went up to the table, waited a minute, all the time not taking his eyes off him, and quietly, without a sound, sat down on the chair beside the sofa; he put his hat on the floor beside him, and leaned both hands on his cane, resting his chin on his hands. It was evident that he had prepared himself to wait a long time. As far as could be made out through the blinking lashes, the man was no longer young, thickset, and with a thick, fair, almost white beard...

About ten minutes passed. It was still light, but evening was coming on. There was complete silence in the room. Not a single sound came even from the stairs. Only some big fly was buzzing and beating about, flying against the glass. At last it became unbearable: Raskolnikov suddenly raised himself and sat up on the sofa.

“Well, speak, what do you want?”

“And I knew all along that you were not asleep but only pretending,” the stranger answered strangely, laughing calmly. “Arkady Ivanovich Svidrigailov, allow me to introduce myself...”
