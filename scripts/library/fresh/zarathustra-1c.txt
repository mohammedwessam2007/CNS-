## Zarathustra’s Speeches · On the Three Metamorphoses

Three metamorphoses of the spirit I name for you: how the spirit becomes a camel, and the camel a lion, and the lion at last a child.

There is much that is heavy for the spirit, the strong, load-bearing spirit in which reverence dwells: its strength longs for the heavy, the heaviest.

What is heavy? So asks the load-bearing spirit; so it kneels down like the camel and wants to be well loaded.

What is the heaviest thing, you heroes? So asks the load-bearing spirit, that I may take it upon me and rejoice in my strength.

Is it not this: to humble oneself in order to wound one’s pride? To let one’s folly shine out in order to mock one’s wisdom?

Or is it this: to part from our cause when it celebrates its victory? To climb high mountains in order to tempt the tempter?

Or is it this: to feed on the acorns and grass of knowledge, and for the sake of truth to suffer hunger of the soul?

Or is it this: to be sick and send the comforters home, and make friends with the deaf, who never hear what you want?

Or is it this: to step into dirty water when it is the water of truth, and not to push away cold frogs and hot toads?

Or is it this: to love those who despise us, and to hold out our hand to the ghost when it wants to frighten us?

All these heaviest things the load-bearing spirit takes upon itself: like the camel that hurries laden into the desert, so it hurries into its desert.

But in the loneliest desert the second metamorphosis happens: here the spirit becomes a lion; it wants to seize freedom as its prey and be master in its own desert.

Here it seeks out its last master: it wants to be the enemy of him and of its last God; it wants to wrestle for victory with the great dragon.

What is the great dragon that the spirit no longer wants to call master and God? “Thou shalt” is the name of the great dragon. But the spirit of the lion says “I will”.

“Thou shalt” lies in its path, glittering with gold, a scaly beast, and on every scale shines in gold: “Thou shalt!”

Values a thousand years old glitter on these scales, and thus speaks the mightiest of all dragons: “All the value of things: it shines on me.

“All value has already been created, and all created value: that is I. Truly, there shall be no more ‘I will’!” Thus speaks the dragon.

My brothers, why is the lion needed in the spirit? Why is the beast of burden, which renounces and is reverent, not enough?

To create new values: even the lion cannot yet do that. But to create freedom for itself for new creating: that the might of the lion can do.

To create freedom for oneself, and a sacred No even to duty: for that, my brothers, the lion is needed.

To take for oneself the right to new values: that is the most terrible taking for a load-bearing and reverent spirit. Truly, to it this is robbery, the work of a beast of prey.

Once it loved “Thou shalt” as most sacred: now it must find illusion and caprice even in the most sacred, so that it may rob itself of freedom from its love: the lion is needed for this robbery.

But tell me, my brothers, what can the child do that even the lion could not? Why must the preying lion still become a child?

The child is innocence and forgetting, a new beginning, a game, a wheel rolling of itself, a first movement, a sacred Yes.

Yes, for the game of creating, my brothers, a sacred Yes is needed: the spirit now wills *its own* will; the one who was lost to the world now wins *its own* world.

Three metamorphoses of the spirit I have named for you: how the spirit became a camel, and the camel a lion, and the lion at last a child.

Thus spoke Zarathustra. And at that time he was staying in the town called the Motley Cow.

## On the Chairs of Virtue

People praised to Zarathustra a wise man who was said to speak well about sleep and virtue: he was greatly honoured and rewarded for it, and all the young men sat before his chair. To him Zarathustra went, and he sat with all the young men before his chair. And thus spoke the wise man:

“Honour and modesty before sleep! That is the first thing! And avoid all who sleep badly and lie awake at night!

“Even the thief is modest before sleep: he always steals softly through the night. But shameless is the night watchman; shamelessly he carries his horn.

“Sleeping is no small art: it takes staying awake all day for it.

“Ten times a day you must overcome yourself: that makes a good tiredness and is poppy for the soul.

“Ten times you must be reconciled with yourself again; for overcoming is bitterness, and the unreconciled sleep badly.

“Ten truths you must find in a day: otherwise you will still be seeking truth at night, and your soul will have stayed hungry.

“Ten times you must laugh in a day and be cheerful: otherwise your stomach, that father of gloom, will disturb you in the night.

“Few know it, but one must have all the virtues to sleep well. Shall I bear false witness? Shall I commit adultery?

“Shall I covet my neighbour’s maidservant? All that would go badly with good sleep.

“And even if one has all the virtues, one must understand one thing more: to send even the virtues to sleep at the right time.

“So that they do not quarrel with one another, the pretty little women! And over you, you wretch!

“Peace with God and your neighbour: so good sleep wills it. And peace even with your neighbour’s devil! Otherwise he will haunt you at night.

“Honour to the authorities, and obedience, even to crooked authorities! So good sleep wills it. Can I help it if power likes to walk on crooked legs?

“He shall always be the best shepherd for me who leads his sheep to the greenest meadow: that goes well with good sleep.

“I do not want many honours, nor great treasures: that inflames the spleen. But one sleeps badly without a good name and a little treasure.

“A little company is more welcome to me than bad company: but it must come and go at the right time. That goes well with good sleep.

“I am also very fond of the poor in spirit: they promote sleep. Blessed are they, especially if one always agrees with them.

“So the day passes for the virtuous. And when night comes, I take good care not to call sleep! Sleep, the master of the virtues, does not want to be called!

“Instead I think of what I have done and thought during the day. Chewing it over, I ask myself, patient as a cow: what were your ten overcomings?

“And what were the ten reconciliations, and the ten truths, and the ten laughs with which my heart enjoyed itself?

“Weighing such things and rocked by forty thoughts, suddenly sleep falls upon me, the uncalled, the master of the virtues.

“Sleep taps on my eyes: they grow heavy. Sleep touches my mouth: it stays open.

“Truly, on soft soles he comes to me, the dearest of thieves, and steals my thoughts: I stand there stupid as this chair.

“But I do not stand long then: already I am lying down.”

When Zarathustra heard the wise man speak thus, he laughed in his heart: for a light had dawned on him. And he spoke thus to his heart:

“This wise man with his forty thoughts is a fool to me: but I believe he knows well how to sleep.

“Happy is anyone who lives near this wise man! Such sleep is catching; it is catching even through a thick wall.

“A magic dwells even in his chair. And not in vain did the young men sit before the preacher of virtue.

“His wisdom is: stay awake in order to sleep well. And truly, if life had no meaning and I had to choose nonsense, this would be for me too the nonsense most worth choosing.

“Now I understand clearly what people once sought above all when they sought teachers of virtue. They sought good sleep for themselves, and poppy-flowered virtues to go with it!

“For all these praised wise men of the lecture chairs, wisdom was sleep without dreams: they knew no better meaning of life.

“Even today there are some like this preacher of virtue, and not always so honest: but their time is up. And they will not stand much longer: already they are lying down.

“Blessed are these sleepy ones: for they shall soon nod off.”

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
