## Book Five, Chapter IV · Rebellion

“I have a confession to make to you,” Ivan began. “I have never been able to understand how one can love one’s neighbours. It is precisely one’s neighbours, in my view, that it is impossible to love; only those far away, perhaps. I read somewhere once about John the Merciful, a saint, that when a hungry, frozen passer-by came to him and asked to be warmed, he lay down with him in his bed, put his arms round him and began breathing into his mouth, which was festering and foul from some terrible disease. I am convinced he did it out of the strain of a lie, out of a love ordered by duty, out of a penance he had loaded on himself. For a man to be loved he has to hide; the moment he shows his face, love is gone.”

“Elder Zosima spoke about that more than once,” Alyosha remarked. “He also said that a man’s face often keeps people who are not yet practised in love from loving him. But there is a great deal of love in mankind all the same, almost like Christ’s love. I know that myself, Ivan…”

“Well, I don’t know it yet, and I can’t understand it, and countless people are with me there. The question is whether it comes from people’s bad qualities or whether it is simply their nature. In my opinion Christ’s love for people is, in its way, a miracle impossible on earth. True, he was God. But we are not gods. Suppose, for instance, that I can suffer deeply; another person can never know how deeply I suffer, because he is someone else and not me, and besides, a man rarely agrees to recognise another as a sufferer (as if it were a rank). Why won’t he, do you think? Because, say, I smell bad, because I have a stupid face, because I once trod on his foot. Then there is suffering and suffering. Degrading suffering, suffering that humiliates me, hunger for instance, my benefactor will still allow me; but suffering a little higher, for an idea for instance, no, that he allows only in rare cases, because he will look at me and suddenly see that I don’t have at all the face that, in his fancy, a man suffering for such and such an idea ought to have. And so he at once withdraws his kindness from me, and not even out of a wicked heart. Beggars, especially noble beggars, ought never to show themselves in the street; they should ask for alms through the newspapers. One can love one’s neighbour in the abstract, and sometimes even from a distance, but close up almost never. If everything were as it is on the stage, in the ballet, where the beggars come on in silken rags and torn lace and beg for alms while dancing gracefully, well, then one might admire them. Admire them, but still not love them. But enough of that. I only needed to put you where I stand. I meant to speak of the suffering of mankind in general, but let us rather keep to the sufferings of children alone. That cuts the scale of my argument by about ten times, but better to keep to children. All the worse for me, of course. But, in the first place, children can be loved even close up, even dirty ones, even ugly ones (though it seems to me that children are never ugly). In the second place, I won’t speak of grown-ups because, besides being disgusting and undeserving of love, they have their retribution: they ate the apple and knew good and evil and became ‘as gods’. They are still eating it. But the little ones have eaten nothing and are not yet guilty of anything. Do you love children, Alyosha? I know you do, and you will understand why I want to speak only of them now. If they too suffer terribly on earth, then of course it is for their fathers, they are punished for their fathers who ate the apple; but that is reasoning from another world, and the human heart here on earth cannot understand it. An innocent cannot suffer for another, and such an innocent at that! Wonder at me, Alyosha: I love little children terribly too. And note this: cruel people, passionate, carnivorous people, Karamazovs, sometimes love children very much. Children, while they are children, up to seven years old for instance, stand terribly far from people, as if they were quite another creature with another nature. I knew a robber in prison. In the course of his career, slaughtering whole families in the houses he broke into at night to rob, he had happened to cut the throats of several children as well. But sitting in prison he loved them strangely. He did nothing but look out of the prison window at the children playing in the yard. He taught one little boy to come and stand under his window, and the boy became great friends with him… You don’t know why I am telling you all this, Alyosha? My head aches somehow, and I am sad.”

“You speak with a strange look,” Alyosha remarked uneasily, “as if you were in some sort of madness.”

“By the way, a Bulgarian in Moscow was telling me lately,” Ivan Fyodorovich went on, as though not listening to his brother, “how the Turks and Circassians there in Bulgaria commit atrocities everywhere, fearing a general rising of the Slavs: they burn, they cut throats, they violate women and children, they nail prisoners to fences by the ears and leave them so till morning, and in the morning they hang them, and so on; it is impossible even to imagine it all. People sometimes speak of man’s ‘bestial’ cruelty, but that is terribly unjust and insulting to the beasts: a beast can never be so cruel as a man, so artfully, so artistically cruel. A tiger simply gnaws and tears; that is all he knows how to do. It would never enter his head to nail people up by the ears for the night, even if he could. These Turks, among other things, tortured children with relish, from cutting them out of their mothers’ wombs with a dagger to tossing nursing babies into the air and catching them on a bayonet before their mothers’ eyes. It was doing it before the mothers’ eyes that made the chief sweetness of it. But here is one little picture that interested me greatly. Imagine: a nursing baby in the arms of its trembling mother, and round them the Turks who have come in. They have thought up an amusing bit of fun: they pet the baby, they laugh to make it laugh; they succeed, the baby laughs. At that moment a Turk points a pistol at it, four inches from its face. The little boy laughs with joy, stretches out his little hands to grab the pistol, and suddenly the artist pulls the trigger right in his face and shatters his little head… Artistic, isn’t it? By the way, they say the Turks are very fond of sweet things.”

“Brother, what is all this for?” asked Alyosha.

“I think that if the devil doesn’t exist, and man has therefore created him, he has created him in his own image and likeness.”

“In that case, just as he did God.”

“You are wonderfully good at turning words round, as Polonius says in Hamlet,” Ivan laughed. “You have caught me out; so be it, I’m glad. A fine God yours is, if man created him in his own image and likeness. You asked just now what all this is for. You see, I am a lover and collector of certain little facts, and, would you believe it, I write down and collect from the newspapers and from people’s stories, from wherever I can, a certain kind of anecdote, and I already have a good collection. The Turks have gone into the collection, of course, but they are all foreigners. I have home-grown specimens too, even better than the Turkish ones. You know, with us it is more beating, more the birch and the lash, and that is national: nailed-up ears are unthinkable with us, we are Europeans after all, but the birch, the lash, that is something of our own and cannot be taken from us. Abroad nowadays they seem hardly to beat at all; manners have been purified, or else the laws have been arranged so that a man no longer dares to flog a man. But they have made up for it with something else, also purely national, as ours is, and so national that with us it seems almost impossible, though it does seem to be taking root here too, especially since the religious movement in our high society. I have a charming little pamphlet, translated from the French, about how in Geneva, quite recently, only five years ago, they executed a villain and murderer called Richard, a young fellow of twenty-three, I think, who repented and was converted to the Christian faith at the very foot of the scaffold. This Richard was someone’s illegitimate child, whom his parents, when he was a little thing of six, made a present of to some Swiss mountain shepherds, and they raised him to use him for work. He grew up among them like a little wild animal. The shepherds taught him nothing; on the contrary, at seven they were already sending him out to graze the flock, in the wet and the cold, almost without clothes and almost without feeding him. And of course nobody who did this thought twice about it or repented of it; on the contrary, each considered himself fully in the right, because Richard had been given to them as a thing, and they did not even think it necessary to feed him. Richard himself testifies that in those years, like the prodigal son in the Gospel, he longed terribly to eat even the mash they gave to the pigs being fattened for sale; but they would not give him even that, and beat him when he stole from the pigs. And so he spent his whole childhood and youth, until he grew up and, having gained his strength, went out to steal for himself. The savage began to earn money as a day labourer in Geneva, drank what he earned, lived like a monster, and ended by killing some old man and robbing him. He was caught, tried and sentenced to death. They don’t go in for sentiment there. And in prison he is at once surrounded by pastors, members of various Christian brotherhoods, charitable ladies and the rest. In prison they taught him to read and write, began expounding the Gospel to him, worked on his conscience, persuaded him, pressed him, nagged him, squeezed him, and at last he solemnly confesses his crime himself. He was converted; he wrote to the court himself that he was a monster and that at last he had been found worthy of having the Lord enlighten him too and send him grace. All Geneva was stirred, all charitable and pious Geneva. Everything that was highest and best brought up rushed to him in prison. They kiss Richard, they embrace him: ‘You are our brother, grace has come down upon you!’ And Richard himself only weeps with tender feeling: ‘Yes, grace has come down upon me! Before, all my childhood and youth, I was glad of the pigs’ food, and now grace has come down on me too, I am dying in the Lord!’ ‘Yes, yes, Richard, die in the Lord; you have shed blood and must die in the Lord. You may not be guilty of not knowing the Lord at all when you envied the pigs their food and were beaten for stealing it from them (which was very wrong of you, for stealing is not allowed), but you have shed blood and must die.’ And so the last day comes. Richard, unstrung, weeps and does nothing but repeat every minute: ‘This is the best of my days, I am going to the Lord!’ ‘Yes,’ cry the pastors, the judges and the charitable ladies, ‘this is your happiest day, for you are going to the Lord!’ The whole crowd moves towards the scaffold behind the shameful cart in which Richard is carried, in carriages, on foot. They reach the scaffold. ‘Die, our brother,’ they cry to Richard, ‘die in the Lord, for grace has come down upon you too!’ And so, covered with his brothers’ kisses, brother Richard was dragged up onto the scaffold, laid on the guillotine, and they chopped his head off in brotherly fashion, because grace had come down upon him too. No, that is characteristic. The pamphlet was translated into Russian by some Lutheranising Russian philanthropists of high society and sent out free with the newspapers and other publications for the enlightenment of the Russian people. The thing about Richard is good because it is national. With us, though it would be absurd to cut off a brother’s head only because he has become our brother and grace has come down upon him, we have, I repeat, something of our own that is almost as good. With us it is a historical, immediate and intimate delight in the torment of beating. Nekrasov has a poem about how a peasant whips a horse across the eyes, ‘across its meek eyes’. Who hasn’t seen that? It is a Russianism. He describes how a feeble little nag with too much piled on it has got stuck with its cart and can’t pull it out. The peasant beats it, beats it in a fury, beats it at last not knowing what he is doing; drunk with beating, he flogs it painfully, past counting: ‘Even if you haven’t the strength, pull, die but pull!’ The nag strains, and he begins to lash it, defenceless as it is, across its weeping, its ‘meek eyes’. Beside itself, it gives a jerk and pulls the cart out and goes on, trembling all over, not breathing, somehow sideways, with a sort of skip, somehow unnaturally and shamefully. In Nekrasov it is horrible. But that is only a horse, and God himself gave us horses to be flogged. So the Tatars explained it to us, and left us the knout as a keepsake. But one can flog people too. And so an intelligent, educated gentleman and his lady flog their own little daughter, a child of seven, with birch rods. I have it written down in detail. Papa is glad that the twigs have knots in them: ‘it will sting more,’ he says, and so he begins to ‘sting’ his own daughter. I know for certain there are floggers who grow more heated with every stroke, to the point of voluptuousness, literal voluptuousness, more and more with each stroke that follows, in a progression. They flog for a minute, they flog at last for five minutes, they flog for ten minutes, further, harder, faster, more stinging. The child screams; the child at last cannot scream, she is choking: ‘Papa, papa, papa dear, papa dear!’ By some devilish, indecent chance the case comes to court. A lawyer is hired. Our Russian people long ago named the lawyer ‘a hired conscience’. The lawyer shouts in his client’s defence: ‘The matter is so simple, a family matter, an ordinary one; a father has flogged his daughter, and, to the shame of our times, it has come to court!’ The jury, convinced, retire and bring in an acquittal. The public roars with joy that the torturer has been acquitted. Ah, I wasn’t there; I would have bellowed a proposal to found a scholarship in the torturer’s name! Charming pictures. But about children I have still better ones; I have collected a great, great deal about Russian children, Alyosha. A little girl of five was hated by her father and mother, ‘most respectable people of rank, educated and well brought up’. You see, I affirm once more, positively, that there is a peculiar trait in many people: a love of torturing children, but children only. Towards all other members of the human race these same torturers behave even kindly and gently, like educated and humane Europeans, but they are very fond of tormenting children; they even love children in that sense. It is precisely the defencelessness of these creatures that tempts the torturers, the angelic trustfulness of a child who has nowhere to go and no one to turn to: that is what inflames the vile blood of the torturer. In every man, of course, a beast lies hidden: the beast of rage, the beast of lustful excitement at the cries of the tortured victim, the beast let off the chain with nothing to hold it, the beast of diseases picked up in debauchery, gout, a bad liver and the rest. These educated parents subjected that poor five-year-old girl to every kind of torture. They beat her, flogged her, kicked her, not knowing why themselves; they turned her whole body into bruises. At last they reached the height of refinement: in the cold, in the frost, they locked her up all night in the privy, and because she did not ask to be taken out at night (as if a child of five, sleeping her sound angelic sleep, could learn to ask at that age), for that they smeared her whole face with her excrement and made her eat it, and it was the mother, the mother who made her! And this mother could sleep while at night the moans of the poor little child locked up in that vile place could be heard! Do you understand that, when a little creature who cannot even grasp what is being done to her beats her torn little breast with her tiny fist in that vile place, in the dark and the cold, and weeps her bloody, unresentful, meek little tears to ‘dear God’ to protect her? Do you understand this nonsense, my friend and my brother, my humble novice of God, do you understand why this nonsense is so necessary and was created? Without it, they say, man could not even have lived on earth, for he would not have known good and evil. Why know this devilish good and evil, when it costs so much? Why, the whole world of knowledge is not worth that child’s little tears to ‘dear God’. I am not speaking of the sufferings of grown-ups; they ate the apple, and the devil take them, let the devil take them all, but these, these! I am torturing you, Alyoshka; you don’t look yourself. I’ll stop if you like.”

“Never mind, I want to be tortured too,” Alyosha muttered.

“One more, just one more little picture, and that only out of curiosity, because it is so very characteristic, and above all I have only just read it in one of our collections of antiquities, the Archive or Antiquity, I must look it up; I have even forgotten where I read it. It was in the darkest days of serfdom, at the beginning of the century, and long live the liberator of the people! There was at that time, at the beginning of the century, a general, a general with great connections and a very rich landowner, but one of those (even then, it seems, very few) who, when they retired from the service, were all but convinced that they had earned the right of life and death over their subjects. There were such men then. Well, the general lives on his estate of two thousand souls, puffed up, treating his small neighbours as his hangers-on and buffoons. He has kennels with hundreds of hounds and nearly a hundred huntsmen, all in uniform, all mounted. And one day a house serf’s boy, a little boy only eight years old, threw a stone in play and hurt the paw of the general’s favourite hound. ‘Why is my favourite dog lame?’ He is told that this very boy threw a stone at it and hurt its paw. ‘Ah, so it was you,’ the general looked him over. ‘Take him!’ They took him, took him from his mother; he spent the whole night in the lock-up. In the morning, at first light, the general rides out in full dress for the hunt, mounts his horse, with his hangers-on, his dogs, his kennelmen and huntsmen round him, all mounted. The house serfs are gathered round for their edification, and in front of them all, the guilty boy’s mother. The boy is brought out of the lock-up. A gloomy, cold, misty autumn day, a famous day for hunting. The general orders the boy to be undressed; the child is stripped stark naked; he shivers, he is out of his mind with fear, he dares not make a sound… ‘Drive him!’ the general commands. ‘Run, run!’ the kennelmen shout at him; the boy runs… ‘At him!’ the general yells, and sets the whole pack of borzois on him. He hunted him down before his mother’s eyes, and the hounds tore the child to pieces!… I believe the general was put under guardianship. Well… what is to be done with him? Shoot him? Shoot him for the satisfaction of our moral feelings? Speak, Alyoshka!”

“Shoot him!” Alyosha said quietly, raising his eyes to his brother with a pale, somehow twisted smile.

“Bravo!” Ivan yelled in a kind of rapture. “If you said it, then… What a hermit! So that is the little devil sitting in your heart, Alyoshka Karamazov!”

“I said something absurd, but…”

“That’s just it, the ‘but’…” Ivan shouted. “Know this, novice: absurdities are only too necessary on earth. The world stands on absurdities, and without them perhaps nothing would ever have happened in it at all. We know what we know!”

“What do you know?”

“I understand nothing,” Ivan went on as if in delirium, “and I don’t want to understand anything now. I want to stay with the fact. I decided long ago not to understand. If I want to understand something, I shall at once betray the fact, and I have decided to stay with the fact…”

“Why are you testing me?” Alyosha cried out with sorrowful strain. “Will you tell me at last?”

“Of course I’ll tell you; that is what I have been leading up to. You are dear to me; I don’t want to let you go, and I won’t give you up to your Zosima.”

Ivan was silent for a minute; his face suddenly became very sad.

“Listen to me. I took only children to make it clearer. Of the other human tears with which the whole earth is soaked from its crust to its centre I won’t say a word; I have narrowed my theme on purpose. I am a bedbug, and I admit in all humility that I cannot understand at all why everything is arranged as it is. People themselves are to blame, it seems: they were given paradise, they wanted freedom and stole fire from heaven, knowing themselves that they would become unhappy; so there is no need to pity them. Oh, with my pitiful, earthly, Euclidean mind, all I know is that there is suffering, that no one is guilty, that everything follows from everything else directly and simply, that everything flows and finds its level. But that is only Euclidean nonsense; I know that, don’t I, and I cannot agree to live by it! What is it to me that no one is guilty and that I know it? I need retribution, or else I shall destroy myself. And retribution not somewhere and some time in infinity, but here on earth, and so that I see it myself. I have believed; I want to see it for myself, and if I am dead by that hour, then let them raise me up, for if it all happens without me it will be too unfair. Surely I didn’t suffer so that I, my crimes and my sufferings, should manure the soil of some future harmony for somebody else. I want to see with my own eyes the hind lie down beside the lion and the murdered man rise up and embrace his murderer. I want to be there when everyone suddenly learns why it all was as it was. All the religions on earth are built on this longing, and I believe. But then there are the children, and what am I to do with them then? That is the question I cannot resolve. For the hundredth time I repeat: there are a multitude of questions, but I have taken only the children, because here what I need to say is irrefutably clear. Listen: if everyone must suffer in order to buy eternal harmony with suffering, what have children to do with it, tell me, please? It is quite incomprehensible why they too should have had to suffer, and why they should buy harmony with sufferings. Why should they too fall into the material and manure with themselves some future harmony for someone? I understand solidarity in sin among people; I understand solidarity in retribution too; but there is no solidarity in sin with children, and if the truth really is that they too share with their fathers in all their fathers’ crimes, then of course that truth is not of this world and is beyond my understanding. Some joker will say, perhaps, that the child would have grown up anyway and had time to sin; but there, he didn’t grow up; at eight years old he was hunted down by dogs. Oh, Alyosha, I am not blaspheming! I do understand what a shaking of the universe it must be when everything in heaven and under the earth flows into one voice of praise and everything living and that has lived cries out: ‘Thou art just, O Lord, for thy ways are revealed!’ When the mother embraces the torturer who had her son torn to pieces by his dogs, and all three cry out with tears, ‘Thou art just, O Lord,’ then of course the crown of knowledge will have come and everything will be explained. But that is just where the comma is; that is what I cannot accept. And while I am on earth I hasten to take my own measures. You see, Alyosha, it may really happen that when I myself live to see that moment, or rise again to see it, I too shall perhaps cry out with the rest, looking at the mother embracing her child’s torturer, ‘Thou art just, O Lord!’ But I don’t want to cry out then. While there is still time, I hasten to protect myself, and so I renounce the higher harmony altogether. It is not worth the tears of even that one tortured child who beat her breast with her little fist and prayed in her stinking kennel, with her unredeemed tears, to ‘dear God’! It is not worth it, because her tears were left unredeemed. They must be redeemed, or there can be no harmony. But with what, with what will you redeem them? Is it possible? By their being avenged? But what do I care for their being avenged, what do I care for a hell for the torturers, what can hell put right when those children have already been tortured? And what sort of harmony is it, if there is hell? I want to forgive, I want to embrace; I don’t want more suffering. And if the sufferings of children went to make up the sum of sufferings needed to buy truth, then I declare in advance that the whole of truth is not worth such a price. I don’t want, in the end, the mother to embrace the torturer who had her son torn to pieces by dogs! She dare not forgive him! If she wants to, let her forgive him for herself, let her forgive the torturer her own immeasurable mother’s suffering; but the sufferings of her torn child she has no right to forgive; she dare not forgive the torturer, even if the child himself forgave him! And if that is so, if they dare not forgive, where is the harmony? Is there in the whole world a being who could forgive and would have the right to? I don’t want harmony; out of love for mankind I don’t want it. I would rather remain with my suffering unavenged. I would rather remain with my unavenged suffering and my unappeased indignation, even if I were wrong. Besides, they have priced harmony too high; it is beyond our means to pay so much for admission. And so I hasten to give back my entrance ticket. And if I am an honest man, I am bound to give it back as far in advance as I can. That is what I am doing. It is not God I don’t accept, Alyosha; I only most respectfully return him the ticket.”

“That is rebellion,” Alyosha said quietly, lowering his eyes.

“Rebellion? I would rather not have had that word from you,” Ivan said with feeling. “Can one live by rebellion? And I want to live. Tell me yourself, straight out; I call on you, answer me: imagine that you yourself are building the edifice of human destiny with the aim of making people happy in the end, of giving them peace and rest at last, but that for this it was necessary and unavoidable to torture to death only one tiny creature, that very child who beat her breast with her little fist, and to found that edifice on her unavenged tears. Would you agree to be the architect on those terms? Tell me, and don’t lie!”

“No, I wouldn’t agree,” Alyosha said quietly.

“And can you admit the idea that the people for whom you are building would themselves agree to accept their happiness on the unjustified blood of a little tortured child, and, having accepted it, remain happy for ever?”

“No, I can’t admit it. Brother,” Alyosha said suddenly, his eyes flashing, “you asked just now whether there is in the whole world a being who could forgive and would have the right to. But there is such a being, and he can forgive everything, everyone and everything and for everything, because he himself gave his innocent blood for all and for everything. You have forgotten him, and it is on him that the edifice is founded, and it is to him that they will cry out: ‘Thou art just, O Lord, for thy ways are revealed.’”

“Ah, that is ‘the only sinless one’ and his blood! No, I haven’t forgotten him; on the contrary, I have been wondering all this time why you were so long in bringing him out, for usually in arguments all your people put him forward first of all. You know, Alyosha, don’t laugh: I once made up a poem, about a year ago. If you can waste another ten minutes with me, I’ll tell it to you.”

“You wrote a poem?”

“Oh no, I didn’t write it,” Ivan laughed, “and I have never in my life composed even two lines of verse. But I made up this poem and remembered it. I made it up with passion. You will be my first reader, that is, my first listener. Why indeed should an author lose even one listener?” Ivan smiled. “Shall I tell it or not?”

“I am listening hard,” said Alyosha.

“My poem is called ‘The Grand Inquisitor’. An absurd thing, but I want to tell it to you.”
