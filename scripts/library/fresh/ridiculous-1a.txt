## I

I am a ridiculous man. They call me mad now. That would be a promotion, if I weren’t still as ridiculous to them as ever. But I am not angry any more. They are all dear to me now, even when they laugh at me; then, in fact, they are somehow especially dear. I would laugh with them, not at myself but because I love them, if it did not make me so sad to look at them. Sad, because they do not know the truth, and I know the truth. Oh, how hard it is to be the only one who knows the truth! But they won’t understand that. No, they won’t.

Before, I used to suffer terribly from seeming ridiculous. Not seeming: being. I was always ridiculous, and I have known it, perhaps, since the day I was born. Perhaps I knew it already at seven. Then I went to school, then to university, and what happened? The more I learned, the more I learned that I was ridiculous. So that in the end all my university learning seemed to exist only to prove and explain to me, the deeper I went into it, that I was ridiculous. And life went the same way as learning. Every year the same awareness of how ridiculous I was in every respect grew and hardened in me. Everyone always laughed at me. But not one of them knew or guessed that if there was one man on earth who knew better than anyone that I was ridiculous, it was I myself; and that was what hurt me most, that they did not know it. But that was my own fault: I was always so proud that I would never, for anything, admit it to anyone. This pride grew in me with the years, and if it had ever happened that I let myself admit to anyone at all that I was ridiculous, I think I would have blown my brains out with a revolver that same evening.

Oh, how I suffered as a boy from the fear that I would break down and somehow admit it to my schoolfellows. But once I became a young man, though every year I learned more and more about my terrible quality, I grew a little calmer, for some reason. I say for some reason, because to this day I cannot say why. Perhaps because a terrible anguish was growing in my soul over one thing that was infinitely greater than all of me: the conviction that had come over me that nothing in the world matters. I had sensed it for a long time, but the full conviction came in the last year, somehow all at once. I suddenly felt that it would make no difference to me whether the world existed or whether there was nothing anywhere at all. I began to hear and feel with my whole being that there was nothing around me. At first I kept thinking that at least there had been a great deal before; but then I realised that there had been nothing before either, it had only seemed so for some reason. Little by little I became convinced that there would never be anything either.

Then I suddenly stopped being angry with people and almost stopped noticing them. Really, it showed even in the smallest trifles: I would walk along the street, for instance, and bump into people. And not because I was lost in thought: what was there to think about? I had stopped thinking altogether by then; nothing mattered to me. It would have been one thing if I had solved my questions; oh, I had not solved a single one, and there were so many! But nothing mattered to me, and all the questions went away.

And then, after that, I learned the truth. I learned the truth last November, the third of November to be exact, and since then I remember every moment of my life. It was a gloomy evening, the gloomiest evening there could be. I was going home after ten at night, and I remember thinking precisely that there could not be a gloomier time. Even physically. It had rained all day, the coldest, gloomiest rain, a menacing rain even, I remember, with an open hostility to people; and then suddenly, after ten, it stopped, and a terrible dampness set in, damper and colder than when it was raining, and a sort of steam rose from everything, from every stone in the street and from every side alley, if you looked deep into it from the street. I suddenly imagined that if the gas lamps went out everywhere it would be more cheerful, and that with the gas it was sadder for the heart, because the gas lit it all up.

I had hardly eaten that day, and since early evening I had been sitting at the place of an engineer I knew, with two other friends of his. I said nothing the whole time and, I think, bored them. They were talking about something provocative and suddenly even got heated. But it did not matter to them, I could see that; they got heated just like that. I suddenly told them so: “Gentlemen,” I said, “it doesn’t really matter to you.” They were not offended, but they all laughed at me. That was because I said it without any reproach, simply because nothing mattered to me. They saw that nothing mattered to me, and they found it funny.

When I thought about the gas lamps in the street, I looked up at the sky. The sky was terribly dark, but you could clearly make out torn clouds, and between them bottomless black patches. Suddenly I noticed a little star in one of those patches and began to look at it fixedly. That was because the little star gave me an idea: I decided to kill myself that night. I had firmly decided it two months before, and poor as I am I had bought a fine revolver and loaded it the same day. But two months had passed, and it was still lying in the drawer. Nothing mattered to me so much that I wanted at last to catch a moment when it did not matter quite so much; why, I don’t know. And so for those two months, every night as I came home, I thought I would shoot myself. I kept waiting for the moment. And now this little star gave me the idea, and I decided that it would certainly be that night. Why the little star gave me the idea, I don’t know.

And just as I was looking at the sky, this little girl suddenly grabbed me by the elbow. The street was already empty, there was hardly anyone about. In the distance a cab driver was asleep on his cab. The girl was about eight, in a headscarf and nothing but a thin dress, soaked through; but I particularly remember her wet, torn shoes, and I remember them now. They caught my eye especially. She suddenly began to tug at my elbow and call to me. She was not crying, but she was shouting out some words in bursts, words she could not get out properly because she was shaking all over with a fine shivering. She was in terror about something and kept crying desperately, “Mama! Mama!” I turned my face towards her, but I did not say a word and kept walking. But she ran beside me and tugged at me, and in her voice there was that sound which in very frightened children means despair. I know that sound. Though she could not finish her words, I understood that her mother was dying somewhere, or that something had happened to them, and that she had run out to call someone, to find something to help her mother. But I did not go with her; on the contrary, the idea suddenly came to me to drive her away. First I told her to go and find a policeman. But she suddenly clasped her little hands and, sobbing, gasping, kept running at my side and would not leave me. It was then that I stamped my foot at her and shouted. She only cried out, “Sir, sir!...” but then suddenly left me and ran headlong across the street: someone else had appeared there, a passer-by, and she evidently rushed from me to him.

I climbed up to my fifth floor. I rent a room from landlords; we have furnished rooms. My room is poor and small, with a semicircular attic window. I have an oilcloth sofa, a table with books on it, two chairs and an easy chair, very, very old, but a proper Voltaire chair. I sat down, lit a candle and began to think. Next door, behind the partition, the bedlam went on. It had been going on since the day before yesterday. A retired captain lived there, and he had guests, half a dozen shabby characters; they were drinking vodka and playing stoss with old cards. The night before there had been a fight, and I know that two of them dragged each other about by the hair for a long time. The landlady wanted to complain, but she is terribly afraid of the captain. The only other lodger in our rooms is a little, thin lady, an officer’s widow, from out of town, with three small children who have fallen ill since they came here. She and the children are afraid of the captain to the point of fainting, and they shake and cross themselves all night; the smallest child even had a sort of fit from fright. This captain, I know for certain, sometimes stops passers-by on the Nevsky Prospect and begs. No one will give him a post; and yet, strangely (and this is why I am telling it), in the whole month he has lived here the captain has never once irritated me. Of course I avoided getting to know him from the start, and he was bored with me himself from the first time; but however much they shouted behind their partition, and however many of them there were, it never mattered to me. I sit all night and really do not hear them; I forget them completely. I don’t sleep at night until dawn, and it has been like that for a year now. I sit up all night in the easy chair at the table and do nothing. I read books only in the daytime. I sit and don’t even think; some thoughts wander about, and I let them go where they like. The candle burns down completely in the night.

I sat down quietly at the table, took out the revolver and laid it in front of me. When I laid it there I remember asking myself, “Is it to be?”, and answering myself quite firmly, “It is.” That is, I would shoot myself. I knew that I would certainly shoot myself that night, but how much longer I would sit at the table before then, I did not know. And of course I would have shot myself, if it hadn’t been for that little girl.

## II

You see: although nothing mattered to me, I could still feel pain, for instance. If someone had struck me, I would have felt pain. It was exactly the same morally: if something very pitiful had happened, I would have felt pity, just as I did when things still mattered to me in life. And I had felt pity a little while before: I would certainly have helped a child. So why didn’t I help the little girl? Because of an idea that had come to me then: when she was tugging at me and calling me, a question suddenly arose in front of me, and I could not answer it. It was an idle question, but it made me angry. It made me angry because of this conclusion: if I had already decided to finish myself off that night, then everything in the world ought now, more than ever, to be a matter of complete indifference to me. So why did I suddenly feel that it was not indifferent to me, and that I pitied the girl? I remember that I pitied her very much; it was almost a strange pain, and quite unbelievable in my position. Really, I don’t know how to convey that fleeting feeling of mine better than that; but the feeling went on at home too, when I had sat down at the table, and I was very irritated, as I had not been for a long time.

One argument followed another. It seemed clear that if I was a man and not yet a zero, and until I turned into a zero, I was alive, and therefore could suffer, be angry and feel shame for my actions. Fine. But if I killed myself in, say, two hours, what would the girl be to me, and what would I care then about shame or anything else in the world? I would turn into a zero, an absolute zero. And could the knowledge that in a moment I would not exist at all, and therefore nothing would exist, really have no influence at all on my feeling of pity for the girl, or on my feeling of shame after the base thing I had done? Why, that was exactly why I had stamped and shouted in a savage voice at the unhappy child: “Not only do I feel no pity, but if I do something inhumanly base, I can do it now, because in two hours everything will go out.” Can you believe that that was why I shouted? I am almost convinced of it now.

It seemed clear that life and the world now depended, as it were, on me. One could even say that the world had now been made, as it were, for me alone: I shoot myself, and there will be no world, at least for me. Not to mention that perhaps there really will be nothing for anyone after me, and that the whole world, as soon as my consciousness goes out, will go out at once like a phantom, like something belonging only to my consciousness, and be abolished, because perhaps the whole of this world and all these people are only me myself.

I remember that as I sat there reasoning, I turned all these new questions, which crowded on one another, in quite another direction, and thought up something altogether new. For instance, a strange thought suddenly occurred to me: suppose I had once lived on the moon or on Mars, and done there the most shameful and dishonourable thing one can imagine, and been reviled and disgraced for it there as one can only feel and imagine sometimes in a dream, a nightmare; and suppose that, finding myself afterwards on earth, I kept the awareness of what I had done on the other planet, and knew besides that I would never, ever go back there; then, looking at the moon from the earth, would it matter to me or not? Would I feel shame for that deed or not? The questions were idle and pointless, since the revolver was already lying in front of me and I knew with my whole being that it would certainly happen; but they excited me, and I was furious. It was as if I could not die now without first resolving something. In a word, that little girl saved me, because with my questions I put off the shot.

Meanwhile, at the captain’s too, everything had grown quiet: they had finished their cards and were settling down to sleep, grumbling and lazily finishing their quarrels. And it was then that I suddenly fell asleep, something that had never happened to me before, at the table in the easy chair. I fell asleep without noticing it at all.

Dreams, as everyone knows, are extremely strange things: one thing appears with terrifying clarity, worked out in detail like a jeweller’s work, while you leap over another as if you did not notice it at all, over space and time, for instance. Dreams, it seems, are driven not by reason but by desire, not by the head but by the heart; and yet what cunning things my reason has sometimes done in dreams! And meanwhile quite incomprehensible things happen to it in dreams. My brother, for instance, died five years ago. I sometimes see him in my dreams: he takes part in my affairs, we are very caught up in them, and yet all the time I fully know and remember, throughout the dream, that my brother is dead and buried. How is it that I am not astonished that, though he is dead, he is still here beside me and busying himself with me? Why does my reason accept all this completely?

But enough. I come to my dream. Yes, I dreamed that dream then, my dream of the third of November! They tease me now that it was only a dream. But does it matter whether it was a dream or not, if that dream revealed the Truth to me? For once you have come to know the truth and seen it, you know that it is the truth and that there is no other and cannot be, whether you are asleep or awake. Well, let it be a dream, let it be; but this life that you all praise so highly I wanted to put out by suicide, and my dream, my dream, oh, it announced to me a new, great, renewed and powerful life! Listen.

## III

I said that I fell asleep without noticing, as if I were still reasoning about the same matters. Suddenly I dreamed that I took the revolver and, sitting there, aimed it straight at my heart; at my heart, not my head, though I had decided before that I would certainly shoot myself in the head, and in the right temple to be exact. With the barrel at my chest, I waited a second or two, and my candle, the table and the wall in front of me suddenly began to move and sway. I fired quickly.

In dreams you sometimes fall from a height, or are stabbed, or beaten, but you never feel pain, except perhaps if you really do hurt yourself somehow against the bed; then you feel the pain and almost always wake up from it. So it was in my dream: I felt no pain, but it seemed to me that with my shot everything in me was shaken and everything suddenly went out, and it became terribly black around me. I seemed to have gone blind and dumb, and I was lying on something hard, stretched out on my back, I could see nothing and could not make the slightest movement. People were walking about and shouting around me, the captain in his deep voice, the landlady shrieking; then suddenly another gap, and now I was being carried in a closed coffin. And I felt the coffin swaying, and reasoned about it, and suddenly for the first time I was struck by the idea that I was dead, completely dead; I knew it and did not doubt it, I could not see and could not move, and yet I felt and reasoned. But I soon came to terms with that and, as usual in a dream, accepted reality without argument.

And now they were burying me in the ground. Everyone went away; I was alone, completely alone. I did not move. Whenever, awake, I had imagined being buried in a grave, the only feeling I had ever really connected with the grave was damp and cold. And so now I felt very cold, especially in the tips of my toes, but I felt nothing else.

I lay there and, strangely, expected nothing, accepting without argument that a dead man has nothing to expect. But it was damp. I don’t know how much time passed, an hour, or a few days, or many days. But suddenly a drop of water that had seeped through the coffin lid fell on my closed left eye, then a minute later another, then a minute later a third, and so on and so on, each a minute apart. A deep indignation suddenly blazed up in my heart, and suddenly I felt a physical pain in it. “That’s my wound,” I thought. “That’s the shot; the bullet is there...” And the drop kept dripping, every minute, straight onto my closed eye. And suddenly I called out, not with my voice, for I could not move, but with my whole being, to the ruler of everything that was happening to me:

“Whoever you are, if you exist, and if there is anything more rational than what is happening now, let it be here too. But if you are taking revenge on me for my irrational suicide by the ugliness and absurdity of further existence, then know that no torment that may befall me can ever compare with the contempt I shall feel in silence, even through millions of years of martyrdom!...”

I called out and fell silent. A deep silence lasted almost a whole minute, and one more drop even fell; but I knew, I knew boundlessly and unshakably and believed, that everything would certainly change at once. And suddenly my grave opened. That is, I don’t know whether it was opened and dug up, but I was taken by some dark being unknown to me, and we found ourselves in space. Suddenly I could see: it was deep night, and never, never had there been such darkness! We were rushing through space, already far from the earth. I asked the one who carried me nothing; I waited, and I was proud. I assured myself that I was not afraid, and caught my breath with delight at the thought that I was not afraid. I don’t remember how long we flew, and I cannot imagine it: everything happened as it always does in dreams, when you leap over space and time and the laws of existence and reason, and stop only at the points the heart dreams of.

I remember that suddenly I saw a little star in the darkness. “Is that Sirius?” I asked, suddenly unable to stop myself, for I had not wanted to ask anything. “No, it is the very star you saw between the clouds on your way home,” answered the being who was carrying me away. I knew that it had something like a human face. Strangely, I did not love this being; I even felt a deep revulsion. I had expected complete non-existence, and with that I had shot myself in the heart. And here I was in the hands of a being, not a human being of course, but one that is, that exists. “So there is life beyond the grave!” I thought, with the strange lightness of a dream; but the essence of my heart remained with me in all its depth. “And if I must be again,” I thought, “and live again by someone’s inescapable will, then I will not be defeated and humiliated!” “You know that I am afraid of you, and you despise me for it,” I said suddenly to my companion, unable to stop myself from asking a humiliating question that contained a confession, and feeling my humiliation in my heart like the prick of a pin. He did not answer my question, but I suddenly felt that I was not despised, not laughed at, not even pitied, and that our journey had a purpose, unknown and mysterious, that concerned me alone.

Fear grew in my heart. Something was being communicated to me from my silent companion, mutely but painfully, and seemed to pierce me through. We were rushing through dark and unknown spaces. I had long since stopped seeing the constellations familiar to the eye. I knew that there are stars in the spaces of the sky whose light reaches the earth only after thousands and millions of years. Perhaps we were already flying through those spaces. I was waiting for something in a terrible anguish that tormented my heart. And suddenly a familiar and supremely calling feeling shook me: I suddenly saw our sun! I knew that it could not be our sun, the sun that gave birth to our earth, and that we were an infinite distance from our sun; but somehow I knew with my whole being that it was exactly the same sun as ours, its repetition and its double. A sweet, calling feeling rang out with rapture in my soul: the kindred power of the light, the same light that had given birth to me, echoed in my heart and brought it back to life, and I felt life, my former life, for the first time since my grave.

“But if this is the sun, if it is exactly the same sun as ours,” I cried, “then where is the earth?” And my companion pointed to a little star shining with an emerald light in the darkness. We were rushing straight towards it.

“Are such repetitions really possible in the universe? Is that really the law of nature?... And if that is the earth there, is it really the same earth as ours... exactly the same, unhappy, poor, but dear and eternally loved, and arousing the same agonising love for itself even in the most ungrateful of its children, as ours does?...” I cried, shaking with irrepressible, rapturous love for that kindred former earth that I had left. The image of the poor little girl I had wronged flashed before me.

“You will see everything,” my companion answered, and a kind of sadness could be heard in his words.

But we were quickly approaching the planet. It grew before my eyes; I could already make out the ocean, the outline of Europe; and suddenly a strange feeling of a great, holy jealousy blazed up in my heart: “How can there be such a repetition, and why? I love, I can love only the earth I have left, where the splashes of my blood remained when I, ungrateful, put out my life with a shot in my heart. But never, never did I stop loving that earth, and even that night, parting from it, I loved it perhaps more agonisingly than ever. Is there suffering on this new earth? On our earth we can truly love only with suffering, and only through suffering! We don’t know how to love otherwise, and we know no other love. I want suffering in order to love. I want, I thirst at this very moment, to kiss, with tears streaming down my face, only that one earth that I have left, and I don’t want, I won’t accept, life on any other!...”

But my companion had already left me. Suddenly, without my noticing it at all, I was standing on this other earth in the bright light of a sunny day as lovely as paradise. I was standing, I think, on one of those islands that on our earth make up the Greek archipelago, or somewhere on the coast of the mainland next to it. Oh, everything was exactly as it is with us, but everything seemed to shine with some festival and a great, holy triumph achieved at last. The gentle emerald sea lapped quietly at the shores and kissed them with a love that was open, visible, almost conscious. Tall, beautiful trees stood in all the splendour of their blossom, and their countless little leaves, I am sure of it, greeted me with their quiet, gentle rustling and seemed to speak words of love. The grass blazed with bright, fragrant flowers. Birds flew about in flocks and, not afraid of me, perched on my shoulders and hands and joyfully beat me with their dear, fluttering little wings. And at last I saw and came to know the people of this happy earth. They came to me of their own accord, they surrounded me, they kissed me. Children of the sun, children of their sun, oh, how beautiful they were! Never had I seen such beauty in a human being on our earth. Only in our children, perhaps, in their very first years, could one find a distant, faint gleam of that beauty. The eyes of these happy people shone with a clear light. Their faces shone with reason and with a kind of consciousness filled to the point of peace; but these faces were cheerful; in the words and voices of these people there rang a child’s joy. Oh, at once, at the first glance at their faces, I understood everything, everything! This was the earth not defiled by the Fall; on it lived people who had not sinned; they lived in the same paradise in which, according to the traditions of all mankind, our first parents lived before they sinned, with the only difference that here the whole earth was everywhere one and the same paradise. These people, laughing joyfully, crowded round me and caressed me; they led me home with them, and each of them wanted to comfort me. Oh, they asked me nothing, but it seemed to me that they already knew everything, and they wanted to drive the suffering from my face as quickly as they could.
