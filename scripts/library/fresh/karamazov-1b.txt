## Chapter III · The Second Marriage and the Second Children

Fyodor Pavlovich, having got the four-year-old Mitya off his hands, very soon afterwards married a second time. This second marriage lasted about eight years. He took this second wife of his, also a very young person, Sofya Ivanovna, from another province, where he had gone on some small contracting business, in company with some little Jew. Though Fyodor Pavlovich caroused and drank and made scenes, he never stopped investing his capital, and always arranged his little affairs successfully, though of course almost always somewhat basely. Sofya Ivanovna was one of the “little orphans”, without family since childhood, the daughter of some obscure deacon, brought up in the rich house of her benefactress, educator and tormentor, a distinguished old general’s widow, the widow of General Vorokhov. I do not know the details, but I have only heard that the ward, meek, gentle and uncomplaining, was once taken down from a noose which she had hung on a nail in the storeroom, so hard was it for her to bear the caprices and eternal reproaches of this old woman, who was apparently not wicked but had become simply a most intolerable petty tyrant from idleness. Fyodor Pavlovich offered his hand; inquiries were made about him and he was driven away; and then once again, as in his first marriage, he proposed an elopement to the little orphan. It is very, very possible that she would not have gone with him for anything if she had learned more details about him in time. But it was in another province; and what could a sixteen-year-old girl understand, except that it was better to go into the river than to stay with her benefactress? So the poor thing exchanged a benefactress for a benefactor. This time Fyodor Pavlovich did not get a penny, because the general’s widow was angry, gave nothing, and besides cursed them both; but he had not counted on getting anything this time; he had been captivated only by the remarkable beauty of the innocent girl and, above all, by her innocent look, which struck him, a sensualist and until then a depraved lover only of coarse female beauty. “Those innocent little eyes slashed my soul like a razor then,” he used to say afterwards, giggling nastily in his own way. However, in a depraved man even this may have been only a sensual attraction. Having received no reward, Fyodor Pavlovich did not stand on ceremony with his wife, and taking advantage of her being, so to speak, “guilty” before him, and of his having almost “taken her down from the noose”, taking advantage moreover of her phenomenal meekness and lack of response, he trampled underfoot even the most ordinary decencies of marriage. Bad women came to the house, right there with the wife present, and orgies were held. As a characteristic trait I will report that the servant Grigory, a gloomy, stupid and stubborn reasoner, who had hated the former mistress, Adelaida Ivanovna, this time took the side of the new mistress, defended her and quarrelled with Fyodor Pavlovich on her account in a way almost impermissible for a servant, and once even broke up an orgy and drove out by force all the disorderly women who had come. Later the unhappy young woman, frightened from her very childhood, developed something like a nervous women’s illness, found most often among common village women, who for this illness are called klikushi, shriekers. From this illness, with terrible hysterical fits, the sick woman at times even lost her reason. She bore Fyodor Pavlovich, however, two sons, Ivan and Alexei, the first in the first year of the marriage and the second three years later. When she died, the boy Alexei was in his fourth year, and though it is strange, I know that he remembered his mother all his life afterwards, as if through a dream, of course. After her death almost exactly the same thing happened to both boys as to the first, Mitya: they were completely forgotten and abandoned by their father and ended up with the same Grigory, in his cottage too. It was in the cottage that the old tyrant, the general’s widow, the benefactress and educator of their mother, found them. She was still alive, and all that time, all eight years, she had not been able to forget the offence done to her. All those eight years she had had the most exact information, secretly, about the life of her “Sofya”, and hearing how ill she was and what outrages surrounded her, she had two or three times said aloud to her hangers-on: “It serves her right; God has sent it to her for her ingratitude.”

Exactly three months after Sofya Ivanovna’s death the general’s widow suddenly appeared in our town in person and went straight to Fyodor Pavlovich’s lodging, and she stayed in the little town only about half an hour, but she did a great deal. It was evening then. Fyodor Pavlovich, whom she had not seen for all eight years, came out to her a little drunk. They relate that instantly, without any explanations, as soon as she saw him, she gave him two good resounding slaps in the face and three times tugged him by his forelock from top to bottom; then, without adding a word, she went straight to the cottage to the two boys. Noticing at the first glance that they were unwashed and in dirty linen, she at once gave Grigory himself another slap and announced to him that she was taking both children away with her; then she led them out just as they were, wrapped them in a rug, put them in her carriage and took them off to her own town. Grigory bore this slap like a devoted slave, did not say a rude word, and when he saw the old lady to her carriage, he bowed to her from the waist and said impressively that “God will repay you for the orphans.” “You’re a blockhead all the same!” the general’s widow shouted to him as she drove off. Fyodor Pavlovich, having thought the whole thing over, found that it was a good thing, and afterwards did not refuse his formal consent on a single point regarding the children’s upbringing by the general’s widow. As for the slaps he had received, he went round the whole town himself telling of them.

It so happened that the general’s widow too died soon afterwards, having, however, stipulated in her will a thousand roubles for each of the two little ones “for their education, and that all this money be spent on them without fail, but so that it lasts right up to their coming of age, because such a handout is more than enough for such children, and if anyone wishes otherwise, let him loosen his own purse strings,” and so on and so on. I have not read the will myself, but I have heard that there was something strange in it of this kind, and far too peculiarly expressed. The chief heir of the old woman, however, turned out to be an honest man, the provincial marshal of nobility of that province, Yefim Petrovich Polenov. Having exchanged letters with Fyodor Pavlovich and guessed in a flash that one would never extract money from him for the upbringing of his own children (though he never refused outright, but only always dragged things out in such cases, sometimes even pouring out sentiments), he took a personal interest in the orphans and especially grew fond of the younger of them, Alexei, so that for a long time the boy even grew up in his family. I ask the reader to note this from the very beginning. And if the young men owed their upbringing and education for their whole lives to anyone, it was precisely to this Yefim Petrovich, a most noble and humane man, of a kind rarely met. He kept the little ones’ thousand each, left by the general’s widow, untouched, so that by the time they came of age it had grown with interest to two thousand each; and he brought them up at his own expense, and of course spent much more than a thousand on each of them. I will not enter now either into a detailed account of their childhood and youth, but will indicate only the most important circumstances. About the elder, Ivan, I will report only that he grew up a somewhat gloomy boy, shut up in himself, far from timid, but as if he had grasped already at ten years old that they were growing up after all in someone else’s family and on someone else’s charity, and that their father was a man of such a kind that it was shameful even to speak of him, and so on and so on. This boy very soon, almost in infancy (so it was said at least), began to show some extraordinary and brilliant aptitude for learning. I do not know exactly, but it somehow came about that he parted from Yefim Petrovich’s family when he was hardly thirteen, going to one of the Moscow gymnasiums and boarding with a certain experienced and, at that time, famous teacher, a childhood friend of Yefim Petrovich. Ivan himself used to say afterwards that it all came, so to speak, from Yefim Petrovich’s “ardour for good deeds”, carried away by the idea that a boy of genius ought also to be brought up by an educator of genius. However, neither Yefim Petrovich nor the educator of genius was alive any longer when the young man, having finished the gymnasium, entered the university. Since Yefim Petrovich had made poor arrangements, and the receipt of the children’s own money bequeathed by the tyrannical general’s widow, which had grown from one thousand to two with interest, was delayed by various formalities and delays quite unavoidable among us, the young man had a very hard time of it in his first two years at the university, since he was forced all that time to feed and keep himself and to study at the same time. It must be noted that he did not even want to make an attempt then to write to his father: perhaps out of pride, out of contempt for him, or perhaps as a result of cold, sound reasoning, which told him that he would not get any even slightly serious support from his papa. However that may be, the young man did not lose his head in the least, and managed to find work, first giving lessons at twenty kopecks, and then running round the newspaper offices and supplying little articles of ten lines about street incidents, signed “Eyewitness”. These little articles, they say, were always composed so curiously and piquantly that they quickly caught on, and in this alone the young man showed all his practical and intellectual superiority over that numerous, eternally needy and unhappy part of our student youth of both sexes, who in the capitals, as a rule, knock from morning to night at the doors of various newspapers and journals, unable to think of anything better than the eternal repetition of the same request for translations from French or copying. Having become acquainted with the editorial offices, Ivan Fyodorovich never afterwards broke his connections with them, and in his last years at the university he began to publish very talented reviews of books on various specialised subjects, so that he even became known in literary circles. However, only quite recently had he happened suddenly to attract special attention in a much wider circle of readers, so that very many people noticed and remembered him all at once. It was a rather curious incident. Having already left the university and preparing to go abroad on his two thousand, Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly published in one of the big newspapers a strange article which attracted the attention even of non-specialists, and above all on a subject apparently quite unfamiliar to him, since he had graduated in natural science. The article was written on the question of ecclesiastical courts, which was being raised everywhere at that time. Examining certain opinions already given on this question, he also stated his own personal view. The main thing was in the tone and in the remarkable unexpectedness of the conclusion. And yet many of the churchmen decidedly took the author for one of their own. And suddenly, alongside them, not only the secularists but even the atheists themselves began to applaud from their side. In the end some shrewd people decided that the whole article was only an insolent farce and a mockery. I mention this incident especially because the article in due course made its way also into our famous monastery near the town, where people were interested in the question of ecclesiastical courts that had arisen; it made its way there and produced complete bewilderment. Learning the author’s name, they were interested too that he was a native of our town and the son of “that very Fyodor Pavlovich”. And then suddenly, at that very time, the author himself appeared among us.

Why Ivan Fyodorovich came to us then, I remember asking myself even then, with a kind of almost uneasiness. This so fateful arrival, which served as the beginning of so many consequences, long remained for me, almost always, an unclear affair. Judging in general, it was strange that a young man so learned, so proud and cautious in appearance, should suddenly appear in such a disgraceful house, to such a father, who had ignored him all his life, did not know him and did not remember him, and though he would certainly not have given him money for anything and in any case if his son had asked him, had nevertheless been afraid all his life that his sons, Ivan and Alexei, would also come some day and ask for money. And here the young man settles in the house of such a father, lives with him a month and another, and the two get along as well as could be. This last especially surprised not only me but many others too. Pyotr Alexandrovich Miusov, of whom I have already spoken above, a distant relation of Fyodor Pavlovich through his first wife, happened to be with us again at that time, on his estate near the town, having come from Paris, where he had now settled for good. I remember that it was precisely he who wondered most of all, having become acquainted with the young man, who interested him extremely and with whom he sometimes crossed swords in learning, not without inner pain. “He is proud,” he said to us about him then; “he will always earn himself a kopeck; even now he has money for going abroad; what does he want here? It is clear to everyone that he has not come to his father for money, because in any case his father won’t give it. He doesn’t like drinking wine and debauchery, and yet the old man can’t do without him, they have got on so well!” That was true; the young man even had a visible influence on the old man; the latter almost began at times to obey him, as it were, though he was extremely and even sometimes spitefully wilful; he even began at times to behave more decently...

Only later was it explained that Ivan Fyodorovich had come partly at the request and on the business of his elder brother, Dmitry Fyodorovich, whom he also knew and saw for the first time in his life at almost this same time, on this same visit, but with whom, however, he had entered into correspondence even before coming from Moscow, over an important matter which concerned Dmitry Fyodorovich more. What this matter was the reader will learn fully in detail in due course. Nevertheless, even when I already knew about this special circumstance, Ivan Fyodorovich still seemed enigmatic to me, and his arrival among us still inexplicable.

I will add that Ivan Fyodorovich had at that time the air of a mediator and peacemaker between his father and his elder brother, Dmitry Fyodorovich, who had then started a big quarrel and even a formal lawsuit against his father.

This little family, I repeat, came together then for the first time in their lives, and some of its members saw each other for the first time in their lives. Only the youngest son, Alexei Fyodorovich, had already been living among us for a year before this, and so had arrived among us before all his brothers. It is about this very Alexei that it is hardest of all for me to speak in my present introductory account, before bringing him on the stage in the novel. But I shall have to write a preface about him too, at least to explain beforehand one very strange point, namely: I am forced to present my future hero to the readers, from the first scene of his novel, in the cassock of a novice. Yes, he had already been living for a year in our monastery then, and seemed to be preparing to shut himself up in it for the rest of his life.

## Chapter IV · The Third Son, Alyosha

He was only twenty then (his brother Ivan was in his twenty-fourth year, and their elder brother Dmitry in his twenty-eighth). First of all I declare that this young man, Alyosha, was not a fanatic at all, and, in my opinion at least, not even a mystic at all. I will say beforehand my full opinion: he was simply an early lover of humanity, and if he took up the monastic road, it was only because at that time it alone struck him and presented to him, so to speak, the ideal way out for his soul, which was struggling out of the darkness of worldly malice towards the light of love. And this road struck him only because on it he had met then what was, in his opinion, an extraordinary being: our famous monastery elder Zosima, to whom he became attached with all the ardent first love of his insatiable heart. However, I do not dispute that he was very strange even then, having begun so even from the cradle. By the way, I have already mentioned about him that, though he was left without his mother when only in his fourth year, he remembered her all his life afterwards, her face, her caresses, “just as if she were standing before me alive”. Such memories can be retained (everyone knows this) even from an earlier age, even from the age of two, but only standing out all one’s life like points of light out of the darkness, like a corner torn from a huge picture which has all faded and vanished except this one little corner. So it was exactly with him: he remembered one evening, a summer evening, quiet, an open window, the slanting rays of the setting sun (the slanting rays were what he remembered most of all), in the corner of the room an icon, before it a lighted lamp, and before the icon, on her knees, his mother, sobbing as if in hysterics, with shrieks and cries, seizing him in both arms, embracing him tightly till it hurt and praying for him to the Mother of God, holding him out from her embrace in both hands towards the icon as if under the protection of the Mother of God... and suddenly the nurse runs in and snatches him from her in fright. There is a picture! Alyosha remembered his mother’s face too at that moment: he used to say that it was frenzied but beautiful, as far as he could remember. But he rarely liked to confide this memory to anyone. In childhood and youth he was not very expansive and even not very talkative, but not from mistrust, not from timidity or sullen unsociability, quite the contrary, but from something else, from a kind of inner preoccupation, personal to himself, not concerning others, but so important to him that because of it he as if forgot others. But he loved people: he seemed all his life to live with complete faith in people, and yet no one ever considered him either a simpleton or a naive person. There was something in him that said and suggested (and did so all his life afterwards) that he did not want to be a judge of people, that he would not take condemnation upon himself and would not condemn for anything. It even seemed that he allowed everything, without condemning in the least, though often grieving very bitterly. More than that, in this sense he had gone so far that no one could either surprise or frighten him, and this even in his very earliest youth. Coming to his father in his twentieth year, positively into a den of filthy debauchery, he, chaste and pure, only withdrew silently when it was unbearable to look, but without the slightest sign of contempt or condemnation of anyone at all. And his father, once a hanger-on and therefore a man sensitive and quick to take offence, who at first received him mistrustfully and sullenly (“he is very silent, they say, and reasons a lot to himself”), soon ended, however, by beginning to hug and kiss him terribly often, no more than two weeks or so later, with drunken tears, it is true, in tipsy sentimentality, but evidently having come to love him sincerely and deeply, and as such a man as he had of course never managed to love anyone...

And indeed everyone loved this young man wherever he appeared, and this from his very earliest childhood. Finding himself in the house of his benefactor and educator, Yefim Petrovich Polenov, he so attached everyone in that family to himself that they decidedly considered him there almost as their own child. And yet he had entered that house at such an infant age that one could never expect in a child calculating cunning, wiliness, or the art of ingratiating oneself and pleasing, the knack of making oneself loved. So the gift of arousing special love towards himself he contained, so to speak, in his very nature, artlessly and directly. It was the same with him at school, and yet, it would seem, he was precisely one of those children who arouse the mistrust of their schoolfellows, sometimes mockery, and perhaps even hatred. He would, for instance, fall into thought and as if withdraw. From his very childhood he loved to go off into a corner and read books, and yet his schoolfellows came to love him so much that he could decidedly be called the general favourite all the time he was at school. He was rarely playful, even rarely merry, but everyone, looking at him, saw at once that this was not at all from any sullenness in him; that, on the contrary, he was even-tempered and serene. Among those of his own age he never wanted to put himself forward. Perhaps for that very reason he was never afraid of anyone, and yet the boys at once understood that he was not at all proud of his fearlessness, but looked as if he did not even understand that he was brave and fearless. He never remembered an offence. It happened that an hour after an offence he would answer the offender or begin talking with him himself with such a trusting and serene air, as if nothing had passed between them at all. And it was not that he had the air of having forgotten the offence by chance or forgiven it on purpose; he simply did not consider it an offence, and this decidedly captivated and conquered the children. There was only one trait in him which in all the classes of the gymnasium, from the lowest to the highest, aroused in his schoolfellows a constant wish to tease him, not out of malicious mockery, but because it amused them. This trait in him was a wild, frenzied modesty and chastity. He could not hear certain words and certain conversations about women. These “certain” words and conversations, unfortunately, cannot be rooted out in schools. Boys pure in soul and heart, still almost children, very often like to talk among themselves in the classroom, and even aloud, about such things, pictures and images as even soldiers will not always talk about; what is more, soldiers do not know and understand much of what is already familiar in this line to such young children of our educated and higher society. There is perhaps no moral depravity here yet; there is no real, depraved, inner cynicism either; but there is an outward one, and this is often considered among them something even delicate, subtle, dashing and worthy of imitation. Seeing that “Alyoshka Karamazov” quickly stopped his ears with his fingers when they began talking “about that”, they would sometimes deliberately crowd round him and, pulling his hands away from his ears by force, shout filth into both his ears, while he struggled, slid down to the floor, lay down, covered himself up, and all this without saying a word to them, without cursing, bearing the offence in silence. In the end, however, they left him alone and no longer teased him as a “little girl”; more than that, they looked at him in this respect with pity. By the way, in class he always stood among the best in his studies, but was never marked first.

When Yefim Petrovich died, Alyosha stayed two more years at the provincial gymnasium. Yefim Petrovich’s inconsolable widow, almost immediately after his death, went off to Italy for a long stay with her whole family, which consisted entirely of persons of the female sex, and Alyosha ended up in the house of two ladies whom he had never seen before, some distant relations of Yefim Petrovich, but on what terms he did not know himself. A characteristic trait of his too, and very much so, was that he never cared at whose expense he lived. In this he was the complete opposite of his elder brother, Ivan Fyodorovich, who had struggled in poverty his first two years at the university, feeding himself by his own labour, and had felt bitterly from his very childhood that he was living on someone else’s bread, with a benefactor. But this strange trait in Alexei’s character, it seems, could not be condemned very severely, because anyone who got to know him even a little became sure at once, when the question arose, that Alexei was certainly one of those young men, something like holy fools, who, if a whole fortune were suddenly to fall into their hands, would not hesitate to give it away at the first request, either to a good cause or perhaps even simply to a clever rogue, if he asked him for it. And in general he seemed not to know the value of money at all; not in the literal sense, of course. When he was given pocket money, which he himself never asked for, he either did not know for whole weeks what to do with it, or was terribly careless with it, and it vanished from him in an instant. Pyotr Alexandrovich Miusov, a man very particular about money and bourgeois honesty, once, later on, having observed Alexei, pronounced the following aphorism about him: “Here, perhaps, is the only man in the world whom you could suddenly leave alone and without money in the square of an unknown city of a million inhabitants, and he would not perish for anything or die of hunger and cold, because he would be fed in an instant, settled in an instant, and if he were not settled, he would settle himself in an instant, and it would cost him no effort and no humiliation, and it would be no burden to the one who settled him; on the contrary, perhaps they would consider it a pleasure.”

He did not finish the course at his gymnasium; he still had a whole year to go when he suddenly announced to his ladies that he was going to his father on a certain matter that had come into his head. They were very sorry for him and did not want to let him go. The journey cost very little, and the ladies did not allow him to pawn his watch, a present from his benefactor’s family before they went abroad, but supplied him lavishly with means, even with new clothes and linen. He gave half the money back to them, however, declaring that he absolutely wanted to travel third class. Arriving in our little town, he made no direct answer to his parent’s first questions, “Why exactly have you come, without finishing the course?”, but was, they say, unusually thoughtful. It soon came out that he was looking for his mother’s grave. He even admitted then himself that this was the only reason he had come. But this hardly exhausted the whole reason for his coming. Most probably he did not know himself then, and could not for anything have explained, what exactly had as if suddenly risen from his soul and irresistibly drawn him onto some new, unknown, but already inevitable road. Fyodor Pavlovich could not show him where he had buried his second wife, because he had never been to her grave since the coffin was covered with earth, and with the passing of years he had quite forgotten where she had been buried...

A word about Fyodor Pavlovich. For a long time before this he had not lived in our town. Three or four years after his second wife’s death he went off to the south of Russia and ended up in Odessa, where he lived for several years on end. He first became acquainted, by his own account, “with a lot of Jews, Jewlings, Jewkins and little Jewlets”, and ended up by being received not only among the Jews but “even among the Hebrews”. One must suppose that it was in this period of his life that he developed a special skill in scraping together and squeezing out money. He came back to our little town for good only about three years before Alyosha’s arrival. His former acquaintances found him terribly aged, though he was not at all such an old man yet. He conducted himself not so much more nobly as somehow more insolently. There appeared, for example, in the former buffoon an insolent need to make buffoons of others. He liked to behave outrageously with the female sex not only as before but even, as it were, more repulsively. Soon he became the founder of many new taverns around the district. It was evident that he had perhaps a hundred thousand, or only a little less. Many of the townspeople and of the inhabitants of the district at once got into debt to him, against the safest securities, of course. In the very last period he had somehow grown flabby, had begun somehow to lose his evenness and self-control, had fallen even into a kind of frivolity, would begin one thing and end with another, somehow sprawled about, and got drunk more and more often; and if it had not been for the same footman Grigory, who had also aged considerably by that time and looked after him sometimes almost like a tutor, then perhaps Fyodor Pavlovich would not have lived without special trouble. Alyosha’s arrival seemed to affect him even on the moral side, as if something had awakened in this prematurely old man of what had long since been stifled in his soul. “Do you know,” he often began to say to Alyosha, looking at him closely, “that you are like her, the shrieker?” That was what he called his late wife, Alyosha’s mother. It was the footman Grigory who at last showed Alyosha the grave of the “shrieker”. He took him to our town cemetery and there, in a remote corner, showed him an inexpensive but neat cast-iron slab, on which there was even an inscription with the name, rank, age and year of death of the deceased, and at the bottom there was even engraved a kind of quatrain, from the old cemetery verses commonly used on the graves of people of the middling sort. To his surprise, this slab turned out to be Grigory’s doing. It was he himself who had put it up over the grave of the poor “shrieker”, at his own expense, after Fyodor Pavlovich, whom he had pestered many times with reminders about this grave, had at last gone off to Odessa, waving his hand not only at graves but at all his memories. Alyosha showed no particular emotion at his mother’s grave; he only listened to Grigory’s solemn and sensible account of the putting up of the slab, stood with his head bowed, and went away without uttering a word. Since then, perhaps for the whole year, he had not even been to the cemetery. But this little episode had its effect on Fyodor Pavlovich too, and a very original one. He suddenly took a thousand roubles and took it to our monastery for prayers for the soul of his wife, but not the second, not Alyosha’s mother, not the “shrieker”, but the first, Adelaida Ivanovna, who used to beat him. In the evening of that day he got drunk and abused the monks to Alyosha. He himself was far from being a religious man; a man who had perhaps never put a five-kopeck candle before an icon. Strange impulses of sudden feelings and sudden thoughts come over such subjects.

I have already said that he had grown very flabby. His physiognomy by that time presented something that bore sharp witness to the character and essence of the whole life he had lived. Besides the long, fleshy little bags under his small eyes, always insolent, suspicious and mocking, besides a multitude of deep little wrinkles on his small but fat little face, there hung under his sharp chin a big Adam’s apple, fleshy and oblong like a purse, which gave him a kind of repulsively sensual look. Add to this a carnivorous, long mouth, with plump lips, from under which small stumps of black, almost decayed teeth could be seen. He spluttered every time he began to speak. He himself, however, liked to joke about his face, though it seems he remained pleased with it. He especially pointed to his nose, not very big, but very thin, with a strongly pronounced hump: “A real Roman one,” he used to say; “together with the Adam’s apple, the real physiognomy of an ancient Roman patrician of the time of the decline.” He seemed proud of this.

And fairly soon after finding his mother’s grave, Alyosha suddenly announced to him that he wanted to enter the monastery and that the monks were ready to admit him as a novice. He explained that this was his extraordinary wish and that he was asking his solemn permission as his father. The old man already knew that the elder Zosima, who was living out his days in the monastery hermitage, had made a special impression on his “quiet boy”.

“That elder, of course, is the most honest monk they have,” he said, having listened to Alyosha in silence and thoughtfully, though hardly at all surprised by his request. “Hm, so that’s where you want to go, my quiet boy!” He was half drunk, and suddenly smiled his long, half-drunken smile, not without cunning and drunken slyness. “Hm, and you know I had a presentiment that you would end up with something just like this, can you imagine it? That is just where you were heading. Well, why not; you have your own two little thousands, there’s a dowry for you, and I will never leave you, my angel, and I’ll pay in for you now whatever is due, if they ask. Well, and if they don’t ask, why should we push ourselves on them, eh? After all, you spend money like a canary, two little grains a week... Hm. You know, at one monastery there is a little settlement outside the town, and everyone there knows that only ‘monastery wives’ live in it, that is what they call them there, thirty wives or so, I think... I’ve been there, and you know, it is interesting, in its own way of course, in the sense of variety. The only bad thing is the dreadful Russianism; there are no French girls at all yet, though there might be, the means are excellent. They’ll get wind of it and come. Well, but here there is nothing, here there are no monastery wives, but about two hundred monks. Honest. Fasters. I confess... Hm. So you want to go to the monks? And you know, I am sorry for you, Alyosha, truly, would you believe it, I have come to love you... However, here is a convenient opportunity: you will pray for us sinners; we have sinned far too much, sitting here. I have kept thinking: who will ever pray for me? Is there such a person in the world? My dear boy, I am terribly stupid on that score, perhaps you don’t believe it? Terribly. You see: stupid as I am, I keep thinking about it, keep thinking, now and then, of course, not all the time. It is impossible, I think, that the devils should forget to drag me off to them with hooks when I die. Well, so I think: hooks? And where do they get them? What are they made of? Iron? Where are they forged? Do they have some kind of factory down there? Why, the monks in the monastery there probably suppose that in hell, for example, there is a ceiling. And I am ready to believe in hell, only without a ceiling; it comes out more delicate, as it were, more enlightened, more Lutheran, that is. And in essence isn’t it all the same: with a ceiling or without a ceiling? Why, that is where the damned question lies! Well, and if there is no ceiling, then there are no hooks either. And if there are no hooks, then everything goes by the board, which again is improbable: who then will drag me off with hooks, because if they don’t drag me off, then what will happen, where is there any justice in the world? Il faudrait les inventer, one would have to invent them, those hooks, for me on purpose, for me alone, because if you knew, Alyosha, what a shameless wretch I am!..”

“Yes, there are no hooks there,” Alyosha said quietly and seriously, looking closely at his father.

“Just so, just so, only the shadows of hooks. I know, I know. It is like a certain Frenchman’s description of hell: ‘J’ai vu l’ombre d’un cocher, qui avec l’ombre d’une brosse frottait l’ombre d’un carrosse’: ‘I saw the shadow of a coachman, who with the shadow of a brush was rubbing the shadow of a carriage.’ How do you know, my dear, that there are no hooks? Stay with the monks a while, and you will sing a different tune. But then, go, get to the truth there, and come and tell me: it will be easier to go to the other world after all, if you know for certain what is there. And it will be more proper for you at the monks’ than with me, with a drunken old man and with girls... though nothing will touch you, as nothing touches an angel. Well, perhaps nothing will touch you there either; that is why I am allowing you, because I am hoping for that last. The devil hasn’t eaten your wits. You will burn out and go out, get cured and come back. And I will wait for you: why, I feel that you are the only man on earth who has not condemned me, my dear boy, I feel it, I can’t help feeling it!..”

And he even began to whimper. He was sentimental. He was wicked and sentimental.
