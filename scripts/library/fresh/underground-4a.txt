## VI

...Somewhere behind the partition, as if under some heavy pressure, as if someone were strangling it, the clock began to wheeze. After an unnaturally long wheezing came a thin, nasty and somehow unexpectedly rapid chime, as if someone had suddenly jumped forward. It struck two. I came to myself, though I had not been asleep, only lying half-conscious.

In the narrow, cramped, low room, crowded with an enormous wardrobe and littered with cardboard boxes, rags and all sorts of old clothes, it was almost completely dark. The candle end burning on the table at the end of the room was going out altogether, now and then flaring up a little. In a few minutes there would be total darkness.

It did not take me long to come to myself; everything came back to me at once, without effort, as if it had been lying in wait for me, ready to pounce again. And even in my stupor there had remained in my memory a kind of point that would not be forgotten, around which my drowsy dreams moved heavily. But it was strange: everything that had happened to me that day seemed to me now, on waking, to have happened long, long ago, as if I had long, long since outlived it all.

My head was heavy with fumes. Something seemed to be hovering over me, brushing me, stirring me up and troubling me. Misery and bile were boiling up again and seeking an outlet. Suddenly, beside me, I saw two open eyes examining me curiously and persistently. The look was coldly indifferent, sullen, as if completely alien; it was oppressive.

A sullen thought was born in my brain and passed through my whole body like a nasty sensation, the kind you have when you go down into a cellar, damp and musty. It was somehow unnatural that only now should these two eyes have decided to begin examining me. I remembered too that for two hours I had not said a single word to this creature, and had not thought it at all necessary; earlier, for some reason, I had even liked that. But now I suddenly saw vividly the absurd idea of debauchery, disgusting as a spider: without love, crudely and shamelessly, it begins straight away with what true love is crowned by. We looked at each other like this for a long time, but she did not lower her eyes before mine and did not change her look, so that at last, for some reason, I began to feel uneasy.

“What’s your name?” I asked abruptly, to get it over with.

“Liza,” she answered almost in a whisper, but somehow quite unfriendly, and looked away.

I was silent.

“The weather today... snow... it’s foul!” I said, almost to myself, putting my hand behind my head wearily and looking at the ceiling.

She did not answer. It was all hideous.

“Are you from here?” I asked a minute later, almost angrily, turning my head a little towards her.

“No.”

“Where from?”

“From Riga,” she said reluctantly.

“German?”

“Russian.”

“Been here long?”

“Where?”

“In this house.”

“Two weeks.” She was speaking more and more abruptly. The candle had gone out completely; I could no longer make out her face.

“Have you a father and mother?”

“Yes... no... I have.”

“Where are they?”

“There... in Riga.”

“What are they?”

“Just...”

“Just what? Who are they, what class?”

“Tradespeople.”

“Did you always live with them?”

“Yes.”

“How old are you?”

“Twenty.”

“Why did you leave them?”

“Just...”

That “just” meant: leave me alone, I’m sick of it. We fell silent.

God knows why I did not go away. I myself was feeling sicker and more miserable. Images of the whole past day began to pass through my memory of their own accord, against my will, in disorder. I suddenly remembered a scene I had seen in the street that morning, as I was anxiously trotting to work.

“Today they were carrying out a coffin, and they nearly dropped it,” I suddenly said aloud, not at all wanting to start a conversation, just like that, almost by accident.

“A coffin?”

“Yes, in the Haymarket; they were carrying it up out of a cellar.”

“Out of a cellar?”

“Not a cellar, a basement... you know... down there... from a bad house... There was such filth all around... Eggshells, rubbish... it smelt... it was vile.”

Silence.

“It’s a foul day for a funeral!” I began again, just so as not to be silent.

“Why foul?”

“Snow, wet...” (I yawned.)

“It makes no difference,” she said suddenly, after a silence.

“No, it’s foul...” (I yawned again.) “The gravediggers must have been swearing because the snow was getting them wet. And there must have been water in the grave.”

“Why water in the grave?” she asked with a kind of curiosity, but speaking even more rudely and abruptly than before. Something suddenly began to egg me on.

“Why, water, at the bottom, six inches deep. You can’t dig a single dry grave at the Volkovo cemetery.”

“Why not?”

“Why not? It’s such a watery place. It’s all marsh here. So they lay them in the water. I’ve seen it myself... many times...”

(I had not seen it even once, and had never been to the Volkovo cemetery; I had only heard people talk about it.)

“Don’t you care at all about dying?”

“Why should I die?” she answered, as if defending herself.

“You will die some day, and you will die just like that dead woman this morning. She was... a girl too... She died of consumption.”

“A tart would have died in hospital...” (She knows about that already, I thought, and she said “tart”, not “girl”.)

“She owed money to her madam,” I retorted, more and more egged on by the argument, “and she went on working for her almost to the very end, consumptive as she was. The cabmen standing around were talking to some soldiers and telling them about it. Probably her former acquaintances. They were laughing. They were even going to drink to her memory in the tavern.” (Here too I lied a great deal.)

Silence, deep silence. She did not even stir.

“And is it better to die in hospital, then?”

“Isn’t it all the same?.. And why should I die?” she added irritably.

“If not now, then later?”

“Well, later then...”

“Oh, no you don’t! Now you are young, pretty, fresh: that’s what you are valued for. But after a year of this life you won’t be the same; you’ll fade.”

“In a year?”

“In any case, in a year you’ll be worth less,” I went on gloatingly. “You’ll move from here to somewhere lower, another house. A year later, to a third, lower and lower, and in seven years or so you’ll end up in a basement in the Haymarket. And that would still be good. But the trouble will be if, on top of that, you get some illness, a weak chest, say... or catch a cold, or something. In a life like this an illness is hard to shake off. Once it takes hold, it may never let go. And so you’ll die.”

“Well, then I’ll die,” she answered, quite spitefully now, and moved quickly.

“But it’s a pity.”

“For whom?”

“A pity about life.”

Silence.

“Were you engaged to anyone? Eh?”

“What’s it to you?”

“I’m not cross-examining you. What do I care? Why are you angry? You must have had your own troubles. What’s it to me? It’s just that I’m sorry.”

“For whom?”

“Sorry for you.”

“No need...” she whispered barely audibly, and stirred again.

That at once made me angry. What! I had been so gentle with her, and she...

“What do you think, then? Are you on a good road, eh?”

“I don’t think anything.”

“That’s just what’s bad, that you don’t think. Wake up while there’s still time. And there is time. You are still young, good-looking; you could fall in love, get married, be happy...”

“Not all married women are happy,” she snapped, in her former rude patter.

“Not all, of course; but still, much better off than here. Incomparably better. And with love one can live even without happiness. Even in grief life is good; it is good to live in the world, however one lives. But here, what is there but... stench. Phew!”

I turned away in disgust; I was no longer coldly reasoning. I was beginning to feel what I was saying myself, and to grow heated. I was already longing to set out my cherished little ideas, nursed in my corner. Something suddenly caught fire in me; some aim had “appeared”.

“Don’t look at me being here; I am no example for you. I may be worse than you. I came here drunk, anyway,” I hastened to justify myself all the same. “Besides, a man is no example for a woman. It’s a different matter; though I degrade and soil myself, I am nobody’s slave: I come, I go, and I’m gone. I shake it off and I’m a different man. But you, from the very start, are a slave. Yes, a slave! You give away everything, your whole will. And later you’ll want to break these chains, but you won’t be able to: they will bind you tighter and tighter. That is the kind of accursed chain it is. I know it. I won’t even speak of other things; you wouldn’t understand, perhaps; but tell me: you must already owe your madam money? There, you see!” I added, though she had not answered, but only listened silently, with her whole being. “There’s your chain! You’ll never buy yourself out. They’ll see to that. It’s like selling your soul to the devil...

“...And besides, I... maybe I am just as unhappy, how do you know, and wallow in the mud on purpose, from misery too. People drink from grief, after all; well, and I am here from grief. Tell me, what is there good in this: here we... came together... a while ago, and we didn’t say a word to each other the whole time, and afterwards you began examining me like a wild thing, and I you. Is that how people love? Is that how one human being should come together with another? It’s simply hideous, that’s what it is!”

“Yes!” she agreed with me sharply and hastily. I was even surprised at the haste of that “yes”. So perhaps the same thought had been wandering in her head when she was examining me a while ago? So she too was capable of some thoughts?.. “Damn it, this is interesting; this is a kindred thing,” I thought, almost rubbing my hands. “And how can one fail to master such a young soul?..”

It was the game that carried me away most of all.

She turned her head closer to me and, as it seemed to me in the dark, propped it on her hand. Perhaps she was examining me. How I regretted that I could not see her eyes. I heard her deep breathing.

“Why did you come here?” I began, now with a certain authority.

“Just...”

“But how good it would be to live in your father’s house! Warm, free; your own nest.”

“And what if it was worse than this?”

“I must strike the right note,” flashed through my mind. “I won’t get far with sentimentality, perhaps.”

But it only flashed through my mind. I swear she really did interest me. Besides, I was somehow weakened and in the mood. And cunning gets on so easily with feeling.

“Who says otherwise!” I hastened to answer. “Anything can happen. I’m sure that someone wronged you, and that they are more to blame towards you than you towards them. I know nothing of your story, of course, but a girl like you surely doesn’t end up here of her own accord...”

“What sort of girl am I?” she whispered barely audibly; but I heard it.

“Damn it, I’m flattering her. That’s vile. But perhaps it’s good...” She was silent.

“You see, Liza, I’ll tell you about myself! If I had had a family since childhood, I wouldn’t be what I am now. I often think about that. However bad things are in a family, they are still your father and mother, not enemies, not strangers. Even if they show you love only once a year. All the same, you know you are at home. I grew up without a family; that must be why I turned out like this... unfeeling.”

I waited again.

“She probably doesn’t even understand,” I thought, “and it’s ridiculous anyway: moralising.”

“If I were a father and had a daughter of my own, I think I would love my daughter more than my sons, really,” I began obliquely, as if not about this at all, to distract her. I confess I was blushing.

“Why is that?” she asked.

Ah, so she’s listening!

“I don’t know, Liza. You see, I knew a father who was a stern, severe man, but he would kneel before his daughter, kiss her hands and feet, could not stop admiring her, really. She would dance at a party, and he would stand five hours in one place without taking his eyes off her. He was mad about her; I understand that. At night she would get tired and fall asleep, and he would wake up and go and kiss her and make the sign of the cross over her as she slept. He went about in a greasy old coat himself, stingy with everyone, but for her he would spend his last kopeck, give her rich presents, and it was a joy to him if she liked the present. A father always loves his daughters more than a mother does. Some girls have a happy life at home! And I think I wouldn’t even let my daughter marry.”

“Why not?” she asked, with a faint smile.

“I’d be jealous, by God. How could she kiss another man? Love a stranger more than her father? It’s painful even to imagine. Of course, it’s all nonsense; of course, everyone comes to his senses in the end. But I think that before I gave her away I would torment myself to death with worry alone: I’d reject every suitor. And I’d end up all the same by marrying her to the one she loved herself. The one the daughter loves always seems the worst of all to the father. That’s just how it is. A lot of trouble comes of it in families.”

“Some are glad to sell their daughter, never mind giving her honourably,” she said suddenly.

Ah! So that’s it!

“That, Liza, is in those accursed families where there is neither God nor love,” I took up hotly, “and where there is no love, there is no sense either. There are such families, true, but I am not talking about them. You can’t have seen any good in your family, to talk like that. You must be really unfortunate. Hm... It mostly comes from poverty.”

“And is it any better among the gentry? Honest people live well in poverty too.”

“Hm... yes. Perhaps. And there is this too, Liza: people like to count only their troubles, and do not count their happiness. If they counted it properly, they would see that every lot has its share. Well, and what if everything goes well in a family, God blesses it, the husband turns out good, loves you, cherishes you, never leaves your side! How good it is in that family! Even when it is half joy and half sorrow, it is good; and where is there no sorrow? Perhaps you’ll get married and find out yourself. But take even the first days of marriage to the man you love: what happiness, what happiness comes sometimes! And very often too. In the early days even quarrels with your husband end well. Some women, the more they love, the more quarrels they stir up with their husbands. Really; I knew one like that: ‘I love you so much, and it’s out of love that I torment you, and you should feel it.’ Do you know that one can torment a person on purpose, out of love? Women especially. And she thinks to herself: ‘But afterwards I’ll love him so much, I’ll caress him so, that it’s no sin to torment him a little now.’ And everyone in the house rejoices in you, and it is good and cheerful and peaceful and honest... Then some are jealous. He goes out somewhere (I knew one like that), and she can’t bear it, and jumps up in the middle of the night and runs out quietly to see: is he there, in that house, with that woman? That is bad. And she knows herself it is bad, and her heart sinks and torments her, but she loves him; it’s all from love. And how good it is to make up after a quarrel, to admit she was wrong, or to forgive him! And they both feel so good, it suddenly becomes so good, as if they had met anew, married anew, as if their love had begun anew. And no one, no one must know what happens between husband and wife, if they love each other. And whatever quarrel they have, they must not call even their own mother to judge between them, or tell tales about each other. They are their own judges. Love is a mystery of God and must be hidden from all strange eyes, whatever happens. It is holier for that, better. They respect each other more, and much is built on respect. And once there has been love, once they married for love, why should love pass? Can it not be kept up? It is rare that it cannot be kept up. And if the husband turns out kind and honest, how can love pass? The first married love will pass, true, but then a still better love will come. Then they will be united in soul; they will share all their affairs; they will have no secrets from each other. And when the children come, every time, even the hardest, will seem happiness; one only has to love and be brave. Then work is a joy; then one goes without bread sometimes for the children, and even that is a joy. For they will love you for it afterwards; so you are saving for yourself. The children grow, and you feel that you are an example to them, a support to them; that even when you die they will carry your feelings and thoughts all their lives, because they received them from you; they will take on your image and likeness. So it is a great duty. How can the father and mother not grow closer then? People say having children is hard. Who says so? It is heavenly happiness! Do you love little children, Liza? I love them terribly. You know: a rosy little boy at your breast, and what husband’s heart would not turn to his wife, seeing her sitting with his child! The baby, rosy and plump, sprawls and luxuriates; little arms and legs so plump, little nails so clean and tiny, so tiny it makes you laugh to look at them; little eyes as if he already understands everything. And while he sucks, he plucks at your breast with his little hand, plays with it. His father comes up, and he pulls away from the breast, bends right back, looks at his father and laughs, as if heaven knows what were so funny, and then goes back to sucking again. Or he’ll bite his mother’s breast, if his teeth are coming through, and look at her sideways with his little eyes: ‘See, I bit you!’ Isn’t it all happiness, when the three of them, husband, wife and child, are together? For such moments much can be forgiven. No, Liza, one must first learn to live oneself, and only then blame others!”

“With pictures, with pictures like these I must get at you!” I thought to myself, though, by God, I had spoken with feeling; and suddenly I blushed. “And what if she suddenly bursts out laughing; where will I hide then?” This idea drove me into a fury. Towards the end of my speech I really had grown heated, and now my vanity was somehow suffering. The silence lasted. I even wanted to nudge her.

“Why do you...” she began suddenly, and stopped.

But I had already understood everything: something different was trembling in her voice now, not sharp, not rude and unyielding as before, but something soft and shamefaced, so shamefaced that I myself suddenly felt ashamed before her, felt guilty.

“What?” I asked, with tender curiosity.

“Why, you...”

“What?”

“You... somehow... you talk just like a book,” she said, and something mocking seemed to sound in her voice again.

That remark stung me painfully. It was not what I had expected.

I did not understand that she was deliberately hiding behind mockery, that this is the usual last trick of people who are shy and chaste at heart, whose souls are being crudely and intrusively forced, and who out of pride will not give in to the last minute, and are afraid to show their feelings before you. From the very timidity with which she approached her mockery, in several attempts, and only at last brought herself to say it, I ought to have guessed. But I did not guess, and a spiteful feeling seized me.

“Just you wait,” I thought.
