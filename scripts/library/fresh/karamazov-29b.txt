## Chapter VII · The Second Visit to Smerdyakov

By that time Smerdyakov had already left the hospital. Ivan Fyodorovich knew his new lodgings: that very lopsided little log house of two rooms divided by a passage. In one room lived Marya Kondratyevna with her mother, and in the other, separately, Smerdyakov. God knows on what terms he had settled with them, whether he lived there for nothing or for money. Afterwards it was supposed that he had settled with them as Marya Kondratyevna’s suitor and was living there for nothing for the time being. Both mother and daughter respected him very much and looked on him as a man above them. Having knocked till he was let in, Ivan Fyodorovich went into the passage and, on Marya Kondratyevna’s direction, went straight to the left into the “best room”, occupied by Smerdyakov. In this room there was a tiled stove, and it was heated very hot. The walls were adorned with blue wallpaper, all torn, it is true, and in the cracks under it cockroaches swarmed in terrible numbers, so that there was an incessant rustling. The furniture was paltry: two benches along the two walls and two chairs by the table. The table, though of plain wood, was covered with a cloth with a pink pattern. On each of the two little windows stood a pot of geraniums. In the corner was an icon case. On the table stood a small, much-dented copper samovar and a tray with two cups. But Smerdyakov had already finished his tea, and the samovar had gone out… He himself was sitting at the table on the bench, looking at a notebook and tracing something with a pen. A bottle of ink stood beside him, as well as a low cast-iron candlestick with a stearin candle in it. Ivan Fyodorovich concluded at once from Smerdyakov’s face that he had completely recovered from his illness. His face was fresher, fuller; his tuft was whipped up, the hair at his temples smoothed down. He was sitting in a motley quilted dressing gown, very much worn, however, and considerably frayed. On his nose were spectacles, which Ivan Fyodorovich had never seen him wear before. This most trivial circumstance seemed suddenly to make Ivan Fyodorovich doubly angry: “Such a creature, and in spectacles too!” Smerdyakov slowly raised his head and looked intently through his spectacles at the newcomer; then he quietly took them off and half rose on the bench, but somehow not at all so respectfully, somehow even lazily, simply to observe the barest courtesy without which one can hardly manage. All this flashed upon Ivan in an instant; he took it all in and noticed it at once, and above all Smerdyakov’s look, decidedly spiteful, unfriendly and even haughty: “Why do you come hanging about? We settled everything that time; why have you come again?” Ivan Fyodorovich could hardly restrain himself.

“It’s hot in here,” he said, still standing, and unbuttoned his overcoat.

“Take it off, sir,” Smerdyakov permitted.

Ivan Fyodorovich took off his overcoat and threw it on the bench; with trembling hands he took a chair, quickly drew it up to the table and sat down. Smerdyakov had managed to lower himself onto his bench before him.

“First of all, are we alone?” Ivan Fyodorovich asked sternly and impetuously. “They can’t hear us from in there?”

“No one will hear anything, sir. You saw for yourself: there is the passage.”

“Listen, my dear fellow: what was that you blurted out when I was leaving you at the hospital, that if I kept quiet about your being a master at shamming fits, you wouldn’t tell the investigator everything about our conversation at the gate? What is this ‘everything’? What could you have meant by it? Were you threatening me, or what? Have I entered into some sort of alliance with you, am I afraid of you, or what?”

Ivan Fyodorovich said this in a perfect fury, evidently giving him to understand on purpose that he scorned all beating about the bush and all roundabout approaches, and was playing openly. Smerdyakov’s eyes flashed spitefully, his left eye began to wink, and at once, though with his usual restraint and measure, he gave his answer: “You want it straight; well, here it is straight for you.”

“What I meant then, and why I said it, was that you, knowing beforehand about this murder of your own father, left him as a sacrifice then; and so that people should not conclude anything bad afterwards about your feelings, and perhaps about something else besides: that is what I promised then not to tell the authorities.”

Though Smerdyakov spoke without haste and to all appearance in command of himself, something firm and insistent could now be heard in his voice, something spiteful and insolently defiant. He stared boldly at Ivan Fyodorovich, and for the first minute everything even swam before Ivan’s eyes.

“What? What? Are you in your right mind or not?”

“Perfectly in my right mind, sir.”

“Did I know about the murder then?” Ivan Fyodorovich cried out at last, and struck the table hard with his fist. “What do you mean, ‘about something else besides’? Speak, you scoundrel!”

Smerdyakov was silent and went on looking Ivan Fyodorovich over with the same insolent look.

“Speak, you stinking rogue, what ‘something else besides’?” Ivan yelled.

“By that ‘something else besides’ I meant just now that perhaps you yourself very much wished for your father’s death then.”

Ivan Fyodorovich jumped up and struck him on the shoulder with his fist with all his might, so that he reeled back against the wall. In an instant his whole face was flooded with tears, and, saying, “It is shameful, sir, to strike a weak man!”, he suddenly covered his eyes with his blue-checked cotton handkerchief, thoroughly soiled with blowing his nose, and sank into quiet, tearful weeping. A minute passed.

“Enough! Stop it!” Ivan Fyodorovich said at last commandingly, sitting down on the chair again. “Don’t drive me out of my last patience.”

Smerdyakov took his rag away from his eyes. Every line of his wrinkled face expressed the insult he had just suffered.

“So you thought then, you scoundrel, that I wanted to kill my father together with Dmitri?”

“I didn’t know your thoughts then, sir,” said Smerdyakov in an injured tone, “and that is why I stopped you then, as you were coming in at the gate, to test you on that very point, sir.”

“To test what? What?”

“Why, that very circumstance: did you or did you not want your father to be killed as soon as possible?”

What revolted Ivan Fyodorovich most of all was this insistent, insolent tone, from which Smerdyakov stubbornly refused to depart.

“It was you who killed him!” he suddenly exclaimed.

Smerdyakov smiled contemptuously.

“That it was not I who killed him, you know yourself for certain. And I thought there was no need for a clever man to say anything more about it.”

“But why, why did such a suspicion of me come to you then?”

“As you know already, sir, from fear alone. For I was in such a position then that, shaking with fear, I suspected everyone. I resolved to test you too, sir, for if you too, I thought, want the same as your brother, then that is the end of this whole business, and I shall perish along with it, like a fly.”

“Listen, two weeks ago you didn’t say that.”

“I meant the very same thing in the hospital, talking with you; only I supposed that you would understand without unnecessary words, and didn’t want a straight talk yourself, being the cleverest of men, sir.”

“Just listen to him! But answer, answer, I insist: from what exactly, by what exactly could I have put such a low suspicion of me into your vile soul then?”

“To kill, you yourself could never have done that, sir, and didn’t want to; but to want someone else to kill, that you did want.”

“And how calmly, how calmly he says it! But why should I want it? What the devil should I want it for?”

“What do you mean, what the devil for, sir? And the inheritance, sir?” Smerdyakov caught it up venomously and even somehow vindictively. “Why, after your father’s death each of the three brothers would have come in for nearly forty thousand, and maybe even more, sir; whereas if Fyodor Pavlovich had married that very lady then, sir, Agrafena Alexandrovna, she would have transferred all the capital to herself immediately after the wedding, for she is no fool, sir, so that you three brothers wouldn’t have got even two roubles after your father. And how much was left then before the wedding? A hair’s breadth, sir: that lady had only to do like this to him with her little finger, and he would have run to the church after her at once with his tongue hanging out.”

Ivan Fyodorovich restrained himself with suffering.

“Very well,” he said at last. “You see, I haven’t jumped up, I haven’t beaten you, I haven’t killed you. Go on: so, in your opinion, I meant my brother Dmitri for it; I was counting on him?”

“How could you not count on him, sir? For if he killed him, he would lose all the rights of his rank, his rank and property, and go into exile, sir. So then his share after your father would be left to you and your brother Alexei Fyodorovich, in equal parts, sir; that is, you would each have got not forty but sixty thousand, sir. You were certainly counting on Dmitri Fyodorovich then!”

“What I put up with from you! Listen, you villain: if I had been counting on anyone then, it would surely have been on you, not on Dmitri; and, I swear, I even had a presentiment of some vileness from you… then… I remember my impression!”

“And I too thought then, for one little minute, that you were counting on me too,” Smerdyakov grinned mockingly, “so that by that very thing you gave yourself away to me still more then; for if you had a presentiment about me and at the same time went away, then by that very thing it was just as if you had said to me: you may kill my father, and I won’t hinder you.”

“Scoundrel! That’s how you understood it!”

“And all through that very Chermashnya, sir. Have mercy! You were getting ready to go to Moscow and refused all your father’s requests to go to Chermashnya, sir! And on one foolish word of mine you suddenly agreed, sir! And why should you have agreed to that Chermashnya then? If you went not to Moscow but to Chermashnya, without reason, on one word of mine, then you must have been expecting something from me.”

“No, I swear, no!” Ivan yelled, grinding his teeth.

“How do you mean, no, sir? On the contrary, for such words of mine then, you, your father’s son, ought first of all to have taken me to the police station and had me flogged, sir… or at least boxed my ears on the spot; whereas you, if you please, sir, on the contrary, without being the least bit angry, at once in a friendly way do exactly as my very foolish word says, sir, and go away, which was quite absurd, sir, for you ought to have stayed to guard your father’s life… How could I not conclude something?”

Ivan sat scowling, his two fists pressed convulsively against his knees.

“Yes, it’s a pity I didn’t box your ears,” he smiled bitterly. “I couldn’t have dragged you to the police station then: who would have believed me, and what could I have pointed to? But your ears… ugh, a pity I didn’t think of it; though ear-boxing is forbidden, I would have made pulp of your mug.”

Smerdyakov looked at him almost with relish.

“In the ordinary cases of life,” he said in that self-satisfied, doctrinaire tone in which he had once argued with Grigory Vasilyevich about faith and teased him, standing behind Fyodor Pavlovich’s table, “in the ordinary cases of life ear-boxing is indeed forbidden by law nowadays, and everyone has stopped beating, sir; but in the distinctive cases of life, not only here but all over the world, even in the most complete French republic, they go on beating just the same as in the days of Adam and Eve, sir, and they never will stop, sir; but you didn’t dare even in a distinctive case then, sir.”

“Why are you learning French words?” Ivan nodded at the notebook lying on the table.

“And why shouldn’t I learn them, sir, to further my education, thinking that I too may some day happen to be in those happy parts of Europe?”

“Listen, you monster,” Ivan’s eyes flashed and he shook all over, “I am not afraid of your accusations; say what you like against me; and if I haven’t beaten you to death just now, it is only because I suspect you of this crime and will bring you to trial. I shall expose you yet!”

“In my opinion, you had better keep quiet, sir. For what can you say against me, in my complete innocence, and who will believe you? Only, if you begin, then I will tell everything too, sir; for how could I not defend myself?”

“Do you think I am afraid of you now?”

“Even if they don’t believe all these words of mine that I have been telling you now in court, sir, the public will believe them, sir, and you will be ashamed, sir.”

“So that means again: ‘It is worth talking to a clever man,’ eh?” Ivan ground out.

“You have hit the very point, sir. So be clever, sir.”

Ivan Fyodorovich got up, trembling all over with indignation, put on his overcoat and, without answering Smerdyakov any more, without even looking at him, went quickly out of the room. The fresh evening air refreshed him. The moon was shining brightly in the sky. A terrible nightmare of thoughts and sensations was seething in his soul. “Shall I go and inform against Smerdyakov at once? But inform of what? He is innocent all the same. On the contrary, he will accuse me. Indeed, why did I go to Chermashnya then? Why, why?” Ivan Fyodorovich asked himself. “Yes, of course I was expecting something, and he is right…” And again for the hundredth time he remembered how on the last night at his father’s he had listened to him from the stairs; but he remembered it now with such suffering that he even stopped on the spot as if pierced: “Yes, I was expecting it then; that is true! I wanted, I precisely wanted the murder! Did I want the murder, did I?… I must kill Smerdyakov!… If I don’t dare to kill Smerdyakov now, life is not worth living!…” Ivan Fyodorovich, without going home, went straight then to Katerina Ivanovna and frightened her by his appearance: he was like a madman. He told her his whole conversation with Smerdyakov, every bit of it. He could not calm down, however much she tried to persuade him; he kept walking about the room and talking in a jerky, strange way. At last he sat down, leaned his elbows on the table, propped his head in both hands and uttered a strange aphorism:

“If it wasn’t Dmitri but Smerdyakov who killed him, then of course I am at one with him, for I egged him on. Whether I egged him on I don’t know yet. But if it was he who killed him, and not Dmitri, then of course I am a murderer too.”

Hearing this, Katerina Ivanovna rose silently from her place, went to her writing desk, unlocked a casket that stood on it, took out a piece of paper and laid it before Ivan. This piece of paper was that very document of which Ivan Fyodorovich afterwards told Alyosha as of “mathematical proof” that his brother Dmitri had killed their father. It was a letter written by Mitya when drunk to Katerina Ivanovna, on the very evening when he met Alyosha in the fields on his way back to the monastery, after the scene in Katerina Ivanovna’s house when Grushenka had insulted her. Then, having parted from Alyosha, Mitya had rushed off to Grushenka; it is not known whether he saw her, but by night he found himself at the Metropolis tavern, where he got thoroughly drunk. Drunk, he demanded pen and paper and scrawled a document of great importance against himself. It was a frenzied, wordy and incoherent letter, a “drunken” one precisely. It was like when a drunken man, coming home, begins with extraordinary heat to tell his wife or someone in the household how he has just been insulted, what a scoundrel the man who insulted him is, what a fine man he himself is, on the contrary, and how he will pay that scoundrel out; and all this long, long, incoherent and excited, with fists banging on the table and drunken tears. The paper they gave him in the tavern was a dirty scrap of ordinary writing paper, of poor quality, with some bill written on the back of it. The drunken verbosity evidently had not enough room, and Mitya filled not only all the margins, but even wrote the last lines crosswise over what was already written. The letter ran as follows:

“Fatal Katya! Tomorrow I shall get the money and give you back your three thousand, and farewell, woman of great wrath, but farewell, my love too! Let us make an end! Tomorrow I shall try to get it from everyone, and if I can’t get it from people, I give you my word of honour I shall go to my father and smash his head and take it from under his pillow, if only Ivan has gone. I shall go to penal servitude, but I shall give back the three thousand. And as for you, farewell. I bow down to the ground before you, for I am a scoundrel before you. Forgive me. No, better not forgive me: it will be easier for me and for you! Better penal servitude than your love, for I love another, and today you have come to know her too well; how can you forgive? I shall kill my thief! I shall go away from you all to the East, so as to know no one. Her too, for you are not my only tormentress; she is too. Farewell!

“P.S. I write a curse, and I adore you! I hear it in my breast. A string is left and it rings. Better my heart torn in two! I shall kill myself, but first of all the cur. I shall tear the three thousand from him and fling it to you. Though a scoundrel before you, I am not a thief! Expect the three thousand. It is under the cur’s mattress, a pink ribbon. It is not I who am the thief, but I shall kill my thief. Katya, don’t look contemptuous: Dmitri is not a thief, but a murderer! He has killed his father and ruined himself, so as to stand firm and not have to bear your pride. And not to love you.

“P.P.S. I kiss your feet; farewell!

“P.P.S.S. Katya, pray to God that people will give me the money. Then I shall not be steeped in blood; but if they don’t give it, in blood! Kill me!

“Your slave and enemy,
“D. Karamazov.”

When Ivan had read the “document”, he got up convinced. So it was his brother who had killed him, and not Smerdyakov. And if not Smerdyakov, then not he, Ivan, either. In his eyes the letter suddenly took on a mathematical meaning. There could no longer be any doubt for him of Mitya’s guilt. Incidentally, Ivan had never had any suspicion that Mitya might have killed him together with Smerdyakov; nor did it fit the facts. Ivan was completely set at rest. The next morning he remembered Smerdyakov and his jeers only with contempt. A few days later he even wondered how he could have been so tormentingly offended by his suspicions. He resolved to despise him and forget him. So a month passed. He did not ask anyone about Smerdyakov any more, but heard in passing, two or three times, that he was very ill and not in his right mind. “He will end in madness,” the young doctor Varvinsky once said of him, and Ivan remembered it. In the last week of that month Ivan himself began to feel very unwell. He had already been to consult the doctor from Moscow whom Katerina Ivanovna had sent for and who had arrived just before the trial. And just at this time his relations with Katerina Ivanovna became strained to the last degree. They were like two enemies in love with each other. Katerina Ivanovna’s returns to Mitya, momentary but strong, now drove Ivan into perfect frenzy. It is strange that until the very last scene, which we have described at Katerina Ivanovna’s, when Alyosha came to her from Mitya, he, Ivan, had not once heard from her during the whole month any doubts of Mitya’s guilt, in spite of all her “returns” to him, which he hated so. It is remarkable too that, feeling that he hated Mitya more and more every day, he understood at the same time that he hated him not for Katya’s “returns” to him, but precisely for having killed their father! He felt and was fully aware of this himself. Nevertheless, some ten days before the trial he went to Mitya and proposed to him a plan of escape, a plan evidently thought out long before. Here, besides the main cause that moved him to such a step, there was also to blame a certain scratch in his heart that would not heal, from one little word of Smerdyakov’s: that it was supposedly to his, Ivan’s, advantage that his brother should be convicted, because the sum of the inheritance from their father would then rise for him and Alyosha from forty to sixty thousand. He resolved to sacrifice thirty thousand on his own part to arrange Mitya’s escape. Coming back from him that time, he was terribly sad and troubled: he suddenly began to feel that he wanted the escape not only so as to sacrifice thirty thousand to it and heal the scratch, but for some other reason too. “Is it because in my soul I am just such a murderer too?” he began to ask himself. Something remote but burning stung his soul. Above all, all that month his pride had suffered terribly; but of that later… When, after his conversation with Alyosha, he had taken hold of the bell of his lodgings and suddenly decided to go to Smerdyakov, Ivan Fyodorovich was obeying a particular indignation that had suddenly boiled up in his breast. He suddenly remembered how Katerina Ivanovna had just cried out to him in Alyosha’s presence: “It was you, you alone who convinced me that he (that is, Mitya) was the murderer!” Remembering this, Ivan was even dumbfounded: never in his life had he assured her that Mitya was the murderer; on the contrary, he had even suspected himself before her when he came back from Smerdyakov. On the contrary, it was she, she, who had laid the “document” before him then and proved his brother’s guilt! And now suddenly she herself exclaims: “I have been to Smerdyakov myself!” When had she been? Ivan knew nothing of it. So she was not at all so sure of Mitya’s guilt! And what could Smerdyakov have told her? What, what exactly had he told her? A terrible anger blazed up in his heart. He could not understand how half an hour before he could have let those words pass and not shouted out at once. He let go of the bell and set off for Smerdyakov’s. “Perhaps I shall kill him this time,” he thought on the way.
