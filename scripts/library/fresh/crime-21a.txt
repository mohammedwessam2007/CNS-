## CHAPTER VIII

When he came to Sonya’s, dusk was already beginning. All day Sonya had been waiting for him in terrible agitation. She and Dunya had waited together. Dunya had come to her in the morning, remembering Svidrigailov’s words of the day before, that Sonya “knew about it”. We will not give the details of the conversation and the tears of the two women, and how close they became to each other. Dunya carried away from this meeting at least one consolation: that her brother would not be alone: it was to her, Sonya, that he had come first with his confession; in her he had sought a human being when he needed a human being; and she would follow him wherever fate sent him. Dunya did not even ask, but she knew that it would be so. She looked at Sonya even with a kind of reverence, and at first almost embarrassed her by this reverent feeling with which she treated her. Sonya was even ready almost to cry: she, on the contrary, considered herself unworthy even to look at Dunya. Dunya’s beautiful image, when she had bowed to her with such attention and respect at their first meeting in Raskolnikov’s room, had remained in her soul ever since as one of the most beautiful and unattainable visions of her life.

At last Dunechka could not bear it and left Sonya, to wait for her brother in his lodging; she kept thinking he would go there first. Left alone, Sonya at once began to be tormented by fear at the thought that perhaps he really would end it by suicide. Dunya feared the same. But all day they had vied with each other in persuading each other by every argument that this could not be, and they had been calmer while they were together. Now, as soon as they had parted, each of them began to think of this alone. Sonya remembered how yesterday Svidrigailov had told her that Raskolnikov had two roads: the Vladimirka or... She knew besides his vanity, his arrogance, his pride and his unbelief. “Can it be that only faint-heartedness and fear of death can make him live?” she thought at last in despair. The sun meanwhile was already setting. She stood sadly before the window and looked out of it intently, but from this window only the blank unwhitewashed wall of the neighbouring house could be seen. At last, when she had come to complete conviction that the unhappy man was dead, he came into her room.

A joyful cry broke from her breast. But looking intently into his face, she suddenly turned pale.

“Well, yes!” said Raskolnikov, smiling. “I have come for your crosses, Sonya. It was you who sent me to the crossroads; what, now that it has come to the point, have you lost your nerve?”

Sonya looked at him in amazement. His tone seemed strange to her; a cold shiver passed over her body, but a minute later she guessed that both the tone and the words were all put on. And he was talking to her looking somehow into a corner, as if avoiding looking her straight in the face.

“You see, Sonya, I have decided that this way will perhaps be more advantageous. There is a circumstance here... Well, it would take long to tell, and there is no point. Do you know what alone makes me angry? I am vexed that all those stupid, bestial mugs will surround me in a moment, will goggle at me with their eyes, ask me their stupid questions, which one has to answer; they will point their fingers... Pah! You know, I am not going to Porfiry; I am sick of him. I would rather go to my friend Gunpowder; how I’ll surprise him, what an effect of a kind I shall produce. But I ought to be cooler; I have become far too bilious lately. Would you believe it: just now I almost shook my fist at my sister only because she turned round to look at me for the last time. Swinishness, such a state! Eh, what I have come to! Well, then, where are the crosses?”

He was as if not himself. He could not even stand still in one place for a minute, could not fix his attention on a single object; his thoughts leapt over one another, he was rambling; his hands were trembling slightly.

Sonya silently took two crosses out of a drawer, one of cypress wood and one of copper, crossed herself, made the sign of the cross over him and put the little cypress cross round his neck.

“So this is a symbol that I am taking up the cross, he-he! And really, I have suffered little so far! Cypress, that is, the common people’s; the copper one is Lizaveta’s; you are taking it for yourself; let me see? So she was wearing it... at that moment? I know two similar crosses too, a silver one and a little icon. I threw them down on the old woman’s breast that time. Those would be just right now, really; those are the ones I ought to put on... But then, I am talking nonsense, I’ll forget the business; I am somehow absent-minded!.. You see, Sonya, I came properly speaking to forewarn you, so that you would know... Well, that’s all... That is all I came for. (Hm, I thought, though, that I would say more.) Why, you wanted me to go yourself; well, so I shall sit in prison, and your wish will come true; well, why are you crying? You too? Stop it, enough; oh, how hard all this is for me!”

A feeling, however, was born in him; his heart contracted as he looked at her. “This one, what about this one?” he thought to himself. “What am I to her? Why is she crying, why is she getting me ready like a mother or Dunya? She will be my nurse!”

“Cross yourself, pray at least once,” Sonya asked in a trembling, timid voice.

“Oh, by all means, as much as you like! And with a pure heart, Sonya, with a pure heart...”

He wanted, however, to say something else.

He crossed himself several times. Sonya seized her shawl and threw it over her head. It was the green drap-de-dames shawl, probably the same one that Marmeladov had mentioned that time, “the family one”. The thought flashed through Raskolnikov’s mind, but he did not ask. Indeed, he had begun to feel himself that he was terribly absent-minded and somehow hideously agitated. He was frightened by this. He was struck too by the fact that Sonya meant to go out with him.

“What are you doing! Where are you going? Stay, stay! I go alone,” he cried in faint-hearted vexation and, almost angrily, went to the door. “And what is the need of a whole retinue!” he muttered as he went out.

Sonya stayed in the middle of the room. He had not even said goodbye to her; he had already forgotten her; one caustic and rebellious doubt was boiling up in his soul.

“But is it right, is it right, all this?” he thought again, going down the stairs. “Is it really impossible to stop even now and put everything right again... and not go?”

But still he went. He suddenly felt, finally, that there was no point in asking himself questions. Coming out into the street, he remembered that he had not said goodbye to Sonya, that she had stayed in the middle of the room, in her green shawl, not daring to move after his shout, and he stopped for a moment. At that same instant a thought suddenly lit him up brightly, as if it had been waiting to strike him finally.

“Well, what for, why did I go to her now? I told her: on business; on what business? There was no business at all! To announce that I am going; what of it? What a need! Do I love her, then? No, surely not? Why, I drove her away just now like a dog. Did I really need crosses from her? Oh, how low I have fallen! No: I needed her tears, I needed to see her fright, to watch her heart ache and be torn! I had to catch hold of something at least, to delay, to look at a human being! And I dared to hope so much in myself, to dream so about myself, beggar that I am, nothing that I am, scoundrel, scoundrel!”

He walked along the embankment of the canal, and he did not have far to go now. But on reaching the bridge he stopped, and suddenly turned onto the bridge, aside, and crossed to the Haymarket.

He looked round greedily to the right and the left, peered intently at every object and could fix his attention on nothing; everything slipped away. “In a week, in a month, I shall be driven somewhere in one of these prison carriages across this bridge; how shall I look at this canal then? Should I remember this?” flashed through his head. “Here is this signboard; how shall I read these very letters then? Here it says ‘Company’; well, remember this a, the letter a, and look at it in a month, at this very a: how shall I look at it then? What shall I feel and think then?.. God, how base all this must be, all these present... concerns of mine! Of course, all this must be curious... in its way... (ha-ha-ha! what am I thinking about!) I am becoming a child, I am showing off before myself; well, why am I shaming myself? Pah, how they push! That fat man, a German, I suppose, who pushed me: well, does he know whom he pushed? A peasant woman with a child is begging; it is curious that she thinks me happier than herself. Why not give her something, for the oddity of it? Ba, a five-kopeck piece has survived in my pocket; where from? Here, here... take it, mother!”

“God keep you!” came the beggar woman’s plaintive voice.

He came into the Haymarket. It was unpleasant, very unpleasant for him to rub shoulders with people, but he went precisely where more people could be seen. He would have given anything in the world to be left alone; but he felt himself that he would not stay alone for a single minute. In the crowd a drunk man was making a nuisance of himself: he kept wanting to dance, but kept falling to one side. People had gathered round him. Raskolnikov squeezed through the crowd, looked at the drunk man for a few minutes and suddenly laughed shortly and abruptly. A minute later he had already forgotten him, did not even see him, though he was looking at him. At last he moved away, not even remembering where he was; but when he reached the middle of the square, a movement suddenly took place in him, a single sensation took hold of him all at once, seized him entirely, body and mind.

He suddenly remembered Sonya’s words: “Go to the crossroads, bow down to the people, kiss the earth, because you have sinned against it too, and say aloud to the whole world: ‘I am a murderer!’” He trembled all over, remembering it. And the hopeless anguish and anxiety of all this time, but especially of the last hours, had so crushed him that he simply flung himself into the possibility of this whole, new, full sensation. It came over him suddenly like a kind of fit: it caught fire in his soul as a single spark, and suddenly, like a fire, seized him entirely. Everything in him softened at once, and the tears gushed out. Just as he stood, he fell to the ground...

He knelt in the middle of the square, bowed to the ground and kissed that dirty earth with delight and happiness. He stood up and bowed a second time.

“Look how sozzled he is!” a lad beside him remarked.

There was laughter.

“He’s going to Jerusalem, brothers, saying goodbye to his children, to his homeland, bowing to the whole world, kissing the capital city of St Petersburg and its soil,” added some tipsy little tradesman.

“Still a young lad!” put in a third.

“One of the gentry!” someone remarked in a solid voice.

“Nowadays you can’t tell who’s gentry and who isn’t.”

All these responses and remarks held Raskolnikov back, and the words “I killed”, which were perhaps ready to fly from his tongue, died away in him. He bore all these shouts calmly, however, and, without looking round, walked straight through a lane in the direction of the police office. One vision flashed before him on the way, but he was not surprised at it; he had already had a presentiment that it must be so. At the moment when, in the Haymarket, he had bowed to the ground the second time, turning to the left, about fifty paces from him he had seen Sonya. She was hiding from him behind one of the wooden booths that stood in the square; so she had accompanied his whole sorrowful procession! Raskolnikov felt and understood at that moment, once and for all, that Sonya was with him now for ever and would follow him even to the end of the world, wherever fate might send him. His whole heart turned over... but here he was already at the fatal place...

He went into the courtyard fairly briskly. He had to climb to the third floor. “I will climb up yet, for the time being,” he thought. In general it seemed to him that the fatal moment was still far off, that a lot of time still remained, that much could still be thought over.

Again the same rubbish, the same eggshells on the spiral staircase, again the doors of the flats wide open, again the same kitchens, from which fumes and stench poured out. Raskolnikov had not been here since that time. His legs were growing numb and giving way, but they went. He stopped for a moment to catch his breath, to collect himself, to go in like a man. “But what for? why?” he thought suddenly, grasping the meaning of his movement. “If I must drink this cup, isn’t it all the same? The nastier the better.” At that moment the figure of Ilya Petrovich Gunpowder flashed into his imagination. “Am I really going to him? Couldn’t I go to another? Couldn’t I go to Nikodim Fomich? Turn round now and go to the superintendent himself at his lodging? At least it would go off in a homely way... No, no! To Gunpowder, to Gunpowder! If I must drink, then drink it all at once...”

Gone cold and hardly aware of himself, he opened the door into the office. This time there were very few people in it; some caretaker was standing there and some other common man. The porter did not even look out from behind his partition. Raskolnikov went into the next room. “Perhaps I won’t have to speak after all,” flashed through him. Here some individual from among the clerks, in a civilian frock coat, was settling down to write something at a desk. In the corner another clerk was taking his seat. Zamyotov was not there. Nikodim Fomich, of course, was not there either.

“Is there no one here?” Raskolnikov asked, turning to the individual at the desk.

“Whom do you want?”

“A-a-ah! Not a sound to be heard, not a sight to be seen, but I smell a Russian smell... how does it go in the fairy tale... I forget! My r-respects to you!” a familiar voice cried suddenly.

Raskolnikov trembled. Gunpowder was standing before him; he had suddenly come out of the third room. “This is fate itself,” thought Raskolnikov. “Why is he here?”

“Come to us? On what business?” exclaimed Ilya Petrovich. (He was evidently in the most excellent and even slightly excited state of spirits.) “If it is on business, you have come too early. I am here myself by chance... But anyway, whatever I can do. I confess to you... what? what? Forgive me...”

“Raskolnikov.”

“Well, of course: Raskolnikov! And could you really suppose that I had forgotten! Please don’t take me for that kind of... Rodion Ro... Ro... Rodionych, I believe?”

“Rodion Romanych.”

“Yes, yes, yes! Rodion Romanych, Rodion Romanych! That is just what I was trying to get at. I even inquired many times. I confess to you that I have sincerely grieved since then that we were so, that time... it was explained to me afterwards; I learned that you were a young writer and even a scholar... and, so to speak, first steps... Oh Lord! Why, which of the writers and scholars did not take original steps at first! My wife and I both respect literature, and my wife to the point of passion!.. Literature and the arts! If only a man is noble; the rest can all be acquired by talents, knowledge, reason, genius! A hat: well, what does a hat mean, for instance? A hat is a pancake; I can buy one at Zimmermann’s; but what is kept under the hat and covered by the hat, that I cannot buy, sir!.. I confess I even wanted to come to you and explain myself, but I thought, perhaps you... But I won’t even ask: do you really need something? They say your family has come?”

“Yes, my mother and sister.”

“I even had the honour and happiness of meeting your sister; a cultivated and charming person. I confess I regretted that we got so heated with each other that time. A curious case! And as for my casting a certain look at you that time, about your fainting, it was afterwards explained in the most brilliant way! Bigotry and fanaticism! I understand your indignation. Perhaps you are changing lodgings, on account of the family’s arrival?”

“N-no, I just... I came in to ask... I thought I would find Zamyotov here.”

“Ah, yes! You have become friends, I heard, sir. Well, Zamyotov is not with us; you have missed him. Yes, sir, we have lost Alexander Grigorievich! Since yesterday he is no longer present; he has transferred... and in transferring he even quarrelled with everyone... so impolitely even... A flighty boy, nothing more; he might even have shown promise; but there you are, with our brilliant youth! He wants to take some examination or other, but with us it is only a matter of talking and showing off, and that will be the end of the examination. It is not as with you, for instance, or Mr Razumikhin there, your friend! Your career is in learning, and failures won’t throw you off any more! For you all these beauties of life, one may say, nihil est, are nothing; an ascetic, a monk, a hermit!.. For you, a book, a pen behind the ear, learned research: that is where your spirit soars! I myself, partly... have you been pleased to read Livingstone’s journals?”

“No.”

“But I have. Nowadays, by the way, a great many nihilists have spread about; well, but that is understandable; what times are these, I ask you? But then, with you... why, of course you are not a nihilist! Answer frankly, frankly!”

“N-no...”

“No, you know, be frank with me, don’t be shy, as if you were alone with yourself! Service is one thing, and... you thought I was going to say friendship; no, sir, you didn’t guess! Not friendship, but the feeling of a citizen and a man, the feeling of humaneness and of love for the Most High. I may be an official person and on duty, but I am always bound to feel the citizen and the man in myself and to give an account... You were pleased to mention Zamyotov. Zamyotov will make some scandal in the French manner in an improper establishment, over a glass of champagne or Don wine; that is what your Zamyotov is! But I, perhaps, so to speak, am consumed with devotion and lofty feelings, and besides have importance, rank, hold a position! I am married and have children. I fulfil the duty of a citizen and a man, and who is he, allow me to ask? I treat you as a man ennobled by education. Here too, these midwives are spreading excessively.”

Raskolnikov raised his eyebrows questioningly. The words of Ilya Petrovich, who had obviously only just risen from the dinner table, rattled and poured out before him mostly as empty sounds. But part of them he did somehow understand; he looked questioningly and did not know how all this would end.

“I mean these cropped-haired girls,” the talkative Ilya Petrovich went on. “I have named them midwives myself, on my own account, and I find the name entirely satisfactory. He-he! They push into the academy, they study anatomy; well, tell me, if I fall ill, am I going to call in a young lady to treat me? He-he!”

Ilya Petrovich guffawed, entirely pleased with his witticisms.

“Granted, it is an immoderate thirst for enlightenment; but once one is enlightened, that is enough. Why abuse it? Why insult noble persons, as that scoundrel Zamyotov does? Why did he insult me, I ask you? And here too, how many of these suicides have spread, you cannot imagine. They all spend their last money and kill themselves. Girls, boys, old men... Here, only this morning there was a report about some gentleman who had recently arrived. Nil Pavlych, hey, Nil Pavlych! What was his name, the gentleman they reported earlier, who shot himself on the Petersburg Side?”

“Svidrigailov,” someone answered hoarsely and indifferently from the other room.

Raskolnikov started.

“Svidrigailov! Svidrigailov has shot himself!” he cried.

“What! You know Svidrigailov?”

“Yes... I know him... He came here recently...”

“Well, yes, he came recently, lost his wife, a man of dissolute conduct, and suddenly he shot himself, and so scandalously that you can’t imagine it... he left a few words in his notebook that he was dying in his right mind and asked that no one be blamed for his death. He had money, they say. How do you come to know him?”

“I... am acquainted... my sister lived in their house as a governess...”

“Ba, ba, ba... Then you can tell us about him. And you had no suspicion?”

“I saw him yesterday... he... was drinking wine... I knew nothing.”

Raskolnikov felt as if something had fallen on him and crushed him.

“You seem to have turned pale again. The air is so stale here...”

“Yes, it is time for me to go, sir,” muttered Raskolnikov. “Forgive me for troubling you...”

“Oh, not at all, as much as you like! You have given me pleasure, and I am glad to declare...”

Ilya Petrovich even held out his hand.

“I only wanted... I came to see Zamyotov...”

“I understand, I understand, and you have given me pleasure.”

“I... am very glad... goodbye, sir...” Raskolnikov smiled.

He went out; he was swaying. His head was spinning. He did not feel whether he was standing on his feet. He began to go down the stairs, leaning his right hand against the wall. It seemed to him that some caretaker with a book in his hand pushed him, climbing up towards him to the office; that some little dog was barking its head off somewhere on the lower floor and that some woman threw a rolling pin at it and shouted. He went down and came out into the courtyard. There in the courtyard, not far from the way out, stood Sonya, pale, numb all over, and she looked at him wildly, wildly. He stopped before her. Something sick and tormented showed in her face, something desperate. She clasped her hands. A hideous, lost smile forced itself onto his lips. He stood there, smiled, and turned back upstairs, to the office again.

Ilya Petrovich had sat down and was rummaging in some papers. Before him stood the same peasant who had just pushed Raskolnikov as he climbed the stairs.

“A-a-ah? You again! Have you left something?.. But what is the matter with you?”

Raskolnikov, with whitened lips and a fixed look, quietly came up to him, went right up to the table, leaned his hand on it, wanted to say something, but could not; only some incoherent sounds could be heard.

“You are unwell; a chair! Here, sit on the chair, sit down! Water!”

Raskolnikov sank onto the chair, but did not take his eyes off the face of Ilya Petrovich, who was very unpleasantly surprised. For a minute both looked at each other and waited. Water was brought.

“It was I...” Raskolnikov began.

“Drink some water.”

Raskolnikov pushed the water away with his hand and said quietly, deliberately, but distinctly:

“It was I who killed the old official’s widow and her sister Lizaveta with an axe that time, and robbed them.”

Ilya Petrovich’s mouth fell open. People came running from all sides.

Raskolnikov repeated his statement.
