## EPILOGUE · I

Siberia. On the bank of a broad, desolate river stands a town, one of the administrative centres of Russia; in the town is a fortress, in the fortress a prison. In the prison, for nine months now, the convict of the second category Rodion Raskolnikov has been confined. Almost a year and a half has passed since the day of his crime.

The legal proceedings in his case went off without great difficulties. The criminal maintained his statement firmly, precisely and clearly, not confusing the circumstances, not softening them in his own favour, not distorting the facts, not forgetting the slightest detail. He told the whole process of the murder down to the last feature: he explained the secret of the pledge (the little wooden board with a metal strip) which had been found in the murdered old woman’s hands; he told in detail how he had taken the keys from the murdered woman, described those keys, described the trunk and what it was filled with; he even listed some of the separate objects that lay in it; he explained the riddle of Lizaveta’s murder; he told how Koch had come and knocked, and after him the student, repeating everything they had said to each other; how he, the criminal, had afterwards run down the stairs and heard Mikolka and Mitka shrieking; how he had hidden in the empty flat and come home; and in conclusion he pointed out the stone in the courtyard, on Voznesensky Prospect, under the gateway, under which the things and the purse were found. In a word, the case came out clear. The investigators and judges were very surprised, among other things, that he had hidden the purse and the things under the stone without making use of them, and most of all that he not only did not remember in detail all the things he had actually stolen, but was even mistaken about their number. The very circumstance that he had not once opened the purse and did not even know exactly how much money lay in it seemed incredible (in the purse there turned out to be three hundred and seventeen silver roubles and three twenty-kopeck pieces; from lying a long time under the stone some of the top notes, the largest ones, had been extremely damaged). They tried for a long time to find out why precisely the accused was lying in this one circumstance, when in everything else he confessed voluntarily and truthfully. At last some (especially among the psychologists) even admitted the possibility that he really had not looked into the purse, and so did not know what was in it, and, not knowing, had carried it straight under the stone; but from this very thing they at once concluded that the crime itself could not have happened otherwise than in a state of a certain temporary insanity, so to speak, of a morbid monomania of murder and robbery, without further aims or calculations of gain. Here, by the way, the newest fashionable theory of temporary insanity came in handy, which in our time people so often try to apply to certain criminals. Besides, Raskolnikov’s long-standing hypochondriac state was precisely attested by many witnesses: Doctor Zosimov, his former comrades, his landlady, the servant. All this greatly contributed to the conclusion that Raskolnikov was not quite like an ordinary murderer, bandit and robber, but that there was something else here. To the greatest vexation of those who defended this opinion, the criminal himself hardly tried to defend himself; to the final questions, what exactly could have inclined him to murder and what had induced him to commit robbery, he answered very clearly, with the coarsest exactness, that the cause of everything had been his wretched position, his poverty and helplessness, his wish to secure the first steps of his career in life with the help of at least three thousand roubles which he had counted on finding at the murdered woman’s. And he had resolved on the murder as a result of his frivolous and faint-hearted character, irritated, moreover, by privations and failures. To the questions what exactly had induced him to come and confess, he answered directly that it was sincere repentance. All this was almost coarse...

The sentence, however, turned out more merciful than might have been expected, judging by the crime committed, and perhaps precisely because the criminal not only did not wish to justify himself, but even seemed to express a desire to accuse himself still more. All the strange and peculiar circumstances of the case were taken into account. The sick and wretched state of the criminal before committing the crime was not subjected to the slightest doubt. The fact that he had not made use of what he had stolen was counted partly as the action of an awakened repentance, partly as a not entirely sound state of his mental faculties at the time of committing the crime. The circumstance of the unpremeditated murder of Lizaveta even served as an example supporting the latter supposition: a man commits two murders and at the same time forgets that the door is standing unlocked! Finally, the confession, made at the very time when the case had become extraordinarily muddled through the false self-accusation of a dispirited fanatic (Nikolai), and besides, when there was not only no clear evidence against the real criminal but almost not even any suspicion (Porfiry Petrovich had fully kept his word): all this finally contributed to softening the accused’s fate.

Other circumstances also came to light, quite unexpectedly, which were strongly in the accused’s favour. The former student Razumikhin dug up information from somewhere and presented proof that the criminal Raskolnikov, while he was at the university, had helped a poor, consumptive fellow student of his out of his last means and had almost kept him for half a year. And when that student died, he had looked after the surviving old and paralysed father of his dead comrade (who had kept and fed his father by his own labour almost from the age of thirteen), had at last placed the old man in a hospital, and when he too died, had buried him. All this information had a certain favourable influence on the decision of Raskolnikov’s fate. His former landlady herself, the mother of Raskolnikov’s dead bride, the widow Zarnitsyna, also testified that when they were still living in another house, at Five Corners, Raskolnikov, during a fire, at night, had carried two small children out of a flat that was already burning, and had been burned in doing so. This fact was carefully investigated and fairly well attested by many witnesses. In a word, it ended with the criminal being sentenced to penal servitude of the second category, for a term of only eight years, in consideration of his confession and of certain extenuating circumstances.

At the very beginning of the trial Raskolnikov’s mother fell ill. Dunya and Razumikhin found it possible to take her away from Petersburg for the whole time of the trial. Razumikhin chose a town on the railway, at a short distance from Petersburg, so as to be able to follow all the circumstances of the trial regularly and at the same time to see Avdotya Romanovna as often as possible. Pulkheria Alexandrovna’s illness was a strange one, a nervous one, and was accompanied by something like insanity, if not completely, then at least partly. Dunya, returning from her last meeting with her brother, found her mother already quite ill, in a fever and delirious. That same evening she agreed with Razumikhin on what exactly to answer her mother’s questions about her brother, and even invented with him, for her mother, a whole story about Raskolnikov’s departure somewhere far away, to the frontier of Russia, on a private commission which would at last bring him both money and fame. But they were struck by the fact that Pulkheria Alexandrovna herself asked nothing about any of this, either then or afterwards. On the contrary, she turned out to have a whole story of her own about her son’s sudden departure; she told with tears how he had come to say goodbye to her; she let it be understood by hints that only she alone knew many very important and mysterious circumstances, and that Rodya had many very powerful enemies, so that he even had to go into hiding. As for his future career, that too seemed to her certain and brilliant, once certain hostile circumstances had passed; she assured Razumikhin that her son would in time even become a statesman, as his article and his brilliant literary talent proved. She read that article incessantly, sometimes even aloud, almost slept with it, and yet she hardly asked where exactly Rodya was now, even though they were obviously avoiding talking to her about it, which alone might have aroused her suspicion. At last they began to fear this strange silence of Pulkheria Alexandrovna on certain points. She did not even complain, for example, that there were no letters from him, whereas before, living in her little town, she had lived only on the hope and expectation of getting a letter soon from her beloved Rodya. This last circumstance was far too inexplicable and greatly worried Dunya; the thought came to her that her mother perhaps had a presentiment of something terrible in her son’s fate and was afraid to ask, so as not to find out something still more terrible. In any case Dunya saw clearly that Pulkheria Alexandrovna was not in a sound state of mind.

Once or twice, however, it happened that she herself steered the conversation so that it was impossible, in answering her, not to mention where exactly Rodya was now; and when the answers inevitably turned out unsatisfactory and suspicious, she suddenly became extremely sad, gloomy and silent, which lasted a very long time. Dunya saw at last that it was hard to lie and invent, and came to the final conclusion that it was better to keep completely silent on certain points; but it became more and more obviously clear that the poor mother suspected something terrible. Dunya remembered, among other things, her brother’s words that their mother had been listening to her delirium, on the night before that last fatal day, after her scene with Svidrigailov: had she not overheard something then? Often, sometimes after several days and even weeks of sullen, gloomy silence and mute tears, the sick woman would come to life somehow hysterically and suddenly begin to talk aloud, almost without stopping, about her son, about her hopes, about the future... Her fancies were sometimes very strange. They humoured her, they agreed with her (perhaps she herself saw clearly that they were agreeing with her and only humouring her), but she went on talking all the same...

Five months after the criminal’s confession his sentence followed. Razumikhin saw him in prison whenever it was possible. So did Sonya. At last the parting came; Dunya swore to her brother that this parting was not for ever; so did Razumikhin. In Razumikhin’s young and ardent head a plan had firmly taken root: during the next three or four years to lay, as far as possible, at least the beginning of a future fortune, to save at least some money and move to Siberia, where the soil is rich in every respect and workers, people and capital are few; to settle there in the very town where Rodya would be and... all together to begin a new life. As they said goodbye, everyone wept. In the very last days Raskolnikov was very thoughtful, asked a great deal about his mother, and was constantly worried about her. He even tormented himself about her too much, which alarmed Dunya. On learning in detail of his mother’s morbid state of mind, he became very gloomy. With Sonya he was for some reason especially taciturn all the time. Sonya, with the help of the money Svidrigailov had left her, had long since got herself ready to follow the party of convicts in which he would be sent. Not a word had ever been said about this between her and Raskolnikov; but both knew that it would be so. At the very last farewell he smiled strangely at the ardent assurances of his sister and Razumikhin about their happy future when he came out of penal servitude, and he foretold that his mother’s sick condition would soon end in disaster. At last he and Sonya set off.

Two months later Dunechka married Razumikhin. The wedding was sad and quiet. Among those invited, however, were Porfiry Petrovich and Zosimov. All this last time Razumikhin had had the look of a man who had firmly made up his mind. Dunya believed blindly that he would carry out all his intentions, and indeed she could not help believing it: an iron will showed in this man. Among other things, he began attending university lectures again, so as to finish his course. Both of them kept making plans for the future every minute; both firmly counted on moving to Siberia within five years for certain. Until then they relied on Sonya there...

Pulkheria Alexandrovna joyfully blessed her daughter’s marriage to Razumikhin; but after this marriage she seemed to become even sadder and more anxious. To give her a pleasant moment, Razumikhin told her, among other things, the facts about the student and his decrepit father, and about how Rodya had been burned and had even been ill after saving two little children from death the year before. Both pieces of news brought Pulkheria Alexandrovna, whose mind was already disordered, almost to a state of rapture. She talked of it incessantly, started conversations even in the street (though Dunya always accompanied her). In public carriages, in shops, catching any listener at all, she turned the conversation to her son, to his article, to how he had helped the student, had been burned in a fire, and so on. Dunechka did not even know how to restrain her. Besides the danger of such a rapturous, morbid state, the mere fact threatened trouble that someone might remember the name Raskolnikov from the recent court case and start talking about it. Pulkheria Alexandrovna even found out the address of the mother of the two little children saved from the fire and absolutely wanted to go and see her. At last her restlessness grew to extreme limits. Sometimes she would suddenly begin to cry; she often fell ill and was delirious in fever. One morning she declared outright that by her reckoning Rodya must arrive soon, that she remembered how, saying goodbye to her, he himself had mentioned that he was to be expected in exactly nine months. She began tidying everything in the flat and preparing for the meeting; she began doing up the room intended for him (her own), cleaning the furniture, washing and putting up new curtains, and so on. Dunya was alarmed, but kept silent, and even helped her arrange the room to receive her brother. After an anxious day spent in incessant fancies, in joyful dreams and tears, she fell ill in the night, and by morning she was already in fever and delirium. A brain fever set in. Within two weeks she was dead. In her delirium words broke from her from which one could conclude that she had suspected much more about her son’s terrible fate than they had even supposed.

For a long time Raskolnikov did not know of his mother’s death, although correspondence with Petersburg had been established from the very beginning of his settling in Siberia. It was arranged through Sonya, who punctually every month wrote to Petersburg in Razumikhin’s name and punctually every month received an answer from Petersburg. At first Sonya’s letters seemed somehow dry and unsatisfactory to Dunya and Razumikhin; but in the end they both found that it was impossible to write better, because from these letters, in the end, there emerged after all the fullest and most exact picture of their unhappy brother’s fate. Sonya’s letters were filled with the most everyday reality, the simplest and clearest description of all the circumstances of Raskolnikov’s convict life. There was no account in them of her own hopes, no guessing about the future, no description of her own feelings. Instead of attempts to explain his state of mind and his whole inner life in general, there were only facts, that is, his own words, detailed news of the state of his health, what he had wished for at such and such a meeting, what he had asked her for, what he had entrusted to her, and so on. All this news was reported in extraordinary detail. In the end the image of the unhappy brother emerged of itself, drawn precisely and clearly; there could be no mistakes here, because everything was true facts.

But Dunya and her husband could draw little comfort from this news, especially at first. Sonya reported constantly that he was always sullen, taciturn, and even hardly at all interested in the news she passed on to him each time from the letters she received; that he sometimes asked about his mother; and when she, seeing that he already guessed the truth, at last told him of her death, to her surprise even the news of his mother’s death did not seem to affect him very strongly, at least so it seemed to her from his outward appearance. She reported, among other things, that in spite of his seeming so withdrawn into himself and as if shut off from everyone, he had taken to his new life very directly and simply; that he clearly understood his position, expected nothing better in the near future, had no frivolous hopes (which are so natural in his position) and was surprised at almost nothing amid his new surroundings, so unlike anything before. She reported that his health was satisfactory. He went to the work, which he neither avoided nor asked for. He was almost indifferent to food, but this food, except on Sundays and holidays, was so bad that at last he willingly accepted some money from her, Sonya, so as to have his own tea every day; as for everything else, he asked her not to worry, assuring her that all these cares about him only annoyed him. Further Sonya reported that his quarters in the prison were shared with everyone; she had not seen the inside of their barracks, but concluded that it was crowded, hideous and unhealthy there; that he slept on the plank bed, spreading a piece of felt under himself, and wanted to arrange nothing else for himself. But that he lived so roughly and poorly not at all according to some preconceived plan or intention, but simply from inattention and outward indifference to his fate. Sonya wrote plainly that, especially at first, he was not only not interested in her visits, but was even almost annoyed with her, was taciturn and even rude to her, but that in the end these meetings became a habit with him and even almost a need, so that he grieved very much when she was ill for several days and could not visit him. She saw him on holidays at the prison gates or in the guardroom, where he was called out to her for a few minutes; and on weekdays at work, where she would come to see him, either in the workshops, or at the brickworks, or in the sheds on the bank of the Irtysh. About herself Sonya reported that she had even managed to make some acquaintances and find some patrons in the town; that she did sewing, and since there was almost no dressmaker in the town, she had even become indispensable in many houses; only she did not mention that through her Raskolnikov too had received the protection of the authorities, that his work was made easier, and so on. At last news came (Dunya even noticed a certain special agitation and alarm in her last letters) that he shunned everyone, that the convicts in the prison had not taken to him; that he was silent for whole days and was becoming very pale. Suddenly, in her last letter, Sonya wrote that he had fallen very seriously ill and was lying in the hospital, in the convicts’ ward...

## EPILOGUE · II

He had been ill for a long time already; but it was not the horrors of convict life, not the work, not the food, not the shaved head, not the patched clothes that had broken him: oh! what did all these torments and tortures matter to him! On the contrary, he was even glad of the work: wearing himself out physically at work, he at least gained for himself a few hours of peaceful sleep. And what did food mean to him, this thin cabbage soup with cockroaches? As a student, in his former life, he had often not had even that. His clothes were warm and suited to his way of life. He did not even feel the fetters on him. Was he to be ashamed of his shaved head and his parti-coloured jacket? But before whom? Before Sonya? Sonya was afraid of him, and was it before her that he should be ashamed?

And yet? He was ashamed even before Sonya, whom he tormented for it with his contemptuous and rough treatment. But it was not of his shaved head and his fetters that he was ashamed: his pride had been badly wounded; it was from wounded pride that he had fallen ill. Oh, how happy he would have been if he could have accused himself! He would have borne everything then, even shame and disgrace. But he judged himself sternly, and his embittered conscience found no especially terrible guilt in his past, except perhaps a simple blunder, which might have happened to anyone. He was ashamed precisely of this: that he, Raskolnikov, had perished so blindly, hopelessly, dully and stupidly, by some sentence of blind fate, and must humble himself and submit before the “senselessness” of some sentence, if he wanted to calm himself at all.

An anxiety without object and without aim in the present, and in the future one unbroken sacrifice by which nothing was gained: that was what lay before him in the world. And what of it that in eight years he would be only thirty-two and could begin to live again! Why should he live? What should he have in view? What should he strive for? To live in order to exist? But a thousand times before he had been ready to give his existence for an idea, for a hope, even for a fancy. Mere existence had always been too little for him; he had always wanted more. Perhaps it was only by the strength of his desires that he had then considered himself a man to whom more was permitted than to another.

And if only fate had sent him repentance: burning repentance, breaking the heart, driving away sleep, such repentance as makes one dream of the noose and the whirlpool in its terrible torment! Oh, he would have been glad of it! Torment and tears: why, that too is life. But he did not repent of his crime.

At least he could have raged at his stupidity, as he had raged before at the hideous and stupidest actions that had brought him to prison. But now, already in prison, at liberty to think, he examined and thought over all his former actions again and did not find them at all so stupid and hideous as they had seemed to him in that fatal time before.

“In what way, in what way,” he thought, “was my thought more stupid than other thoughts and theories that have swarmed and collided with one another in the world since this world began? One has only to look at the matter with a completely independent, broad view, free from everyday influences, and then of course my thought will turn out to be not at all so... strange. O negators and sages at five kopecks a head, why do you stop halfway!

“Well, why does my action seem so hideous to them?” he said to himself. “Because it is a villainy? What does the word ‘villainy’ mean? My conscience is at peace. Of course, a criminal offence has been committed; of course, the letter of the law has been broken and blood has been shed; well, take my head for the letter of the law... and that’s enough! Of course, in that case even many benefactors of humanity, who did not inherit power but seized it themselves, ought to have been executed at their very first steps. But those men carried their steps through, and so they are right, while I did not carry mine through, and so I had no right to permit myself that step.”

That was the only thing in which he acknowledged his crime: only in not having carried it through and having made his confession.

He suffered too from the thought: why had he not killed himself then? Why had he stood over the river then and preferred to confess? Was there really such strength in this wish to live, and was it so hard to overcome? Svidrigailov, who was afraid of death, had overcome it, hadn’t he?

He asked himself this question in torment and could not understand that even then, when he stood over the river, he had perhaps foreseen in himself and in his convictions a profound falsehood. He did not understand that this presentiment might be the herald of a future turning point in his life, of his future resurrection, of a future new view of life.

He was more inclined to admit here only the dull weight of instinct, which it was not for him to break, and which again he did not have the strength to step over (because of weakness and worthlessness). He looked at his fellow convicts and was amazed: how they all loved life too, how they valued it! It seemed to him precisely that in prison they loved and valued it even more, and held it dearer, than in freedom. What terrible torments and tortures some of them had endured, the vagrants for instance! Could a single ray of sunshine mean so much to them, a dense forest, a cold spring somewhere in an unknown wilderness, marked as far back as two years before, which the vagrant dreams of meeting as one dreams of meeting a lover, sees it in his sleep, with the green grass round it and a bird singing in a bush? Looking further, he saw examples still more inexplicable.

In the prison, in the surroundings about him, there was of course much that he did not notice, and he did not want to notice it at all. He lived with his eyes somehow lowered: it was loathsome and unbearable to him to look. But in the end much began to surprise him, and he began, somehow involuntarily, to notice what he had not even suspected before. In general, what began to surprise him most was that terrible, that impassable gulf which lay between him and all these people. It seemed that he and they were of different nations. He and they looked at each other mistrustfully and with hostility. He knew and understood the general causes of such separation; but he had never admitted before that these causes were in fact so deep and strong. In the prison there were also exiled Poles, political prisoners. They simply regarded all these people as ignoramuses and boors and despised them from on high; but Raskolnikov could not look at it that way: he saw clearly that these ignoramuses were in many ways far cleverer than these same Poles. There were Russians here too who despised these people too much: one former officer and two seminarists; Raskolnikov clearly noticed their mistake too.

He himself was disliked and avoided by everyone. In the end they even began to hate him; why? He did not know. They despised him, laughed at him; those who were much greater criminals than he laughed at his crime.

“You’re a gentleman!” they said to him. “Was it for you to go about with an axe? That’s no business for a gentleman at all.”

In the second week of Great Lent it came to his turn to fast and take communion together with his barrack. He went to church to pray together with the others. Over what, he did not know himself, a quarrel arose one day; everyone at once fell on him in fury.

“You’re godless! You don’t believe in God!” they shouted at him. “You ought to be killed.”

He had never talked to them about God or faith, but they wanted to kill him as godless; he was silent and did not object. One convict rushed at him in a decided frenzy; Raskolnikov waited for him calmly and silently: his eyebrow did not stir, not a feature of his face quivered. The guard managed to step between him and the would-be murderer in time, or else blood would have been shed.

There was one more question insoluble for him: why had they all come to love Sonya so much? She did not court them; they met her rarely, sometimes only at work, when she came for a minute to see him. And yet everyone knew her already; they knew too that she had followed him; they knew how she lived, where she lived. She did not give them money or render them any special services. Only once, at Christmas, she brought alms for the whole prison: pies and kalach loaves. But little by little certain closer relations grew up between them and Sonya: she wrote letters for them to their families and sent them by post. Their relatives, men and women, who came to the town, left things for them, and even money, in Sonya’s hands, as they directed. Their wives and sweethearts knew her and went to see her. And when she appeared at work, coming to see Raskolnikov, or met a party of convicts going to work, they all took off their caps, they all bowed: “Little mother, Sofya Semyonovna, you are our mother, tender and compassionate!” these coarse, branded convicts said to this small, thin creature. She smiled and bowed in return, and they all liked it when she smiled at them. They even liked her walk; they turned round to watch her as she went, and praised her; they praised her even for being so small; they no longer even knew what to praise her for. They even went to her to be treated when they were ill.

He lay in the hospital all the end of Lent and all of Easter week. When he was already recovering, he remembered the dreams he had had while still lying in fever and delirium. He had dreamed in his illness that the whole world was condemned to fall victim to some terrible, unheard-of and unseen pestilence, coming from the depths of Asia upon Europe. All were to perish, except certain, very few, chosen ones. Some new trichinae had appeared, microscopic beings that took up their dwelling in men’s bodies. But these beings were spirits, endowed with intelligence and will. People who took them into themselves at once became possessed and mad. But never, never had people considered themselves so clever and so unshakeable in the truth as those who were infected considered themselves. Never had they considered their judgements, their scientific conclusions, their moral convictions and beliefs more unshakeable. Whole villages, whole towns and nations were infected and went mad. All were in alarm and did not understand one another; each thought that the truth was contained in him alone, and suffered looking at the others, beat his breast, wept and wrung his hands. They did not know whom to judge or how; they could not agree on what to consider evil and what good. They did not know whom to accuse, whom to acquit. People killed each other in some senseless malice. They gathered against each other in whole armies, but the armies, already on the march, suddenly began to tear themselves to pieces; the ranks fell apart, the warriors threw themselves on each other, stabbed and cut, bit and ate each other. In the towns the alarm bell was rung all day: everyone was summoned, but who was summoning and why, no one knew, and everyone was in alarm. The most ordinary trades were abandoned, because each proposed his own ideas, his own improvements, and they could not agree; agriculture came to a stop. Here and there people ran together in groups, agreed on something together, swore not to part, but at once began something completely different from what they had themselves just proposed, began to accuse each other, fought and cut each other down. Fires began, famine began. Everyone and everything was perishing. The pestilence grew and moved further and further. Only a few people in the whole world could be saved; they were the pure and the chosen, destined to begin a new race of people and a new life, to renew and purify the earth; but no one had seen these people anywhere, no one had heard their word or their voice.

What tormented Raskolnikov was that this senseless delirium echoed so sadly and so painfully in his memories, that the impression of these fevered dreams lasted so long. It was already the second week after Easter; there were warm, clear spring days; in the convicts’ ward the windows had been opened (barred windows, under which a sentry walked). All through his illness Sonya had been able to visit him in the ward only twice; each time permission had to be asked, and that was difficult. But she often came to the hospital yard, under the windows, especially towards evening, and sometimes just to stand in the yard for a minute and look at the windows of the ward, even from a distance. One evening Raskolnikov, almost completely recovered, fell asleep; on waking, he happened to go to the window and suddenly saw Sonya in the distance, at the hospital gate. She was standing as if waiting for something. Something seemed to pierce his heart at that moment; he started and quickly moved away from the window. The next day Sonya did not come, nor the third day; he noticed that he was waiting for her anxiously. At last he was discharged. Coming back to the prison, he learned from the convicts that Sofya Semyonovna had fallen ill, was lying at home and did not go out anywhere.

He was very uneasy and sent to inquire about her. He soon learned that her illness was not dangerous. Learning in her turn that he was pining and worrying so about her, Sonya sent him a note written in pencil, telling him that she was much better, that she had a slight, light cold and that she would soon, very soon, come to see him at work. When he read this note his heart beat hard and painfully.

The day was again clear and warm. Early in the morning, at about six o’clock, he set off for work, on the bank of the river, where a kiln for baking alabaster had been set up in a shed and where it was pounded. Only three workers went there. One of the convicts took a guard and went with him to the fortress for some tool; another began preparing wood and loading it into the kiln. Raskolnikov came out of the shed onto the very bank, sat down on some logs piled by the shed and began looking at the broad and desolate river. From the high bank a wide view of the country opened out. From the distant other bank a song could be faintly heard. There, in the boundless steppe flooded with sunlight, the nomads’ yurts showed black as barely noticeable dots. There was freedom there, and other people lived there, quite unlike the people here; there it was as if time itself had stopped, as if the age of Abraham and his flocks had not yet passed. Raskolnikov sat and looked motionless, without tearing himself away; his thought passed into reverie, into contemplation; he was thinking of nothing, but a kind of anguish stirred and tormented him.

Suddenly Sonya was beside him. She had come up almost inaudibly and sat down beside him. It was still very early; the morning chill had not yet softened. She was wearing her poor old cloak and the green shawl. Her face still bore signs of illness; it had grown thinner, paler, more drawn. She smiled at him kindly and joyfully, but, as usual, timidly held out her hand to him.

She always held out her hand to him timidly, sometimes did not even offer it at all, as if afraid that he would push it away. He always took her hand as if with disgust, always met her as if with vexation, sometimes stubbornly kept silent during her whole visit. It happened that she trembled before him and went away in deep grief. But now their hands did not part; he glanced at her quickly, said nothing and lowered his eyes to the ground. They were alone; no one saw them. The guard had turned away at that moment.

How it happened he did not know himself, but suddenly something seemed to seize him and throw him at her feet. He wept and embraced her knees. In the first instant she was terribly frightened, and her whole face went numb. She jumped up from her place and, trembling, looked at him. But at once, in that same instant, she understood everything. Infinite happiness lit up in her eyes; she understood, and for her there was no longer any doubt that he loved her, loved her infinitely, and that at last this moment had come...

They wanted to speak, but could not. Tears stood in their eyes. They were both pale and thin; but in these sick and pale faces there already shone the dawn of a renewed future, of a full resurrection into a new life. They had been resurrected by love; the heart of each held infinite sources of life for the heart of the other.

They resolved to wait and be patient. Seven years were still left to them; and until then, so much unbearable torment and so much infinite happiness! But he had risen again, and he knew it, felt it fully with his whole renewed being, and she: why, she lived only his life!

In the evening of that same day, when the barracks had already been locked, Raskolnikov lay on the plank bed and thought about her. That day it had even seemed to him that all the convicts, his former enemies, were already looking at him differently. He had even started talking to them himself, and they had answered him kindly. He remembered this now, but of course that was how it was bound to be: shouldn’t everything change now?

He thought about her. He remembered how he had constantly tormented her and torn her heart; he remembered her pale, thin little face, but these memories hardly tormented him now: he knew with what infinite love he would now redeem all her sufferings.

And what were all these, all these torments of the past! Everything, even his crime, even his sentence and exile, seemed to him now, in the first impulse, some external, strange fact, as if it had not even happened to him. That evening, however, he could not think for long and steadily about anything, could not concentrate his mind on anything; and he would not have resolved anything consciously now; he only felt. Instead of dialectics there was life, and something quite different had to work itself out in his consciousness.

Under his pillow lay the Gospels. He took the book mechanically. It belonged to her; it was the same one from which she had read to him about the raising of Lazarus. At the beginning of his penal servitude he had thought that she would torment him with religion, would talk about the Gospels and press books on him. But to his greatest surprise she never once spoke of it, never once even offered him the Gospels. He himself had asked her for it not long before his illness, and she had silently brought him the book. Until now he had not even opened it.

He did not open it now either, but one thought flashed through him: “Can her convictions not be my convictions now? Her feelings, her aspirations, at least...”

She too had been in agitation all that day, and in the night she even fell ill again. But she was so happy that she was almost frightened of her happiness. Seven years, only seven years! At the beginning of their happiness, at some moments, they were both ready to look on those seven years as seven days. He did not even know that the new life would not be given to him for nothing, that it had still to be dearly bought, paid for with a great future deed...

But here a new story begins, the story of the gradual renewal of a man, the story of his gradual regeneration, of his gradual passing from one world into another, of his acquaintance with a new, hitherto completely unknown reality. That might make the subject of a new tale; but our present tale is ended.
