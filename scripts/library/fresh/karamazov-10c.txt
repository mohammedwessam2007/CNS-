## Chapter III · The Brothers Get Acquainted

Ivan, however, was not in a private room. It was only a place by the window, screened off, but those sitting behind the screens could not be seen by others all the same. This room was the entrance room, the first one, with a sideboard against the side wall. Waiters kept darting through it every minute. Of the customers there was only one little old man, a retired military man, drinking tea in a corner. But in the other rooms of the tavern all the usual tavern bustle was going on: calls for the waiter, the uncorking of beer bottles, the click of billiard balls; an organ was droning. Alyosha knew that Ivan almost never went to this tavern and was no lover of taverns in general; so he must be here, Alyosha thought, only in order to meet brother Dmitry by arrangement. And yet brother Dmitry was not there.

“I’ll order you fish soup or something; you don’t live on tea alone, do you?” cried Ivan, apparently terribly pleased to have caught Alyosha. He himself had already finished his dinner and was drinking tea.

“Let’s have fish soup, and then tea too; I’m hungry,” said Alyosha cheerfully.

“And cherry jam? They have it here. Do you remember how you loved cherry jam at Polenov’s when you were little?”

“You remember that? Let’s have the jam too; I still love it.”

Ivan rang for the waiter and ordered fish soup, tea and jam.

“I remember everything, Alyosha; I remember you until you were eleven; I was in my fifteenth year then. Fifteen and eleven: that is such a difference that brothers of those ages are never companions. I don’t know whether I even loved you. When I went to Moscow, for the first years I didn’t even think of you at all. Then, when you came to Moscow yourself, I think we met only once somewhere. And here I have been living four months now, and until now we haven’t said a word to each other. I am leaving tomorrow, and I was just thinking, sitting here: how can I see him, to say goodbye; and you come walking past.”

“And did you very much want to see me?”

“Very much. I want to get acquainted with you once and for all, and to acquaint you with me. And then say goodbye. In my opinion it is best to get acquainted before parting. I saw how you looked at me all these three months; there was a kind of unbroken expectation in your eyes, and that is just what I can’t stand; that is why I didn’t come up to you. But in the end I learned to respect you: the little fellow, I thought, stands firm. Note that, though I am laughing now, I am speaking seriously. You do stand firm, don’t you? I love such firm people, whatever they stand on, even if they are such little boys as you. Your expectant look was not at all repulsive to me in the end; on the contrary, at last I came to love your expectant look... You seem to love me for some reason, Alyosha?”

“I do love you, Ivan. Brother Dmitry says of you: Ivan is a grave. I say of you: Ivan is a riddle. You are a riddle to me even now, but I have already grasped something in you, and only since this morning!”

“What is that?” laughed Ivan.

“You won’t be angry?” Alyosha laughed too.

“Well?”

“That you are just as young a man as all other young men of twenty-three, just as young, youthful, fresh and fine a boy; well, a greenhorn of a boy, in short! What, have I hurt you very much?”

“On the contrary, you have struck me by a coincidence!” Ivan cried cheerfully and warmly. “Would you believe that after our meeting at her place this morning I have been thinking of nothing but that, of my twenty-three-year-old greenness, and now you suddenly guess it exactly and begin with that very thing. I was just sitting here, and do you know what I was saying to myself: if I didn’t believe in life, if I lost faith in the woman I love, lost faith in the order of things, were convinced even that everything, on the contrary, is a disorderly, damned and perhaps devilish chaos, if all the horrors of human disillusionment struck me, still I would want to live, and once I have set my lips to this cup I won’t tear myself away from it until I have drained it all! However, by thirty I shall probably drop the cup, even if I haven’t drunk it all, and go away... I don’t know where. But until I am thirty, I know this firmly, my youth will overcome everything: every disillusionment, every disgust with life. I have asked myself many times: is there in the world such despair as would overcome in me this frenzied and perhaps indecent thirst for life? And I have decided that there seems to be none, that is, again, until those thirty years, and then I won’t want it myself; so it seems to me. Some consumptive snotty moralists often call this thirst for life base, especially the poets. It is partly a Karamazov trait, it’s true, this thirst for life in spite of everything; it certainly sits in you too; but why is it base? There is still a terrible amount of centripetal force on our planet, Alyosha. One wants to live, and I live, even if it is against logic. I may not believe in the order of things, but the sticky little leaves that open in the spring are dear to me, the blue sky is dear to me, some person is dear to me, whom sometimes, would you believe it, you love without knowing why; some human deed is dear to me, in which one has perhaps long since stopped believing, and yet out of old habit one honours it with one’s heart. Here, they have brought your fish soup; eat it and good health to you. The fish soup is splendid; they cook well here. I want to go to Europe, Alyosha; I shall go from here; and of course I know that I shall be going only to a graveyard, but to the most, the most precious graveyard, that’s what! Precious dead lie there; every stone over them speaks of such ardent past life, of such passionate faith in their deed, in their truth, in their struggle and in their science, that I know beforehand I shall fall on the ground and kiss those stones and weep over them, while at the same time convinced with all my heart that it has all long been nothing but a graveyard and nothing more. And I shall weep not from despair, but simply because I shall be happy in the tears I shed. I shall get drunk on my own tenderness. I love the sticky spring leaves, the blue sky, that’s what! It is not the mind here, not logic; here one loves with one’s insides, with one’s belly; one loves one’s first young strength... Do you understand anything of my rubbish, Alyoshka, or not?” Ivan suddenly laughed.

“I understand it only too well, Ivan: one wants to love with one’s insides and one’s belly; you put that beautifully, and I am terribly glad that you want so much to live,” exclaimed Alyosha. “I think that everyone must love life above everything in the world.”

“Love life more than its meaning?”

“Certainly so: love it before logic, as you say; certainly before logic, and only then shall I understand its meaning too. That is what I have long been dimly seeing. Half your work is done, Ivan, and won: you love to live. Now you must try for your second half, and you are saved.”

“You are saving me already, but perhaps I wasn’t perishing! And what is it, your second half?”

“It is that you must raise your dead, who perhaps never died at all. Well, let’s have tea. I’m glad we are talking, Ivan.”

“I see you are in a kind of inspiration. I terribly love such professions de foi, professions of faith, from such... novices. You are a firm man, Alexei. Is it true that you want to leave the monastery?”

“It is true. My elder is sending me into the world.”

“Then we shall see each other again in the world; we shall meet before thirty, when I begin to tear myself away from the cup. Now Father doesn’t want to tear himself away from his cup until seventy; he even dreams of eighty, he said so himself; with him it is far too serious, though he is a buffoon. He has taken his stand on his sensuality as if on a rock too... though after thirty, it is true, there is perhaps nothing else to stand on but that... But to seventy is base; better to thirty: one can keep ‘a shade of nobility’, deceiving oneself. Have you seen Dmitry today?”

“No, I haven’t, but I saw Smerdyakov.” And Alyosha told his brother quickly and in detail about his meeting with Smerdyakov. Ivan suddenly began to listen with great concern; he even asked about some things again.

“Only he asked me not to tell brother Dmitry what he had said about him,” Alyosha added.

Ivan frowned and fell to thinking.

“Is it because of Smerdyakov that you are frowning?” asked Alyosha.

“Yes, because of him. To the devil with him; I really did want to see Dmitry, but now there’s no need...” Ivan said reluctantly.

“And are you really leaving so soon, brother?”

“Yes.”

“And what about Dmitry and Father? How will it end between them?” Alyosha said anxiously.

“You and your same old rigmarole! What have I to do with it? Am I my brother Dmitry’s keeper, or what?” Ivan began to snap irritably, but suddenly smiled somehow bitterly. “Cain’s answer to God about his murdered brother, eh? Perhaps that is what you are thinking at this moment? But, the devil take it, I can’t really stay here as their keeper, can I? I have finished my business and I am going. You don’t think, do you, that I am jealous of Dmitry, that I have been trying all these three months to take his beauty Katerina Ivanovna away from him? Eh, the devil, I had my own business. I have finished my business and I am going. I finished my business this morning; you were a witness.”

“This morning, at Katerina Ivanovna’s?”

“Yes, at hers; and I got free all at once. And what of it? What business is Dmitry of mine? Dmitry has nothing to do with it. I had only my own business with Katerina Ivanovna. You know yourself that, on the contrary, Dmitry behaved as if he were in a conspiracy with me. I didn’t ask him at all, and he solemnly handed her over to me himself and gave us his blessing. It is all quite laughable. No, Alyosha, no; if you knew how light I feel now! I was sitting here having dinner, and would you believe it, I was going to ask for champagne to celebrate my first hour of freedom. Pah, almost half a year, and suddenly, all at once, I have thrown it all off. Well, did I even suspect yesterday that if one wanted to, it would cost nothing to end it!”

“Are you talking about your love, Ivan?”

“Love, if you like, yes; I fell in love with a young lady, an institute girl. I tormented myself over her, and she tormented me. I sat over her... and suddenly it all flew off. This morning I spoke with inspiration, but I went out and burst out laughing; do you believe it? No, I mean it literally.”

“And even now you speak of it so cheerfully,” Alyosha remarked, gazing into his face, which had indeed suddenly brightened.

“But how was I to know that I didn’t love her at all! He-he! And so it turned out that I don’t. And yet how I liked her! How I liked her even this morning, when I was making my speech. And do you know, I like her terribly even now, and yet how easy it is to go away from her. Do you think I am showing off?”

“No. Only perhaps it wasn’t love.”

“Alyoshka,” laughed Ivan, “don’t go reasoning about love! It isn’t proper for you. This morning, this morning you jumped in, ah! I even forgot to kiss you for it... And how she tormented me! Truly I was sitting at the breaking point. Oh, she knew that I loved her! She loved me, not Dmitry,” Ivan insisted cheerfully. “Dmitry is only a strain she puts herself under. Everything I said to her this morning is the real truth. But the whole point, the most important point, is that she needs perhaps fifteen or twenty years to realise that she doesn’t love Dmitry at all, but loves only me, whom she torments. And perhaps she will never realise it, even after today’s lesson. Well, so much the better: I got up and left for ever. By the way, how is she now? What happened there after I left?”

Alyosha told him about the hysterics and that she seemed to be unconscious now and delirious.

“Isn’t Khokhlakova lying?”

“I think not.”

“I must find out. No one has ever died of hysterics, though. And let it be hysterics; God sent woman hysterics out of love. I shan’t go there at all. Why push in again.”

“But you told her this morning that she had never loved you.”

“I did that on purpose. Alyoshka, shall I order champagne? We’ll drink to my freedom. No, if you knew how glad I am!”

“No, brother, better not drink,” Alyosha said suddenly. “Besides, I feel somehow sad.”

“Yes, you have been sad for a long time; I have seen it for a long time.”

“So you will certainly go tomorrow morning?”

“Morning? I didn’t say morning... But then, perhaps in the morning. Would you believe it, I dined here today solely so as not to dine with the old man, he has become so repulsive to me. Because of him alone I would have left long ago. And why are you so worried about my going? You and I have still God knows how much time before I go. A whole eternity of time, immortality!”

“If you are leaving tomorrow, what eternity is there?”

“But what has that to do with you and me?” laughed Ivan. “We shall still have time to talk over what is ours, what we came here for. Why are you staring in surprise? Answer: what did we meet here for? To talk about my love for Katerina Ivanovna, about the old man and Dmitry? About going abroad? About the fatal situation of Russia? About the Emperor Napoleon? Is that it, is it for that?”

“No, not for that.”

“So you understand yourself what for. Others have one thing, but we greenhorns have another; first of all we must solve the everlasting questions; that is our concern. All young Russia is talking now only about the eternal questions. Precisely now, when the old men have all suddenly taken to practical questions. What did you look at me in expectation for all these three months? To ask me: ‘What is your creed, or have you none at all?’ That is what your three months of looks came to, Alexei Fyodorovich, isn’t it?”

“Perhaps it is,” smiled Alyosha. “You aren’t laughing at me now, brother?”

“I, laughing? I don’t want to grieve my little brother, who has looked at me for three months in such expectation. Alyosha, look straight at me: I am just such a little boy as you are myself, only not a novice. How do Russian boys go about things until now? Some of them, I mean? Here, for instance, is this stinking tavern; they meet here and sit down in a corner. They never knew each other before in their lives, and when they leave the tavern they won’t know each other again for forty years; well, and what will they talk about while they have caught a moment in the tavern? About the world questions, nothing less: is there a God, is there immortality? And those who don’t believe in God, well, they will talk about socialism and anarchism, about remaking all humanity on a new pattern; and that comes to the same devil; still the same questions, only from the other end. And a great many, a great many of the most original Russian boys do nothing but talk about the eternal questions among us in our time. Isn’t that so?”

“Yes, for real Russians the questions of whether there is a God and whether there is immortality, or, as you say, the questions from the other end, are of course the first questions and come before everything, and so they should,” said Alyosha, still gazing at his brother with the same quiet and searching smile.

“Here is the thing, Alyosha: to be a Russian is sometimes not at all clever, but still it is impossible to imagine anything stupider than what Russian boys busy themselves with now. But there is one Russian boy, Alyoshka, whom I love terribly.”

“How nicely you led up to that,” Alyosha suddenly laughed.

“Well, tell me then where to begin; give the order yourself: with God? Does God exist, is that it?”

“Begin with whatever you like, even from ‘the other end’. You proclaimed yesterday at Father’s that there is no God,” Alyosha looked at his brother searchingly.

“Yesterday at dinner at the old man’s I teased you with that on purpose, and I saw how your little eyes lit up. But now I am not at all averse to talking it over with you, and I say so very seriously. I want to become close to you, Alyosha, because I have no friends; I want to try. Well, just imagine, perhaps I too accept God,” laughed Ivan; “that’s unexpected for you, eh?”

“Yes, of course, if only you are not joking even now.”

“‘Joking’. They said at the elder’s yesterday that I was joking. You see, my dear, there was an old sinner in the eighteenth century who declared that if there were no God, he would have to be invented: s’il n’existait pas Dieu, il faudrait l’inventer. And man really has invented God. And what is strange, what would be marvellous, is not that God really exists; what is marvellous is that such a thought, the thought of the necessity of God, could creep into the head of such a wild and wicked animal as man, so holy is it, so touching, so wise, and so much does it do honour to man. As for me, I have long since resolved not to think about whether man created God or God created man. I won’t, of course, go through all the modern axioms of Russian boys on this score, all derived from European hypotheses; because what is a hypothesis there is at once an axiom for a Russian boy, and not only for the boys but perhaps for their professors too, because Russian professors among us now are very often the same Russian boys. And so I bypass all the hypotheses. What is our task now, yours and mine? The task is for me to explain to you as quickly as possible my essence, that is, what kind of man I am, what I believe in and what I hope for; isn’t that so, isn’t it? And so I declare that I accept God simply and directly. But here is what must be noted, however: if God exists and if he really did create the earth, then, as we know perfectly well, he created it according to Euclidean geometry, and the human mind with a notion of only three dimensions of space. And yet there have been and still are geometers and philosophers, even some of the most remarkable, who doubt whether the whole universe, or, still more broadly, all being, was created only according to Euclidean geometry; they even dare to dream that two parallel lines, which according to Euclid can never meet on earth, might perhaps meet somewhere in infinity. I have decided, my dear, that if I cannot understand even this, then how can I understand about God? I humbly admit that I have no ability to solve such questions; I have a Euclidean, earthly mind, and so how are we to decide about what is not of this world? And I advise you too never to think about it, friend Alyosha, and above all about God: does he exist or not? All these are questions entirely unsuited to a mind created with a notion of only three dimensions. And so I accept God, and not only willingly, but, what is more, I accept his wisdom too, and his purpose, which are completely unknown to us; I believe in the order, in the meaning of life; I believe in the eternal harmony in which we are all supposed to merge; I believe in the Word, towards which the universe is striving and which itself ‘was with God’ and which itself is God; well, and so on and so on, and so forth to infinity. Many words have been made up on this score. It seems I am on the right road already, eh? Well, then imagine that in the final result I do not accept this world of God’s, and though I know that it exists, I don’t admit it at all. It is not God that I don’t accept, understand that; it is the world created by him, God’s world, that I don’t accept and cannot agree to accept. I will make a reservation: I am convinced, like a child, that suffering will be healed and smoothed over, that the whole offensive comedy of human contradictions will vanish like a pitiful mirage, like the nasty invention of the feeble and puny, atom-like Euclidean human mind; that at last, in the world’s finale, at the moment of eternal harmony, something will happen and appear so precious that it will suffice for all hearts, for the soothing of all indignation, for the redemption of all the villainies of men, of all the blood they have shed; it will suffice not only to make it possible to forgive but even to justify everything that has happened with men; let it, let all this come and appear, but I don’t accept it and don’t want to accept it! Even if the parallel lines meet and I see it myself: I shall see and say that they have met, and still I won’t accept it. That is my essence, Alyosha; that is my thesis. I have told you this seriously. I deliberately began this conversation of ours as stupidly as it could be begun, but I have brought it round to my confession, because that is all you need. It wasn’t God you needed; you only needed to find out what your beloved brother lives by. And I have told you.”

Ivan ended his long tirade suddenly with a kind of special and unexpected feeling.

“And why did you begin ‘as stupidly as it could be begun’?” asked Alyosha, looking at him thoughtfully.

“Well, in the first place, if only for the Russianism of it: Russian conversations on these subjects are all conducted as stupidly as they could be. And in the second place, again: the stupider, the closer to the point. The stupider, the clearer. Stupidity is brief and artless, while intelligence wriggles and hides. Intelligence is a scoundrel, but stupidity is straightforward and honest. I have brought the matter round to my despair, and the more stupidly I have presented it, the better for me.”

“Will you explain to me why you ‘don’t accept the world’?” said Alyosha.

“Of course I will explain; it’s no secret; that is what I was leading up to. My little brother, it is not you I want to corrupt and move from your foundation; perhaps I want to heal myself by you,” Ivan smiled suddenly, quite like a meek little boy. Alyosha had never seen such a smile on him before.
