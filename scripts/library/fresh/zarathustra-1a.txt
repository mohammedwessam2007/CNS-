## First Part · Zarathustra’s Prologue

## 1

When Zarathustra was thirty years old, he left his home and the lake of his home and went into the mountains. There he enjoyed his spirit and his solitude, and for ten years did not tire of it. But at last his heart changed, and one morning he rose with the dawn, stepped before the sun, and spoke to it thus:

“You great star! What would your happiness be, if you did not have those for whom you shine?

“For ten years you have come up here to my cave: you would have grown weary of your light and of this road, without me, my eagle and my serpent.

“But we waited for you every morning, took your overflow from you, and blessed you for it.

“Look! I am weary of my wisdom, like a bee that has gathered too much honey. I need hands held out to take it.

“I want to give it away and share it out, until the wise among men are glad once more of their folly, and the poor once more of their riches.

“For that I must go down into the depths: as you do in the evening, when you go behind the sea and bring light even to the underworld, you overflowing star!

“Like you, I must *go under*, as men call it, the men to whom I want to go down.

“So bless me, then, you calm eye that can look without envy even on too great a happiness!

“Bless the cup that wants to overflow, so that the water may flow golden from it and carry the reflection of your delight everywhere!

“Look! This cup wants to be empty again, and Zarathustra wants to be a man again.”

Thus began Zarathustra’s going-under.

## 2

Zarathustra went down the mountain alone, and no one met him. But when he came into the forest, there suddenly stood before him an old man, who had left his holy hut to look for roots in the forest. And the old man spoke thus to Zarathustra:

“This wanderer is no stranger to me: many years ago he passed this way. Zarathustra he was called; but he has changed. Then you carried your ashes up the mountain: will you now carry your fire into the valleys? Are you not afraid of the punishment for arsonists?

“Yes, I recognise Zarathustra. His eye is clear, and no disgust hides around his mouth. Does he not walk like a dancer?

“Zarathustra has changed; Zarathustra has become a child; Zarathustra is awake: what do you want now among the sleepers?

“You lived in solitude as in the sea, and the sea carried you. Alas, do you want to climb ashore? Alas, do you want to drag your own body again?”

Zarathustra answered: “I love mankind.”

“Why,” said the saint, “did I go into the forest and the wilderness? Was it not because I loved mankind far too much?

“Now I love God: mankind I do not love. Man is too imperfect a thing for me. Love of man would kill me.”

Zarathustra answered: “What did I say about love! I am bringing mankind a gift.”

“Give them nothing,” said the saint. “Rather take something from them, and carry it with them: that will do them most good, if only it does you good!

“And if you want to give to them, give no more than alms, and make them beg for that!”

“No,” answered Zarathustra, “I do not give alms. I am not poor enough for that.”

The saint laughed at Zarathustra and spoke thus: “Then see that they accept your treasures! They are suspicious of hermits and do not believe that we come to give.

“Our footsteps sound too lonely to them through the streets. And as when, at night in their beds, they hear a man walking long before sunrise, they probably ask themselves: where is the thief going?

“Do not go to men; stay in the forest! Better still, go to the animals! Why will you not be as I am: a bear among bears, a bird among birds?”

“And what does the saint do in the forest?” asked Zarathustra.

The saint answered: “I make songs and sing them; and when I make songs, I laugh, weep and hum: that is how I praise God.

“With singing, weeping, laughing and humming I praise the God who is my God. But what do you bring us as a gift?”

When Zarathustra had heard these words, he bowed to the saint and said: “What would I have to give you! But let me go quickly, so that I take nothing from you!” And so they parted from each other, the old man and the man, laughing as two boys laugh.

But when Zarathustra was alone, he said to his heart: “Could it be possible! This old saint in his forest has not yet heard that *God is dead*!”

## 3

When Zarathustra came into the nearest town, which lies on the edge of the forest, he found a great crowd of people gathered in the market place: for it had been announced that a tightrope walker would perform. And Zarathustra spoke thus to the people:

“I teach you the Overman. Man is something that shall be overcome. What have you done to overcome him?

“All beings so far have created something beyond themselves: and do you want to be the ebb of this great flood, and would you rather go back to the beasts than overcome man?

“What is the ape to man? A laughing-stock or a painful shame. And man shall be just that for the Overman: a laughing-stock or a painful shame.

“You have made your way from worm to man, and much in you is still worm. Once you were apes, and even now man is more ape than any ape.

“But even the wisest among you is only a discord and a cross between plant and ghost. But do I bid you become ghosts or plants?

“Look, I teach you the Overman!

“The Overman is the meaning of the earth. Let your will say: the Overman *shall be* the meaning of the earth!

“I beg you, my brothers, *stay faithful to the earth*, and do not believe those who speak to you of hopes beyond this world! They are poisoners, whether they know it or not.

“They are despisers of life, dying and poisoned themselves, of whom the earth is weary: so let them go!

“Once, sin against God was the greatest sin; but God died, and these sinners died with him. To sin against the earth is now the most terrible thing, and to honour the entrails of the unknowable more highly than the meaning of the earth!

“Once the soul looked with contempt on the body, and then this contempt was the highest thing: it wanted the body thin, hideous and starved. So it thought to escape the body and the earth.

“Oh, this soul was itself thin, hideous and starved: and cruelty was the delight of this soul!

“But you too, my brothers, tell me: what does your body say about your soul? Is your soul not poverty and filth and a wretched contentment?

“Truly, man is a polluted stream. One must be a sea to take in a polluted stream without becoming unclean.

“Look, I teach you the Overman: he is this sea; in him your great contempt can go under.

“What is the greatest thing you can experience? It is the hour of the great contempt. The hour in which even your happiness disgusts you, and so do your reason and your virtue.

“The hour when you say: ‘What does my happiness matter! It is poverty and filth and a wretched contentment. But my happiness ought to justify existence itself!’

“The hour when you say: ‘What does my reason matter! Does it long for knowledge as the lion for its food? It is poverty and filth and a wretched contentment!’

“The hour when you say: ‘What does my virtue matter! It has not yet made me rage. How weary I am of my good and my evil! It is all poverty and filth and a wretched contentment!’

“The hour when you say: ‘What does my justice matter! I do not see that I am fire and coal. But the just man is fire and coal!’

“The hour when you say: ‘What does my pity matter! Is not pity the cross on which he is nailed who loves mankind? But my pity is no crucifixion.’

“Have you ever spoken like this? Have you ever cried out like this? Ah, if only I had heard you cry out like this!

“Not your sin: your moderation cries out to heaven; your very meanness in your sinning cries out to heaven!

“Where is the lightning that would lick you with its tongue? Where is the madness with which you should be inoculated?

“Look, I teach you the Overman: he is this lightning, he is this madness!”

When Zarathustra had spoken thus, one of the people cried out: “We have heard enough about the tightrope walker; now let us see him too!” And all the people laughed at Zarathustra. But the tightrope walker, who thought the words were meant for him, began his performance.

## 4

But Zarathustra looked at the people and wondered. Then he spoke thus:

“Man is a rope, tied between beast and Overman: a rope over an abyss.

“A dangerous crossing, a dangerous on-the-way, a dangerous looking-back, a dangerous shuddering and standing still.

“What is great in man is that he is a bridge and not a goal: what can be loved in man is that he is a *going-over* and a *going-under*.

“I love those who do not know how to live except as those who go under, for they are those who go over.

“I love the great despisers, because they are the great reverers, and arrows of longing for the other shore.

“I love those who do not first look behind the stars for a reason to go under and be sacrificed, but who sacrifice themselves to the earth, so that the earth may one day belong to the Overman.

“I love him who lives in order to know, and who wants to know so that the Overman may one day live. And so he wills his own going-under.

“I love him who works and invents so as to build the house for the Overman and to prepare earth, animal and plant for him: for so he wills his own going-under.

“I love him who loves his virtue: for virtue is the will to go under, and an arrow of longing.

“I love him who keeps back not one drop of spirit for himself, but wants to be wholly the spirit of his virtue: so he strides as spirit over the bridge.

“I love him who makes of his virtue his inclination and his destiny: so for his virtue’s sake he wants to live on, and to live no longer.

“I love him who does not want to have too many virtues. One virtue is more virtue than two, because it is more of a knot for destiny to hang on.

“I love him whose soul squanders itself, who wants no thanks and gives nothing back: for he always gives and does not want to keep himself.

“I love him who is ashamed when the dice fall in his favour, and who then asks: am I a cheat? For he wants to perish.

“I love him who throws golden words ahead of his deeds and always does more than he promises: for he wills his own going-under.

“I love him who justifies the people of the future and redeems the people of the past: for he wants to perish of the people of the present.

“I love him who chastises his God because he loves his God: for he must perish of his God’s wrath.

“I love him whose soul is deep even in its wounds, and who can perish of a small experience: so he gladly goes over the bridge.

“I love him whose soul is so overfull that he forgets himself, and all things are in him: so all things become his going-under.

“I love him who has a free spirit and a free heart: so his head is only the entrails of his heart, but his heart drives him to go under.

“I love all those who are like heavy drops falling one by one from the dark cloud that hangs over mankind: they announce that the lightning is coming, and they perish as heralds.

“Look, I am a herald of the lightning and a heavy drop from the cloud: but this lightning is called Overman.”

## 5

When Zarathustra had spoken these words, he looked at the people again and was silent. “There they stand,” he said to his heart; “there they laugh: they do not understand me; I am not the mouth for these ears.

“Must one first smash their ears, so that they learn to hear with their eyes? Must one rattle like kettledrums and preachers of repentance? Or do they believe only those who stammer?

“They have something they are proud of. What do they call it, this thing that makes them proud? They call it education; it sets them apart from goatherds.

“That is why they do not like to hear the word ‘contempt’ about themselves. So I will speak to their pride.

“So I will speak to them of the most contemptible thing: and that is the *last man*.”

And Zarathustra spoke thus to the people:

“It is time for man to set himself a goal. It is time for man to plant the seed of his highest hope.

“His soil is still rich enough for it. But one day this soil will be poor and tame, and no tall tree will be able to grow from it any more.

“Alas! The time is coming when man will no longer shoot the arrow of his longing beyond man, and the string of his bow will have forgotten how to whir!

“I tell you: one must still have chaos in oneself to give birth to a dancing star. I tell you: you still have chaos in you.

“Alas! The time is coming when man will give birth to no more stars. Alas! The time of the most contemptible man is coming, the man who can no longer despise himself.

“Look! I show you the *last man*.

“‘What is love? What is creation? What is longing? What is a star?’ So asks the last man, and blinks.

“The earth has become small then, and on it hops the last man, who makes everything small. His kind is as hard to wipe out as the flea-beetle; the last man lives longest.

“‘We have invented happiness,’ say the last men, and blink.

“They have left the places where it was hard to live: for one needs warmth. One still loves one’s neighbour and rubs against him: for one needs warmth.

“To fall ill and to be mistrustful seem sinful to them: one walks carefully. A fool, whoever still stumbles over stones or people!

“A little poison now and then: that makes for pleasant dreams. And much poison at the end, for a pleasant death.

“One still works, for work is entertainment. But one takes care that the entertainment does not tire one.

“One no longer becomes poor or rich: both are too much trouble. Who still wants to rule? Who still wants to obey? Both are too much trouble.

“No shepherd and one herd! Everyone wants the same thing; everyone is the same: whoever feels differently goes voluntarily into the madhouse.

“‘Formerly the whole world was mad,’ say the most refined, and blink.

“One is clever and knows everything that has happened: so there is no end to the mockery. One still quarrels, but one soon makes up: otherwise it upsets the stomach.

“One has one’s little pleasure for the day and one’s little pleasure for the night: but one honours health.

“‘We have invented happiness,’ say the last men, and blink.”

And here ended the first speech of Zarathustra, which is also called “the Prologue”: for at this point he was interrupted by the shouting and delight of the crowd. “Give us this last man, O Zarathustra,” they cried, “make us into these last men! Then we will make you a present of the Overman!” And all the people cheered and clicked their tongues. But Zarathustra grew sad and said to his heart:

“They do not understand me: I am not the mouth for these ears.

“I have lived too long in the mountains, it seems; I have listened too much to brooks and trees: now I speak to them as to goatherds.

“My soul is unmoved and bright as the mountains in the morning. But they think I am cold, and a mocker with terrible jokes.

“And now they look at me and laugh: and while they laugh, they hate me too. There is ice in their laughter.”
