## The Convalescent

## 1

One morning, not long after his return to the cave, Zarathustra sprang up from his bed like a madman, cried out in a terrible voice, and behaved as if someone else were still lying on the bed who would not get up from it; and Zarathustra’s voice rang out so that his animals came running in fright, and from all the caves and hiding places near Zarathustra’s cave every creature scurried away, flying, fluttering, creeping, leaping, according to the kind of foot or wing it had been given. But Zarathustra spoke these words:

Up, abysmal thought, out of my depth! I am your cock and your dawn, sleepy worm: up! up! My voice shall crow you awake!

Unfasten the fetters of your ears: listen! For I want to hear you! Up! Up! Here is thunder enough to make even graves learn to listen!

And wipe the sleep and all the dullness and blindness out of your eyes! Hear me with your eyes too: my voice is a remedy even for those born blind.

And once you are awake, you shall stay awake for ever. It is not my way to wake great-grandmothers from their sleep only to tell them to go on sleeping!

You stir, you stretch, you rattle in your throat? Up! Up! You shall not rattle, you shall speak to me! Zarathustra calls you, the godless one!

I, Zarathustra, the advocate of life, the advocate of suffering, the advocate of the circle: I call you, my most abysmal thought!

Hail to me! You are coming: I hear you! My abyss speaks; I have turned my last depth inside out into the light!

Hail to me! Come here! Give me your hand: ha! let go! Haha! Disgust, disgust, disgust: woe is me!

## 2

But hardly had Zarathustra spoken these words when he fell down like a dead man, and remained like a dead man for a long time. But when he came to himself again, he was pale and trembled and stayed lying down, and for a long time would neither eat nor drink. He was like this for seven days; but his animals did not leave him by day or night, except that the eagle flew out to fetch food. And whatever it fetched and seized it laid on Zarathustra’s bed: so that in the end Zarathustra lay among yellow and red berries, grapes, rosy apples, sweet-smelling herbs and pine cones. But at his feet lay two lambs, which the eagle had with difficulty stolen from their shepherds.

At last, after seven days, Zarathustra sat up on his bed, took a rosy apple in his hand, smelled it, and found its smell lovely. Then his animals thought the time had come to speak to him.

“O Zarathustra,” they said, “you have been lying like this for seven days now, with heavy eyes: will you not at last get on your feet again?

Step out of your cave: the world is waiting for you like a garden. The wind is playing with heavy fragrances that want to come to you; and all the streams would like to run after you.

All things long for you, since you have stayed alone for seven days. Step out of your cave! All things want to be your doctors!

Has some new knowledge come to you, a sour, heavy one? You lay like leavened dough; your soul rose and swelled over all its edges.”

“O my animals,” answered Zarathustra, “go on chattering like this and let me listen! It refreshes me so to hear you chatter: where there is chatter, the world already lies before me like a garden.

How lovely it is that there are words and sounds: are not words and sounds rainbows and illusory bridges between things eternally apart?

To each soul belongs another world; for each soul every other soul is a world behind.

It is just between the most alike that illusion lies most beautifully; for the smallest gap is the hardest to bridge.

For me, how could there be anything outside me? There is no outside! But we forget this with all sounds; how lovely it is that we forget!

Have not names and sounds been given to things so that man may refresh himself with things? It is a beautiful folly, speaking: with it man dances over all things.

How lovely is all speaking and all the lying of sounds! With sounds our love dances on bright rainbows.”

“O Zarathustra,” said the animals then, “to those who think as we do, all things dance by themselves: they come and give each other their hands and laugh and run away, and come back.

Everything goes, everything comes back; the wheel of being rolls eternally. Everything dies, everything blossoms again; the year of being runs eternally.

Everything breaks, everything is joined anew; the same house of being builds itself eternally. Everything parts, everything greets itself again; the ring of being stays eternally true to itself.

In every Now being begins; around every Here rolls the ball There. The middle is everywhere. Crooked is the path of eternity.”

“O you buffoons and barrel organs!” answered Zarathustra, and smiled again. “How well you know what had to be fulfilled in seven days:

and how that monster crawled into my throat and choked me! But I bit its head off and spat it away from me.

And you, you have already made a hurdy-gurdy song of it? But now I lie here, still tired from that biting and spitting away, still sick from my own redemption.

And you watched all this? O my animals, are you cruel too? Did you want to watch my great pain, as men do? For man is the cruellest animal.

At tragedies, bullfights and crucifixions he has so far felt best on earth; and when he invented hell for himself, look, that was his heaven on earth.

When the great man cries out, the little man runs up at once; and his tongue hangs out of his mouth with lust. But he calls it his ‘pity’.

The little man, especially the poet, how eagerly he accuses life in words! Listen to him, but do not fail to hear the delight that is in all accusing!

Such accusers of life: life overcomes them with a wink of the eye. ‘You love me?’ says the shameless one; ‘wait a little, I have no time for you yet.’

Man is the cruellest animal towards himself; and in everything that calls itself ‘sinner’ and ‘cross-bearer’ and ‘penitent’, do not fail to hear the voluptuousness that is in this lamenting and accusing!

And I myself, do I want to be man’s accuser by this? Ah, my animals, this alone I have learned so far, that man needs what is worst in him for what is best in him:

that everything worst is his best strength, and the hardest stone for the highest creator; and that man must become better and more evil:

it was not to this stake of torture that I was nailed, to know that man is evil; but I cried out as no one has yet cried out:

‘Ah, that his worst is so very small! Ah, that his best is so very small!’

The great disgust with man: that was what choked me and had crawled into my throat; and what the soothsayer prophesied: ‘All is the same, nothing is worth while, knowledge chokes.’

A long twilight limped ahead of me, a sadness tired to death, drunk with death, which spoke with a yawning mouth.

‘He returns eternally, the man you are tired of, the little man’: so my sadness yawned, and dragged its foot, and could not fall asleep.

The human earth turned into a cave for me, its breast sank in; everything living became to me human decay and bones and mouldering past.

My sighing sat on all human graves and could no longer get up; my sighing and questioning croaked and choked and gnawed and lamented by day and night:

‘Ah, man returns eternally! The little man returns eternally!’

Naked I had once seen both, the greatest man and the smallest man: all too like each other, all too human even the greatest!

All too small the greatest! That was my disgust with man! And eternal return even of the smallest! That was my disgust with all existence!

Ah, disgust! Disgust! Disgust!” So spoke Zarathustra, and sighed and shuddered; for he remembered his sickness. But then his animals would not let him go on speaking.

“Do not speak any further, you convalescent!” so his animals answered him, “but go out, where the world is waiting for you like a garden.

Go out to the roses and bees and flocks of doves! But above all to the songbirds: so that you may learn singing from them!

For singing is for convalescents; the healthy may talk. And even when the healthy want songs, they want other songs than the convalescent.”

“O you buffoons and barrel organs, be quiet!” answered Zarathustra, and smiled at his animals. “How well you know what comfort I invented for myself in seven days!

That I must sing again: that comfort I invented for myself, and this recovery: do you want to make a hurdy-gurdy song of that straight away too?”

“Do not speak any further,” his animals answered him again; “rather, you convalescent, first make yourself a lyre, a new lyre!

For look, O Zarathustra! For your new songs new lyres are needed.

Sing and overflow, O Zarathustra, heal your soul with new songs: so that you may bear your great fate, which has not yet been any man’s fate!

For your animals know well, O Zarathustra, who you are and must become: look, you are the teacher of the eternal return; that is now your fate!

That you must be the first to teach this teaching: how could this great fate not also be your greatest danger and sickness!

Look, we know what you teach: that all things return eternally, and we ourselves with them, and that we have already been here an eternal number of times, and all things with us.

You teach that there is a great year of becoming, a monster of a great year: it must, like an hourglass, turn itself over again and again, so that it may run down and run out anew:

so that all these years are the same as each other, in the greatest and in the smallest; so that we ourselves are the same in every great year, in the greatest and in the smallest.

And if you wanted to die now, O Zarathustra: look, we know too how you would speak to yourself then; but your animals beg you not to die yet!

You would speak without trembling, rather breathing a sigh of happiness: for a great heaviness and sultriness would be taken from you, you most patient one!

‘Now I die and vanish,’ you would say, ‘and in a moment I am nothing. Souls are as mortal as bodies.

But the knot of causes in which I am entangled returns: it will create me again! I myself belong to the causes of the eternal return.

I come again, with this sun, with this earth, with this eagle, with this snake: not to a new life or a better life or a similar life:

I come back eternally to this same, selfsame life, in the greatest and in the smallest, to teach again the eternal return of all things,

to speak again the word of the great noon of earth and man, to proclaim the Overman to men again.

I spoke my word, I break on my word: so my eternal lot wills it; as a proclaimer I perish!

The hour has now come when the one who goes under blesses himself. So Zarathustra’s going-under ends.’”

When the animals had spoken these words, they fell silent and waited for Zarathustra to say something to them: but Zarathustra did not hear that they were silent. Rather he lay still, with his eyes closed, like one asleep, though he was not asleep: for he was talking with his soul. But the snake and the eagle, finding him so silent, respected the great stillness around him and carefully went away.

## On the Great Longing

O my soul, I taught you to say “today” as you say “once” and “formerly”, and to dance your round-dance over every Here and There and Yonder.

O my soul, I freed you from all corners; I swept dust, spiders and twilight off you.

O my soul, I washed the little shame and the corner-virtue off you, and persuaded you to stand naked before the eyes of the sun.

With the storm that is called “spirit” I blew over your surging sea; I blew all clouds away; I even strangled the strangler that is called “sin”.

O my soul, I gave you the right to say No like the storm, and to say Yes as the open sky says Yes: still as light you stand, and now go through denying storms.

O my soul, I gave you back freedom over the created and the uncreated: and who knows, as you know, the delight of what is to come?

O my soul, I taught you the contempt that does not come like the gnawing of worms, the great, the loving contempt, which loves most where it despises most.

O my soul, I taught you to persuade so that you persuade even the grounds to come to you: like the sun, which persuades even the sea up to its height.

O my soul, I took from you all obeying, bending of the knee and saying “Lord”; I gave you yourself the name “turning of need” and “fate”.

O my soul, I gave you new names and bright playthings; I called you “fate” and “circumference of circumferences” and “umbilical cord of time” and “azure bell”.

O my soul, I gave your soil all wisdom to drink, all new wines, and also all the strong wines of wisdom, old beyond memory.

O my soul, I poured every sun on you, and every night and every silence and every longing: then you grew up for me like a vine.

O my soul, over-rich and heavy you stand there now, a vine with swelling udders and crowded brown golden grapes:

crowded and pressed by your happiness, waiting in your overflowing, and still bashful at your waiting.

O my soul, there is now nowhere a soul that would be more loving and more embracing and more comprehensive! Where would future and past be closer together than in you?

O my soul, I have given you everything, and all my hands have become empty for you: and now! Now you say to me, smiling and full of melancholy: “Which of us has to give thanks?

Has not the giver to give thanks that the receiver received? Is giving not a need? Is receiving not mercy?”

O my soul, I understand the smile of your melancholy: your over-richness itself now stretches out longing hands!

Your fullness looks out over roaring seas and searches and waits; the longing of over-fullness looks out of the smiling sky of your eyes!

And truly, O my soul! Who could see your smile and not melt into tears? The angels themselves melt into tears at the over-goodness of your smile.

It is your goodness and over-goodness that does not want to lament and weep: and yet, O my soul, your smile longs for tears, and your trembling mouth for sobs.

“Is not all weeping a lamenting? And all lamenting an accusing?” So you say to yourself, and that is why, O my soul, you would rather smile than pour out your sorrow:

pour out in streaming tears all your sorrow over your fullness, and over all the vine’s urgent longing for the vintner and the vintner’s knife!

But if you will not weep, nor weep out your purple melancholy, then you will have to sing, O my soul! Look, I myself smile, who foretell this to you:

sing with a roaring song, until all seas grow still to listen to your longing,

until over still, longing seas the boat floats, the golden wonder, around whose gold all good, bad, wonderful things leap:

and many great and small animals too, and everything that has light, wonderful feet, so that it can run on violet-blue paths,

towards the golden wonder, the boat that comes of its own will, and towards its master: but that is the vintner, who waits with a diamond knife,

your great deliverer, O my soul, the nameless one, for whom only future songs will find names! And truly, your breath already smells of future songs;

already you glow and dream, already you drink thirstily at all deep, resounding wells of comfort, already your melancholy rests in the bliss of future songs!

O my soul, now I have given you everything, and even my last, and all my hands have become empty for you: that I bade you sing, look, that was my last!

That I bade you sing, speak now, speak: which of us now has to give thanks? But better still: sing to me, sing, O my soul! And let me give thanks!

Thus spoke Zarathustra.

## The Other Dance Song

## 1

“Into your eye I looked lately, O life: I saw gold glinting in your night-eye; my heart stood still with delight:

I saw a golden boat glinting on dark waters, a sinking, drinking, again beckoning golden rocking-boat!

At my foot, mad for dancing, you threw a glance, a laughing, questioning, melting rocking-glance:

only twice you shook your rattle with your little hands, and already my foot was rocking with dance-madness.

My heels reared up, my toes listened to understand you: for the dancer carries his ear in his toes!

I leapt towards you: then you fled back from my leap; and the tongue of your fleeing, flying hair licked towards me!

I leapt away from you and from your snakes: then you stood already half turned, your eye full of longing.

With crooked glances you teach me crooked paths; on crooked paths my foot learns tricks!

I fear you near, I love you far; your fleeing lures me, your seeking stops me: I suffer, but what would I not gladly have suffered for you!

You whose coldness kindles, whose hatred seduces, whose flight binds, whose mockery moves:

who would not hate you, you great binder, entwiner, temptress, seeker, finder! Who would not love you, you innocent, impatient, wind-swift, child-eyed sinner!

Where are you pulling me now, you paragon and tomboy? And now you flee me again, you sweet wild thing and ingrate!

I dance after you, I follow you even on a faint trail. Where are you? Give me your hand! Or just one finger!

Here are caves and thickets: we shall lose our way! Stop! Stand still! Do you not see owls and bats whirring?

You owl! You bat! You want to make a fool of me? Where are we? It was from the dogs that you learned this howling and yapping.

You bare your little white teeth at me sweetly; your wicked eyes spring at me out of your curly little mane!

This is a dance over stick and stone: I am the hunter; will you be my dog or my chamois?

Now beside me! And quickly, you wicked jumper! Now up! And over! Alas! I fell myself as I jumped!

Oh, see me lying there, you high spirits, and begging for mercy! I would gladly walk lovelier paths with you!

the paths of love through quiet bright bushes! Or there along the lake: there goldfish swim and dance!

You are tired now? Over there are sheep and sunsets: is it not lovely to sleep when shepherds play the flute?

You are so terribly tired? I will carry you there; just let your arms sink! And if you are thirsty, I might have something, but your mouth will not drink it!

Oh, this cursed, nimble, supple snake and slippery witch! Where have you gone? But on my face I feel from your hand two spots and red blotches!

I am truly tired of always being your sheepish shepherd! You witch, until now I have sung to you; now you shall scream for me!

To the beat of my whip you shall dance and scream for me! I did not forget the whip, did I? No!”

## 2

Then life answered me thus, and held her delicate ears as she did so:

“O Zarathustra! Do not crack your whip so terribly! You know very well: noise murders thoughts, and just now such tender thoughts are coming to me.

We are both two real good-for-nothings and do-no-harms. Beyond good and evil we found our island and our green meadow, we two alone! So we must be good to each other!

And even if we do not love each other from the bottom of our hearts, must one bear a grudge because one does not love from the bottom of the heart?

And that I am fond of you, and often too fond, you know: and the reason is that I am jealous of your wisdom. Ah, this mad old fool of a wisdom!

If your wisdom ever ran away from you, ah! then my love would quickly run away from you too.”

Then life looked thoughtfully behind her and around her and said softly: “O Zarathustra, you are not faithful enough to me!

You do not love me nearly as much as you say; I know you are thinking of leaving me soon.

There is an old, heavy, heavy booming bell: it booms at night up to your cave:

when you hear this bell strike the hour at midnight, then between one and twelve you think of it,

you think of it, O Zarathustra, I know, that you will soon leave me!”

“Yes,” I answered hesitantly, “but you know this too...” And I said something into her ear, right in among her tangled, yellow, foolish tresses.

“You know that, O Zarathustra? No one knows that.”

And we looked at each other, and looked at the green meadow over which the cool evening was just running, and wept together. But then life was dearer to me than all my wisdom had ever been.

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
