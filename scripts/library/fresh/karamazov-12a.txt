## Book Five, Chapter V · The Grand Inquisitor

“You see, even here I can’t do without a preface, a literary preface, I mean. Damn it!” Ivan laughed. “And what kind of author am I? Look: my story takes place in the sixteenth century, and back then (you must remember this from school) it was the custom in poetry to bring the powers of heaven down to earth. I won’t even mention Dante. In France the law clerks, and the monks in the monasteries too, used to put on whole performances with the Madonna on stage, and angels, and saints, and Christ, and God himself. All of it was very naive in those days. In Victor Hugo’s Notre-Dame de Paris, in the Paris of Louis XI, to celebrate the birth of the Dauphin, an edifying play is put on for the people, free of charge, in the great hall of the town hall, called The Good Judgement of the Most Holy and Gracious Virgin Mary, and she appears in person and pronounces her good judgement. Here in Moscow, in the old days before Peter the Great, almost the same sort of plays were put on now and then, especially from the Old Testament. And besides plays, all sorts of tales and verses went round the world in which saints, angels and all the powers of heaven took part as they were needed. In our monasteries too they translated and copied such poems, and even wrote them, and when? Under the Tatars.

“There is one little monastery poem, for instance (from the Greek, of course), called The Descent of the Mother of God into Hell, with scenes as bold as anything in Dante. The Mother of God visits hell, and the Archangel Michael is her guide through the torments. She sees the sinners and their sufferings. Among them, by the way, there is one most remarkable class of sinners in a burning lake: those who sink so deep in the lake that they can never swim up again, ‘these God forgets’, an expression of extraordinary depth and power. And the Mother of God, shaken and weeping, falls before the throne of God and begs mercy for everyone in hell, everyone she has seen there, without distinction. Her conversation with God is tremendously interesting. She pleads, she will not go away, and when God points to the nail-pierced hands and feet of her Son and asks, ‘How can I forgive his torturers?’, she commands all the saints, all the martyrs, all the angels and archangels to fall down with her and pray for mercy for all, without distinction. In the end she wins from God a respite from torment every year, from Good Friday to Trinity Sunday, and the sinners in hell at once thank the Lord and cry out to him: ‘Thou art just, O Lord, in thy judgement.’

“Well, my little poem would have been of that kind if it had appeared in those days. He appears on my stage. True, he says nothing in the poem; he only appears and passes by. Fifteen centuries have gone by since he promised to come in his kingdom, fifteen centuries since his prophet wrote, ‘Behold, I come quickly.’ ‘But of that day and that hour knoweth no man, not even the Son, but the Father only,’ as he said himself while still on earth. But humanity awaits him with the same faith as before, and the same tenderness. Oh, with even greater faith, for fifteen centuries have passed since heaven stopped giving pledges to man:

> Believe what the heart tells you;
> heaven gives no pledges.

— Schiller, ‘Longing’ (as Ivan quotes it)

“Nothing but faith in what the heart has said! True, there were many miracles then. There were saints who worked miraculous cures; to some righteous men, so their Lives say, the Queen of Heaven came down in person. But the devil does not sleep, and doubt had already begun among men about the truth of these miracles. Just then, in the north, in Germany, a terrible new heresy appeared. A great star, ‘burning as it were a lamp’ (that is, a church), ‘fell upon the fountains of waters, and they were made bitter’. These heresies blasphemously denied miracles. But those who remained faithful believed all the more ardently. The tears of humanity rose up to him as before; people waited for him, loved him, hoped in him, longed to suffer and die for him as before. So many centuries had humanity prayed with faith and fire, ‘O Lord God, appear to us’, so many centuries had it called to him, that in his immeasurable compassion he resolved to come down to those who prayed. He had come down before, he had visited some of the righteous, the martyrs and holy hermits while they were still on earth, as is written in their Lives. Our own Tyutchev, who believed deeply in the truth of his words, proclaimed that

> Bowed beneath the burden of the cross,
> through all of you, my native land,
> in the form of a servant, the King of Heaven
> has walked, blessing as he went.

— Tyutchev, ‘These Poor Villages’

“And I tell you that this was certainly so. And now he wished to appear, even for a moment, to the people: to the tormented, suffering, foully sinful people, who loved him like children. My story takes place in Spain, in Seville, at the most terrible time of the Inquisition, when fires were lit every day throughout the land to the glory of God, and

> in splendid acts of faith
> they burned the wicked heretics.

— Polezhaev

“Oh, of course, this was not the coming in which he will appear, as he promised, at the end of time in all his heavenly glory, the coming that will be sudden, ‘as the lightning cometh out of the east, and shineth even unto the west’. No, he wanted only to visit his children for a moment, and precisely there, where the fires of the heretics had just been crackling. In his boundless mercy he walks once more among men, in the same human form in which he walked among them for three years fifteen centuries before. He comes down into the hot squares of the southern city, in which, only the day before, in a ‘magnificent auto-da-fé’, in the presence of the king, the court, the knights, the cardinals and the loveliest ladies of the court, before the whole population of Seville, the Cardinal Grand Inquisitor had burned almost a hundred heretics at once, ad majorem gloriam Dei, for the greater glory of God.

“He appeared quietly, unnoticed, and yet (this is the strange thing) everyone recognises him. That could be one of the best passages in the poem: why exactly they recognise him. The people are drawn to him by an irresistible force, they surround him, they grow into a crowd round him, they follow him. He walks among them in silence with a quiet smile of infinite compassion. The sun of love burns in his heart, rays of Light, Enlightenment and Power stream from his eyes and, pouring over the people, shake their hearts with answering love. He stretches out his hands to them, he blesses them, and from touching him, even only his clothes, there comes a healing power. Here an old man, blind from childhood, cries out from the crowd, ‘Lord, heal me, so that I too may see you,’ and something like scales falls from his eyes, and the blind man sees him. The people weep and kiss the ground where he walks. Children throw flowers in front of him, sing and cry out to him, ‘Hosanna!’ ‘It is he, it is he himself,’ they all repeat. ‘It must be he, it can be no one but he.’

“He stops on the steps of the cathedral of Seville at the very moment when a small open white coffin is being carried into the church, with weeping. In it lies a girl of seven, the only daughter of a prominent citizen. The dead child lies covered in flowers. ‘He will raise your child,’ people shout from the crowd to the weeping mother. The cathedral priest who has come out to meet the coffin looks on in bewilderment and frowns. But now the mother of the dead child cries out. She throws herself at his feet. ‘If it is you, raise my child!’ she cries, stretching out her hands to him. The procession stops; the little coffin is set down on the steps at his feet. He looks with compassion, and his lips once more softly say, ‘Talitha cumi’, ‘and the damsel arose’. The girl rises in the coffin, sits up and looks round, smiling, with wide, astonished eyes. In her hands is the bunch of white roses she lay with in the coffin.

“There is confusion among the people, shouting, sobbing, and at that very moment the Cardinal Grand Inquisitor himself crosses the square in front of the cathedral. He is an old man of nearly ninety, tall and straight, with a withered face and sunken eyes, in which there is still a gleam like a spark of fire. Oh, he is not in his splendid cardinal’s robes, the ones he paraded in yesterday before the people when the enemies of the Roman faith were burned; no, at this moment he wears only his old, coarse monk’s cassock. At a certain distance behind him follow his grim assistants and servants and the ‘holy’ guard. He stops in front of the crowd and watches from a distance. He has seen everything: he has seen them set the coffin down at his feet, he has seen the girl rise, and his face has darkened. He knits his thick grey brows, and his eyes flash with an ominous fire. He stretches out his finger and orders the guards to seize him. And such is his power, so well trained, submissive and tremblingly obedient are the people to him, that the crowd at once parts before the guards, and they, amid the deathly silence that has suddenly fallen, lay hands on him and lead him away. At once the whole crowd, as one man, bows to the ground before the old Inquisitor; he blesses the people in silence and passes on. The guards bring the prisoner to a cramped, gloomy, vaulted prison in the ancient building of the Holy Office and lock him in.

“The day passes, and the dark, hot, ‘breathless’ night of Seville comes on. The air ‘smells of laurel and lemon’. In the deep darkness the iron door of the prison suddenly opens, and the old Grand Inquisitor himself slowly comes in with a lamp in his hand. He is alone; the door is locked behind him at once. He stops at the entrance and looks into his face for a long time, a minute or two. At last he quietly comes closer, sets the lamp on the table and says to him:

“‘Is it you? You?’ But, getting no answer, he quickly adds: ‘Don’t answer. Be silent. And what could you say? I know too well what you would say. And you have no right to add anything to what you said before. Why have you come to get in our way? For you have come to get in our way, and you know it. But do you know what will happen tomorrow? I do not know who you are, and I do not want to know, whether you are he or only his likeness; but tomorrow I will condemn you and burn you at the stake as the worst of heretics, and the very people who kissed your feet today will rush, at a single sign from me, to rake up the coals for your fire. Do you know that? Yes, perhaps you do know it,’ he added, lost in thought, not taking his eyes off his prisoner for a moment.”

“I don’t quite understand, Ivan, what this is,” said Alyosha, who had listened all this time in silence, smiling. “Is it just wild fantasy, or some mistake on the old man’s part, some impossible mix-up?”

“Take it as the second, if you like,” laughed Ivan, “if modern realism has spoiled you so much that you can’t stand anything fantastic. You want a mix-up: let it be a mix-up. It’s true,” he laughed again, “the old man is ninety, and he could long ago have gone mad over his idea. The prisoner might have struck him by his appearance. It might simply be delirium, the vision of an old man of ninety before his death, inflamed by the auto-da-fé of the day before, with its hundred burnt heretics. But isn’t it all the same to you and me whether it’s a mix-up or wild fantasy? The point is only that the old man has to speak out, that at last, after ninety years, he speaks out and says aloud what he has kept silent about for ninety years.”

“And the prisoner is silent too? He looks at him and doesn’t say a word?”

“That’s how it has to be, in any case,” laughed Ivan again. “The old man himself tells him that he has no right to add anything to what he said before. If you like, that is the most basic feature of Roman Catholicism, in my opinion at least: ‘Everything,’ they say, ‘was handed over by you to the Pope, so everything is now in the Pope’s hands, and you might as well not come at all now, or at least don’t get in the way for the time being.’ They don’t only say it, they write it, the Jesuits at any rate. I have read it in their theologians myself.

“‘Have you the right to reveal to us even one of the mysteries of the world you have come from?’ my old man asks him, and answers for him: ‘No, you have not; so as not to add to what was said before, and so as not to take away from people the freedom you stood up for so much when you were on earth. Everything you might reveal now would encroach on people’s freedom of faith, because it would come as a miracle, and the freedom of their faith was dearer to you than anything, even then, fifteen hundred years ago. Wasn’t it you who said so often then, “I will make you free”? Well, now you have seen these “free” men,’ the old man adds suddenly with a thoughtful smile. ‘Yes, this business has cost us dear,’ he goes on, looking at him sternly, ‘but we have finished it at last, in your name. For fifteen centuries we have been tormented by this freedom, but now it is over, and over for good. You don’t believe that it is over for good? You look at me meekly and do not even deign to be angry with me? But know that now, today, these people are more certain than ever before that they are completely free, and yet they themselves brought us their freedom and laid it humbly at our feet. But it was we who did that; and is that what you wanted, that kind of freedom?’”

“Again I don’t understand,” Alyosha broke in. “Is he being ironic? Is he laughing?”

“Not in the least. He is counting it as a merit, for himself and his kind, that at last they have overcome freedom and have done it in order to make people happy. ‘For only now’ (he is talking about the Inquisition, of course) ‘has it become possible to think for the first time of people’s happiness. Man was made a rebel; can rebels be happy? You were warned,’ he says to him. ‘You had no lack of warnings and signs, but you did not listen to the warnings; you rejected the only way by which people could be made happy. But, fortunately, when you left, you handed the work over to us. You promised, you confirmed it with your word, you gave us the right to bind and to loose, and of course you cannot even think of taking that right away from us now. Why, then, have you come to get in our way?’”

“And what does it mean, ‘no lack of warnings and signs’?” asked Alyosha.

“That is exactly the main thing the old man has to say.
