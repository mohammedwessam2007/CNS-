## The Soothsayer

“...and I saw a great sadness come over mankind. The best grew tired of their works.

A teaching went out, and a faith ran beside it: ‘All is empty, all is the same, all has been!’

And from all the hills it echoed: ‘All is empty, all is the same, all has been!’

We have harvested, it is true: but why did all our fruits turn rotten and brown? What fell down from the evil moon last night?

All our work was in vain; our wine has turned to poison; an evil eye has scorched our fields and hearts yellow.

We have all grown dry; and if fire falls on us, we fly up like ashes: yes, we have made even fire tired.

All our wells have dried up, even the sea has drawn back. All the ground wants to split open, but the depths will not swallow!

‘Ah, where is there still a sea in which one could drown?’ So our lament sounds, out over flat swamps.

Truly, we have grown too tired even to die; now we stay awake and live on, in burial vaults!”

So Zarathustra heard a soothsayer speak; and his prophecy went to his heart and changed him. He went about sad and tired; and he became like those of whom the soothsayer had spoken.

“Truly,” he said to his disciples, “it will not be long before this long twilight comes. Ah, how shall I save my light through it!

Let it not suffocate in this sadness! It is meant to be a light for more distant worlds, and for the most distant nights!”

So, troubled in his heart, Zarathustra went about; and for three days he took no food or drink, had no rest, and lost his speech. At last it happened that he fell into a deep sleep. But his disciples sat around him in long night watches, and waited anxiously to see whether he would wake and speak again and be cured of his gloom.

And this is the speech Zarathustra made when he woke; but his voice came to his disciples as if from far away.

“Hear the dream I dreamed, my friends, and help me guess its meaning!

It is still a riddle to me, this dream; its meaning is hidden in it and imprisoned, and does not yet fly above it on free wings.

I had renounced all life, so I dreamed. I had become a night-watchman and a guardian of graves, there in the lonely mountain castle of death.

Up there I guarded his coffins: the musty vaults stood full of these signs of victory. Life that had been overcome looked out at me from glass coffins.

I breathed the smell of dusty eternities: my soul lay sultry and dusty. And who could have aired his soul there!

The brightness of midnight was always around me, loneliness crouched beside it; and, as the third, a death-rattling silence, the worst of my women friends.

I carried keys, the rustiest of all keys; and I knew how to open the creakiest of all gates with them.

Like a bitter, evil croaking the sound ran through the long corridors when the wings of the gate were raised: this bird cried out unkindly; it did not like being woken.

But it was more fearful still, and more heart-gripping, when it fell silent again and everything around was still, and I sat alone in that treacherous silence.

So time went by me and crept, if there still was time: what do I know of it! But at last that happened which woke me.

Three times blows struck the gate, like thunder; the vaults echoed and howled three times: then I went to the gate.

‘Alpa!’ I cried, ‘who is carrying his ashes up the mountain? Alpa! Alpa! Who is carrying his ashes up the mountain?’

And I pressed the key and lifted at the gate and struggled. But it was not yet open a finger’s breadth:

then a roaring wind tore its wings apart: whistling, shrieking and cutting, it threw a black coffin towards me.

And in the roaring and whistling and shrieking the coffin burst open and spat out a thousandfold laughter.

And out of a thousand grimaces of children, angels, owls, fools and child-sized butterflies it laughed and mocked and roared at me.

I was horribly frightened by it: it threw me to the ground. And I screamed with horror as I had never screamed.

But my own scream woke me: and I came to myself.”

So Zarathustra told his dream, and then fell silent: for he did not yet know the meaning of his dream. But the disciple he loved most rose quickly, took Zarathustra’s hand and said:

“Your life itself explains this dream to us, O Zarathustra!

Are you not yourself the wind with its shrill whistling that tears open the gates of the castles of death?

Are you not yourself the coffin full of life’s bright malice and angel-grimaces?

Truly, like a thousandfold children’s laughter Zarathustra comes into all the death chambers, laughing at these night-watchmen and guardians of graves, and whoever else rattles gloomy keys.

You will frighten them and knock them over with your laughter; fainting and waking will prove your power over them.

And even when the long twilight comes, and the weariness of death, you will not set in our sky, you advocate of life!

You have let us see new stars and new splendours of the night; truly, you have spread laughter itself over us like a bright tent.

Now children’s laughter will always pour out of coffins; now a strong wind will always come victorious over all weariness of death: of this you yourself are our guarantee and our soothsayer!

Truly, it was they yourself you dreamed, your enemies: that was your hardest dream!

But as you woke from them and came to yourself, so they shall wake from themselves, and come to you!”

So spoke the disciple; and all the others now crowded around Zarathustra and took him by the hands and tried to persuade him to leave his bed and his sadness and come back to them. But Zarathustra sat upright on his couch, with a strange look. Like one coming home from a long time abroad, he looked at his disciples and searched their faces; and still he did not recognise them. But when they lifted him and set him on his feet, look, all at once his eye changed; he understood everything that had happened, stroked his beard, and said in a strong voice:

“Well then! This has had its time; but see to it, my disciples, that we have a good meal, and soon! So I mean to do penance for bad dreams!

But the soothsayer shall eat and drink at my side: and truly, I will yet show him a sea in which he can drown!”

Thus spoke Zarathustra. But then he looked for a long time into the face of the disciple who had played the interpreter of dreams, and shook his head.

## On Redemption

One day, as Zarathustra was crossing the great bridge, the cripples and beggars surrounded him, and a hunchback spoke to him thus:

“Look, Zarathustra! The people too learn from you and are coming to believe in your teaching: but for them to believe you completely, one thing more is needed: you must first convince us cripples too! Here you have a fine selection, and truly, a chance with more than one forelock to seize it by! You can heal the blind and make the lame walk; and from the one who has too much behind him you could surely take a little away too: that, I think, would be the right way to make the cripples believe in Zarathustra!”

But Zarathustra answered the one who spoke thus: “If you take away the hunchback’s hump, you take away his spirit: so the people teach. And if you give the blind man his eyes, he sees too many bad things on earth, so that he curses the one who healed him. But whoever makes the lame man walk does him the greatest harm: for hardly can he walk when his vices run away with him: so the people teach about cripples. And why should Zarathustra not learn from the people too, if the people learn from Zarathustra?

But it is the least thing to me, since I have been among men, to see: ‘This one lacks an eye, and that one an ear, and a third a leg, and there are others who have lost their tongue or their nose or their head.’

I see, and have seen, worse things, and many so abominable that I would not like to speak of each of them, and of some I would not like even to keep silent: namely, people who lack everything except that they have too much of one thing; people who are nothing more than a big eye, or a big mouth, or a big belly, or something else big: I call such people inverse cripples.

And when I came out of my solitude and crossed this bridge for the first time, I did not trust my eyes, and looked, and looked again, and said at last: ‘That is an ear! An ear as big as a man!’ I looked still more closely: and indeed, under the ear something else was moving, something pitifully small and poor and thin. And truly, the enormous ear sat on a little thin stalk; but the stalk was a man! Anyone who put a glass to his eye could even make out a little envious face; and also that a bloated little soul dangled from the stalk. But the people told me that the big ear was not only a man, but a great man, a genius. But I never believed the people when they spoke of great men; and I kept my belief that it was an inverse cripple, who had too little of everything and too much of one thing.”

When Zarathustra had spoken thus to the hunchback, and to those whose mouthpiece and advocate he was, he turned to his disciples with deep displeasure and said:

“Truly, my friends, I walk among men as among the fragments and limbs of men!

This is the terrible thing for my eye, that I find man broken into pieces and scattered, as over a battlefield and a butcher’s field.

And if my eye flees from the now to the past, it always finds the same: fragments and limbs and dreadful accidents, but no men!

The now and the past on earth, ah, my friends, that is what I find most unbearable; and I would not know how to live if I were not also a seer of what must come.

A seer, one who wills, a creator, a future himself and a bridge to the future; and ah, also, as it were, a cripple on this bridge: all this is Zarathustra.

And you too have often asked yourselves: ‘Who is Zarathustra to us? What shall we call him?’ And, like me, you gave yourselves questions for answers.

Is he one who promises? Or one who fulfils? A conqueror? Or an heir? An autumn? Or a ploughshare? A doctor? Or one who has been cured?

Is he a poet? Or one who is truthful? A liberator? Or a tamer? A good man? Or an evil one?

I walk among men as among the fragments of the future: that future which I see.

And this is all my poetry and striving: to compose into one and bring together what is fragment and riddle and dreadful accident.

And how could I bear to be a man, if man were not also a poet and a guesser of riddles and the redeemer of chance!

To redeem those who are past, and to transform every ‘It was’ into ‘Thus I willed it!’: that alone I would call redemption!

Will: that is the name of the liberator and the bringer of joy; so I have taught you, my friends! But now learn this as well: the will itself is still a prisoner.

Willing frees: but what is the name of that which puts even the liberator in chains?

‘It was’: that is the name of the will’s gnashing of teeth and its loneliest misery. Powerless against what has been done, it is an angry spectator of everything past.

The will cannot will backwards; that it cannot break time and time’s craving: that is the will’s loneliest misery.

Willing frees: what does willing itself devise to get free of its misery and mock its prison?

Ah, every prisoner becomes a fool! The imprisoned will, too, redeems itself foolishly.

That time does not run backwards: that is its fury; ‘That which was’: that is the name of the stone it cannot roll.

And so it rolls stones out of fury and ill-humour, and takes revenge on whatever does not feel fury and ill-humour as it does.

So the will, the liberator, became a doer of harm: and on everything that can suffer it takes revenge for not being able to go back.

This, yes, this alone is revenge itself: the will’s ill will against time and its ‘It was’.

Truly, a great folly lives in our will; and it became a curse to everything human that this folly learned to think!

The spirit of revenge: my friends, that has so far been mankind’s best reflection; and where there was suffering, there was always to be punishment.

For ‘punishment’ is what revenge calls itself: with a lying word it pretends to have a good conscience.

And because there is suffering in the one who wills, since it cannot will backwards, willing itself and all life were to be punishment!

And now cloud after cloud rolled over the spirit, until at last madness preached: ‘Everything passes away, therefore everything deserves to pass away!’

‘And this itself is justice, that law of time that it must devour its children’: so madness preached.

‘Things are ordered morally, according to justice and punishment. Oh, where is redemption from the flux of things and from the punishment called existence?’ So madness preached.

‘Can there be redemption if there is eternal justice? Ah, the stone It was cannot be rolled: all punishments must be eternal too!’ So madness preached.

‘No deed can be annihilated: how could it be undone by punishment! This, this is what is eternal in the punishment called existence, that existence must also eternally be deed and guilt again!

Unless the will at last redeemed itself, and willing became not-willing’: but you know, my brothers, this fable-song of madness!

I led you away from these fable-songs when I taught you: ‘The will is a creator.’

Every ‘It was’ is a fragment, a riddle, a dreadful accident, until the creating will says to it: ‘But thus I willed it!’

Until the creating will says to it: ‘But thus I will it! Thus shall I will it!’

But has it ever spoken so? And when does this happen? Has the will been unharnessed from its own folly yet?

Has the will become its own redeemer and bringer of joy yet? Has it unlearned the spirit of revenge and all gnashing of teeth?

And who has taught it reconciliation with time, and something higher than all reconciliation?

The will that is the will to power must will something higher than any reconciliation: but how does this happen to it? Who has taught it to will backwards too?”

But at this point in his speech it happened that Zarathustra suddenly stopped, and looked exactly like someone who has been terribly frightened. With frightened eyes he looked at his disciples; his eye pierced their thoughts and their thoughts behind their thoughts as if with arrows. But after a little while he was laughing again, and said, calmed:

“It is hard to live with people, because keeping silent is so hard. Especially for a chatterbox.”

Thus spoke Zarathustra. But the hunchback had listened to the conversation and covered his face as he did so; but when he heard Zarathustra laugh, he looked up curiously and said slowly:

“But why does Zarathustra speak differently to us than to his disciples?”

Zarathustra answered: “What is surprising about that! With hunchbacks one may speak in a hunchbacked way!”

“Good,” said the hunchback; “and with pupils one may tell tales out of school.

But why does Zarathustra speak differently to his pupils than to himself?”

## On Human Prudence

Not the height: the slope is what is terrible!

The slope, where the gaze plunges down and the hand reaches up. There the heart grows dizzy before its double will.

Ah, friends, can you guess my heart’s double will too?

This, this is my slope and my danger: that my gaze plunges into the height, and that my hand would like to hold on and support itself on the depth!

My will clings to man; with chains I bind myself to man, because I am torn upward to the Overman: for that is where my other will wants to go.

And that is why I live blind among men, as if I did not know them: so that my hand may not entirely lose its faith in something firm.

I do not know you men: this darkness and consolation is often spread around me.

I sit at the gateway for every rogue, and ask: who wants to deceive me?

This is my first human prudence: that I let myself be deceived, so as not to be on guard against deceivers.

Ah, if I were on guard against man, how could man be an anchor for my balloon! Too easily I would be torn up and away!

This providence is over my fate: that I must be without caution.

And whoever does not want to die of thirst among men must learn to drink out of all glasses; and whoever wants to stay clean among men must know how to wash even in dirty water.

And so I often said to comfort myself: “Well then! Up! Old heart! A misfortune failed to happen to you: enjoy this as your good fortune!”

But this is my other human prudence: I spare the vain more than the proud.

Is not wounded vanity the mother of all tragedies? But where pride is wounded, something better than pride may well grow.

For life to be good to watch, its play must be well played: but for that one needs good actors.

I have found all the vain to be good actors: they act and want people to like watching them; all their spirit is in this will.

They perform themselves, they invent themselves; near them I love to watch life: it cures one of melancholy.

That is why I spare the vain: they are doctors for my melancholy, and they keep me attached to man as to a play.

And then: who can measure the whole depth of the vain man’s modesty! I am fond of him, and pity him for his modesty.

It is from you that he wants to learn his belief in himself; he feeds on your glances, he eats praise out of your hands.

He even believes your lies when you lie well about him: for in his deepest depths his heart sighs: “What am I?”

And if that is the true virtue, which does not know about itself: well, the vain man does not know about his modesty!

But this is my third human prudence: that I do not let your fearfulness spoil for me the sight of the wicked.

I am happy to see the wonders that the hot sun hatches: tigers and palms and rattlesnakes.

Among men too there is a beautiful brood of the hot sun, and much that is wonderful in the wicked.

Indeed, just as your wisest did not seem to me so very wise, so I found men’s wickedness, too, beneath its reputation.

And often I asked, shaking my head: why still rattle, you rattlesnakes?

Truly, there is still a future even for evil! And the hottest south has not yet been discovered for man.

How many things are already called the worst wickedness that are only twelve feet wide and three months long! But one day greater dragons will come into the world.

For, so that the Overman may not lack his dragon, the over-dragon that is worthy of him, much hot sun must still glow on damp jungle!

Your wildcats must first have become tigers, and your poisonous toads crocodiles: for the good hunter shall have a good hunt!

And truly, you good and just! There is much to laugh at in you, and especially your fear of what has so far been called “devil”!

So strange are you in your souls to what is great that the Overman would be terrible to you in his goodness!

And you wise and knowing ones, you would flee from the burning sun of wisdom in which the Overman joyfully bathes his nakedness!

You highest men my eye has met! This is my doubt about you and my secret laughter: I guess you would call my Overman a devil!

Ah, I grew tired of these highest and best: out of their “height” I longed to go up, out, away to the Overman!

A horror came over me when I saw these best ones naked: then wings grew on me, to soar away into distant futures.

Into more distant futures, into more southern souths than any artist ever dreamed: there, where gods are ashamed of all clothes!

But I want to see you in disguise, you neighbours and fellow men, and well dressed, and vain, and dignified, as “the good and the just”;

and I myself want to sit among you in disguise, so that I mistake you and myself: for that is my last human prudence.

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
