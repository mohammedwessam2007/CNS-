## Chapter XXIV

“Yes, there is something repulsive about me, something that pushes people away,” thought Levin, as he left the Shcherbatskys’ and set off on foot to his brother’s. “And I am no good for other people. Pride, they say. No, I haven’t even any pride. If I had pride, I would not have put myself in such a position.” And he pictured to himself Vronsky, happy, kind, clever and calm, who had surely never been in the terrible position he had been in that evening. “Yes, she was bound to choose him. That is how it must be, and I have no one to complain of, and nothing to complain about. I am to blame myself. What right had I to think that she would want to join her life to mine? Who am I? And what am I? A nonentity, needed by no one and for nothing.” And he remembered his brother Nikolai and dwelt on the memory with joy. “Isn’t he right that everything in the world is bad and vile? And I doubt that we judge, or have judged, brother Nikolai fairly. Of course, from the point of view of Prokofy, who saw him in a torn fur coat and drunk, he is a contemptible man; but I know him differently. I know his soul, and I know that he and I are alike. And instead of going to find him, I went out to dine, and came here.” Levin went up to a street-lamp, read his brother’s address, which he had in his pocket-book, and called a cab. All the long way to his brother’s Levin vividly recalled all the events of brother Nikolai’s life that he knew. He remembered how his brother, at the university and for a year after the university, in spite of his comrades’ mockery, had lived like a monk, strictly observing all the rites of religion, the services, the fasts, and avoiding every pleasure, especially women; and then how he had suddenly broken loose, taken up with the vilest people and plunged into the most dissolute debauchery. He remembered then the business of the boy whom he had taken from the country to bring up, and, in a fit of fury, beaten so badly that proceedings were started against him for causing injury. He remembered then the business of the card-sharper to whom he had lost money, given a promissory note, and against whom he had himself brought a complaint, trying to prove that the man had cheated him. (That was the money Sergei Ivanych had paid.) Then he remembered how he had spent a night in the police station for disorderly conduct. He remembered the shameful lawsuit he had started against his brother Sergei Ivanych, for supposedly not having paid him his share of their mother’s estate; and the last affair, when he had gone to take up a post in the Western Region and had been brought to trial there for beating a village elder… It was all terribly vile, but to Levin it did not seem nearly as vile as it must have seemed to those who did not know Nikolai Levin, did not know his whole story, did not know his heart.

Levin remembered how, at the time when Nikolai was in his period of piety, of fasts, monks and church services, when he was seeking in religion a help, a bridle for his passionate nature, not only had no one supported him, but everyone, Levin himself included, had laughed at him. They had teased him, called him Noah, the monk; and when he broke loose, no one had helped him, but everyone had turned away with horror and disgust.

Levin felt that his brother Nikolai, in his soul, in the very foundation of his soul, in spite of all the ugliness of his life, was no more in the wrong than the people who despised him. He was not to blame for having been born with his unrestrainable character and a mind somehow cramped. But he had always wanted to be good. “I will tell him everything, I will make him tell me everything, and I will show him that I love him and so understand him,” Levin decided to himself, as he drove up, after ten o’clock, to the hotel named in the address.

“Upstairs, numbers 12 and 13,” the porter answered Levin’s question.

“Is he in?”

“He must be.”

The door of number 12 was half open, and from it, in a strip of light, came the thick smoke of bad, weak tobacco, and a voice unknown to Levin could be heard; but Levin knew at once that his brother was there: he heard his cough.

As he came in at the door, the unknown voice was saying:

“Everything depends on how reasonably and consciously the thing is carried on.”

Konstantin Levin looked in at the door and saw that the speaker was a young man with an enormous shock of hair, in a peasant’s coat, while a young pock-marked woman in a woollen dress without cuffs or collar was sitting on the sofa. His brother could not be seen. Konstantin’s heart contracted painfully at the thought of the strange people among whom his brother was living. No one heard him, and Konstantin, taking off his galoshes, listened to what the gentleman in the peasant coat was saying. He was talking about some enterprise.

“Well, the devil take them, the privileged classes,” his brother’s voice said, clearing his throat. “Masha! Get us some supper and give us some wine, if there’s any left; otherwise send out for it.”

The woman got up, went behind the partition and saw Konstantin.

“There’s some gentleman, Nikolai Dmitrich,” she said.

“Who does he want?” said Nikolai Levin’s voice angrily.

“It’s me,” answered Konstantin Levin, coming out into the light.

“Who’s me?” Nikolai’s voice repeated, still more angrily. He could be heard getting up quickly, catching against something, and Levin saw before him in the doorway the enormous, thin, stooping figure of his brother, so familiar and yet so startling in its wildness and sickliness, with his big frightened eyes.

He was still thinner than three years before, when Konstantin Levin had seen him last. He was wearing a short frock-coat. And his hands and broad bones seemed still more enormous. His hair had grown thinner; the same straight moustache hung over his lips; the same eyes looked strangely and naively at the newcomer.

“Ah, Kostya!” he said suddenly, recognizing his brother, and his eyes lit up with joy. But in that same second he looked round at the young man and made that convulsive movement of his head and neck, so familiar to Konstantin, as though his tie were choking him; and quite another expression, wild, suffering and cruel, settled on his emaciated face.

“I wrote to you and to Sergei Ivanych that I don’t know you and don’t want to know you. What do you, what do you want?”

He was not at all as Konstantin had imagined him. The hardest and worst thing in his character, what made it so difficult to have anything to do with him, had been forgotten by Konstantin Levin when he thought of him; and now, when he saw his face, and especially that convulsive turning of the head, he remembered it all.

“I don’t need to see you for anything,” he answered timidly. “I simply came to see you.”

His brother’s timidity evidently softened Nikolai. His lips twitched.

“Ah, is that it?” he said. “Well, come in, sit down. Do you want some supper? Masha, bring three portions. No, wait. Do you know who this is?” he said to his brother, pointing to the gentleman in the peasant coat. “This is Mr Kritsky, my friend from Kiev days, a very remarkable man. The police persecute him, of course, because he is not a scoundrel.”

And he looked round, from habit, at everyone in the room. Seeing that the woman standing in the doorway was about to go, he shouted to her: “Wait, I said.” And with that inability, that clumsiness in conversation, which Konstantin knew so well, he began, again looking round at everyone, to tell his brother Kritsky’s story: how he had been expelled from the university because he had started a society for helping poor students, and Sunday schools; and how he had afterwards become a teacher in a village school, and how he had been driven out of there too; and how he had then been tried for something.

“You were at Kiev University?” Konstantin Levin said to Kritsky, to break the awkward silence that had set in.

“Yes, Kiev,” said Kritsky angrily, scowling.

“And this woman,” Nikolai Levin interrupted him, pointing to her, “is the companion of my life, Marya Nikolaevna. I took her out of a house,” and his neck jerked as he said it. “But I love her and respect her, and all who want to know me,” he added, raising his voice and frowning, “I ask to love and respect her. She is the same as my wife, just the same. So now you know whom you are dealing with. And if you think you will be lowering yourself, there is God, and there is the door.”

And again his eyes ran questioningly over everyone.

“Why should I be lowering myself? I don’t understand.”

“Then, Masha, have them bring supper: three portions, vodka and wine… No, wait… No, never mind… Go.”
