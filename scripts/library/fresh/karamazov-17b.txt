## Chapter III · An Onion

Grushenka lived in the busiest part of the town, near Cathedral Square, in the house of the merchant’s widow Morozova, from whom she rented a small wooden lodge in the yard. Morozova’s house was large, of stone, two storeys high, old and very unattractive to look at; the owner herself lived in it in seclusion, an old woman, with her two nieces, also rather elderly spinsters. She had no need to let her lodge in the yard, but everyone knew that she had taken in Grushenka as a lodger (some four years before) solely to oblige her relative, the merchant Samsonov, Grushenka’s open protector. It was said that the jealous old man, in placing his “favourite” with Morozova, had at first had in mind the old woman’s sharp eye, to watch over the new lodger’s conduct. But the sharp eye very soon proved unnecessary, and it ended with Morozova rarely even meeting Grushenka and, towards the end, not troubling her with any supervision at all. True, four years had passed since the old man had brought to this house from the provincial capital a girl of eighteen, timid, shy, slender, thin, pensive and sad, and much water had flowed since then. The story of this girl, however, was little known in our town, and in a muddled way; nothing more was learned of late either, even when a great many people had begun to take an interest in the “raving beauty” that Agrafena Alexandrovna had turned into in four years. There were only rumours that, when still a girl of seventeen, she had been deceived by someone, an officer supposedly, and then at once abandoned by him. The officer, it was said, had gone away and married somewhere afterwards, and Grushenka had been left in disgrace and poverty. It was said, however, that though Grushenka had indeed been taken by her old man out of poverty, she came of an honest family and was of clerical origin somehow, the daughter of some supernumerary deacon or something of the kind. And in four years the sensitive, injured and pitiful little orphan had become a rosy, full-bodied Russian beauty, a woman of a bold and decided character, proud and insolent, who understood money, acquisitive, stingy and cautious, who, by fair means or foul, had already managed, so they said, to scrape together a little capital of her own. Everyone was convinced of one thing only: that Grushenka was hard of access, and that, apart from the old man, her protector, there had not been a single man in all those four years who could boast of her favour. This was a firm fact, because not a few eager men had come forward to win that favour, especially in the last two years. But all the attempts came to nothing, and some of the suitors were even forced to retreat with a comic and shameful ending, thanks to the firm and mocking rebuff of the strong-willed young person. It was known too that the young person, especially in the last year, had gone in for what is called “business”, and that in this direction she proved to have extraordinary abilities, so that in the end many called her a regular Jewess. Not that she lent money at interest; but it was known, for instance, that in company with Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov she had for a time really been buying up promissory notes for next to nothing, ten kopecks to the rouble, and afterwards realised on some of those notes a rouble for every ten kopecks. The sick Samsonov, who in the last year had lost the use of his swollen legs, a widower, the tyrant of his grown-up sons, a man worth a great many hundreds of thousands, stingy and implacable, had nevertheless fallen under the strong influence of his protégée, whom at first he had kept in an iron grip and on short commons, “on Lenten oil”, as the wags said then. But Grushenka had managed to emancipate herself, while inspiring him with a boundless trust in her faithfulness to him. This old man, a great man of business (long dead now), was also of a remarkable character, above all stingy and hard as flint; and though Grushenka had so struck him that he could not live without her (in the last two years, for instance, that was really so), still he did not settle any large, considerable capital on her, and even if she had threatened to leave him altogether, he would have remained implacable. But he did settle a small capital on her, and when this became known it too was a matter of general surprise. “You are no fool of a woman yourself,” he told her, settling some eight thousand on her; “go and manage it yourself; but know that, apart from your yearly allowance as before, until my death you will get nothing more from me, and in my will I shan’t leave you anything more either.” And so he kept his word: he died and left everything to his sons, whom all his life he had kept by him on the same footing as servants, with their wives and children, and he did not even mention Grushenka in his will at all. All this became known later. But he helped Grushenka a good deal with advice on how to manage “her own capital” and pointed out “deals” to her. When Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov, who had first got involved with Grushenka over some chance piece of “business”, ended, quite unexpectedly for himself, by falling madly in love with her and as it were losing his wits, old Samsonov, who by then had one foot in the grave, laughed at him heartily. It is remarkable that throughout their acquaintance Grushenka was completely, and even heartily, frank with her old man, and with him, it seems, alone of all men in the world. Most recently, when Dmitri Fyodorovich too had suddenly appeared with his love, the old man stopped laughing. On the contrary, he once gave Grushenka serious and stern advice: “If you must choose between the two, father or son, choose the old man, but on condition that the old scoundrel marries you without fail and settles at least some capital on you first. And have nothing to do with the captain; no good will come of it.” These were the very words of the old voluptuary to Grushenka, who already felt his death near and did in fact die five months after giving this advice. I will note too, in passing, that though many in our town even then knew of the absurd and monstrous rivalry of the Karamazovs, father and son, of which Grushenka was the object, few people then understood the real meaning of her attitude to both of them, to the old man and to the son. Even both of Grushenka’s maids (after the catastrophe that broke later, of which more is to come) testified afterwards in court that Agrafena Alexandrovna received Dmitri Fyodorovich only out of fear, because, it was said, “he threatened to kill her”. She had two maids: a very old cook, from her parents’ family, sick and almost deaf, and her granddaughter, a lively young girl of about twenty, Grushenka’s housemaid. Grushenka lived very thriftily and in surroundings by no means rich. In her lodge there were only three rooms, furnished by the landlady with ancient mahogany furniture in the style of the twenties. When Rakitin and Alyosha came in it was already quite dusk, but the rooms were not yet lit. Grushenka herself was lying in her drawing room on her big clumsy sofa with a back of imitation mahogany, hard and covered with leather that had long since worn thin and into holes. Under her head were two white down pillows from her bed. She lay on her back, stretched out motionless, both hands behind her head. She was dressed up as if expecting someone, in a black silk dress and a light lace cap on her head, which suited her very well; over her shoulders a lace kerchief was thrown, fastened with a massive gold brooch. She really was expecting someone; she lay as if in anguish and impatience, her face somewhat pale, her lips and eyes burning, impatiently tapping the arm of the sofa with the tip of her right foot. As soon as Rakitin and Alyosha appeared there was a small commotion: one could hear from the hall how Grushenka jumped up quickly from the sofa and suddenly cried out in fright: “Who is there?” But the maid met the visitors and at once called back to her mistress:

“It isn’t him, ma’am, it’s others; these are all right.”

“What’s the matter with her?” Rakitin muttered, leading Alyosha by the hand into the drawing room. Grushenka was standing by the sofa as if still frightened. A thick lock of her dark-brown plait had suddenly slipped out from under her cap and fallen onto her right shoulder, but she did not notice it or put it right until she had looked closely at her visitors and recognised them.

“Ah, it’s you, Rakitka? You quite frightened me. Who is that with you? Who have you brought? Lord, look who he has brought!” she exclaimed, making out Alyosha.

“Have some candles brought, then!” said Rakitin with the free and easy air of a most intimate acquaintance, someone close who even has the right to give orders in the house.

“Candles… of course, candles… Fenya, bring him a candle… Well, you have found a time to bring him!” she exclaimed again, nodding at Alyosha, and, turning to the mirror, she quickly began to put her plait back in place with both hands. She seemed to be displeased.

“Haven’t I pleased you?” asked Rakitin, almost offended in an instant.

“You frightened me, Rakitka, that’s what,” Grushenka turned with a smile to Alyosha. “Don’t be afraid of me, Alyosha, my dear; I’m awfully glad to see you, my unexpected guest. But you frightened me, Rakitka: I thought Mitya was breaking in. You see, I fooled him earlier and made him give me his word of honour to believe me, and I lied to him. I told him I was going to Kuzma Kuzmich, my old man, for the whole evening and would be counting money with him till night. I go to him every week for the whole evening to settle accounts. We lock ourselves in: he clicks away on the abacus and I sit and write in the books; he trusts no one but me. Mitya believed I was there, and I have locked myself in at home; I’m sitting here waiting for a certain message. How did Fenya let you in? Fenya, Fenya! Run to the gate, open it and look round, see if the captain isn’t somewhere about. Perhaps he’s hiding and spying; I’m frightened to death!”

“There’s no one, Agrafena Alexandrovna; I looked round just now, and I keep going and peeping through the crack every minute; I’m all of a tremble myself.”

“Are the shutters closed, Fenya? And the curtain should be drawn, there!” She drew the heavy curtains herself. “Or he’ll fly straight at the light. I’m afraid of Mitya, your brother, today, Alyosha.” Grushenka spoke loudly, anxious, yet also as if in a kind of rapture.

“Why are you so afraid of Mitenka today?” Rakitin inquired. “You don’t seem timid with him as a rule; he dances to your tune.”

“I tell you, I’m waiting for a message, a golden little message, so that I don’t need Mitenka at all now. And he didn’t believe me, I can feel it, that I was going to Kuzma Kuzmich. I expect he is sitting there now, at Fyodor Pavlovich’s, in the garden at the back, watching for me. And if he has settled down there, he won’t come here, so much the better! I did really run to Kuzma Kuzmich’s; Mitya saw me there himself; I said I’d sit there till midnight and that he must come at midnight without fail to see me home. He went away, and I sat with the old man for ten minutes and then came back here; oh, I was frightened; I ran so as not to meet him.”

“And what are you dressed up for? Look at the curious cap you’re wearing!”

“And how curious you are yourself, Rakitin! I tell you, I’m waiting for a certain little message. When it comes, I’ll jump up and fly away, and that’s the last you’ll see of me. That’s why I’m dressed up, to be ready.”

“And where will you fly to?”

“Curiosity killed the cat.”

“Look at that. All joy… I’ve never seen you like this before. Got up as if for a ball,” Rakitin looked her over.

“Much you know about balls.”

“And you know a lot?”

“I’ve seen a ball. Two years ago Kuzma Kuzmich married off his son, and I watched from the gallery. But why should I be talking to you, Rakitka, when there is such a prince standing here? There’s a guest for you! Alyosha, my dear, I look at you and don’t believe it; Lord, how did you come to be here with me? To tell you the truth, I never expected it, never dreamed of it, and I never believed before that you could come. Though it isn’t the moment for it now, I’m awfully glad to see you! Sit on the sofa, here, like that, my bright young moon. Really, I can’t seem to take it in yet… Oh, Rakitka, if only you had brought him yesterday or the day before!… Well, I’m glad as it is. Perhaps it’s better that it’s now, at such a moment, and not the day before yesterday…”

She sat down briskly beside Alyosha on the sofa, right next to him, and looked at him with decided admiration. And she really was glad; she was not lying when she said so. Her eyes were burning, her lips were laughing, but good-naturedly, merrily. Alyosha had not expected such a kind expression on her face… He had met her little until yesterday and had formed a terrifying idea of her, and yesterday he had been so dreadfully shaken by her spiteful and treacherous outburst against Katerina Ivanovna that he was very surprised now to see in her suddenly quite another and unexpected being. And crushed though he was by his own grief, his eyes rested on her involuntarily with attention. All her manners too seemed to have changed completely for the better since yesterday: there was almost none of yesterday’s mawkishness in her speech, none of those pampered and affected movements… everything was simple, artless; her movements were quick, direct, trusting; but she was very excited.

“Lord, what things are coming to pass today, really,” she prattled again. “And why I am so glad to see you, Alyosha, I don’t know myself. Ask me, and I don’t know.”

“Oh, so you don’t know what you’re glad about?” Rakitin smirked. “Before, you kept pestering me for some reason: bring him, bring him; you had some aim.”

“Before, I had another aim, but now that has passed; it isn’t the moment. I’m going to treat you both, that’s what. I’ve grown kind now, Rakitka. And sit down yourself, Rakitka; why are you standing? Or have you sat down already? Trust Rakitushka not to forget himself. There he is now, Alyosha, sitting opposite us and taking offence: why didn’t I ask him to sit down before you? Oh, my Rakitka is touchy, touchy!” Grushenka laughed. “Don’t be cross, Rakitka; today I’m kind. But why are you sitting so sad, Alyoshechka? Are you afraid of me?” She peered into his eyes with merry mockery.

“He is in grief. He wasn’t promoted,” Rakitin boomed.

“Promoted to what?”

“His elder has begun to stink.”

“What do you mean, begun to stink? You are grinding out some nonsense; you want to say something nasty. Be quiet, you fool. Will you let me sit on your knee, Alyosha, like this!” And suddenly in an instant she sprang up and jumped, laughing, onto his knee, like a coaxing kitten, her right arm tenderly round his neck. “I’ll cheer you up, my pious boy! No, really, will you let me sit on your knee? You won’t be angry? Tell me to, and I’ll jump down.”

Alyosha was silent. He sat afraid to move; he heard her words, “Tell me to, and I’ll jump down,” but he did not answer, as if he had frozen. But what was in him was not what, for instance, Rakitin, watching greedily from his place, might have expected or imagined in him now. The great grief of his soul swallowed up all the sensations that might have been born in his heart, and if he could have given himself a full account at that moment, he would have guessed himself that he was now in the strongest armour against every seduction and temptation. Nevertheless, in spite of all the vague unaccountability of his state of mind and of all the grief that oppressed him, he could not help wondering at a new and strange sensation being born in his heart. This woman, this “terrible” woman, not only did not frighten him now with the old fear, the fear that used to rise in him at any thought of a woman, if such a thought flashed through his soul; on the contrary, this woman, whom he had feared more than anyone, sitting on his knee and embracing him, now suddenly roused in him a quite different, unexpected and peculiar feeling, a feeling of some extraordinary, immense and most pure-hearted curiosity about her, and all this without any fear, without the slightest trace of his former horror: that was the main thing, and it involuntarily surprised him.

“That’s enough of your nonsense,” Rakitin shouted. “You had better bring the champagne; you owe it, you know you do!”

“True, I do. You see, Alyosha, I promised him champagne on top of everything if he brought you. Out with the champagne, and I’ll drink too! Fenya, Fenya, bring us champagne, the bottle Mitya left; run, quick. Stingy as I am, I’ll bring out a bottle, not for you, Rakitka, you’re a toadstool, but he’s a prince! And though it isn’t that my soul is full of now, so be it, I’ll drink with you; I feel like a spree!”

“But what is this moment of yours, and what sort of ‘message’ is it, may one ask, or is it a secret?” Rakitin put in again with curiosity, trying with all his might to look as if he paid no attention to the flicks that kept flying at him.

“Oh, it’s no secret, and you know it yourself,” Grushenka said suddenly with a worried look, turning her head to Rakitin and leaning away a little from Alyosha, though still sitting on his knee with her arm round his neck. “The officer is coming, Rakitin, my officer is coming!”

“I heard he was coming; but is he so near already?”

“He is at Mokroye now; he’ll send a courier from there; he wrote so himself; I got a letter today. I’m sitting and waiting for the courier.”

“Well, well! Why at Mokroye?”

“It’s a long story, and you have heard enough.”

“So what about Mitenka now? Oh, oh! Does he know or not?”

“Know what! He knows nothing at all! If he found out, he’d kill me. But I’m not afraid of that at all now; I’m not afraid of his knife now. Be quiet, Rakitka, don’t remind me of Dmitri Fyodorovich: he has crushed my heart to pieces. And I don’t want to think about any of that at this moment. I can think of Alyoshechka; I’m looking at Alyoshechka… Smile at me, my dear, cheer up, smile at my foolishness, at my joy… Why, he smiled, he smiled! Look how kindly he looks. You know, Alyosha, I kept thinking you were angry with me for the day before yesterday, for that young lady. I was a dog, that’s what… Only it is good all the same that it happened so. It was bad and it was good,” Grushenka suddenly smiled thoughtfully, and a sort of cruel little line flashed in her smile. “Mitya said she screamed, ‘She ought to be flogged!’ I offended her terribly then. She invited me, wanted to conquer me, to seduce me with her chocolate… No, it’s good that it happened so,” she smiled again. “Only I keep being afraid that you are angry…”

“Why, it’s true,” Rakitin suddenly put in with serious surprise. “She really is afraid of you, Alyosha, a little chick like you.”

“To you, Rakitka, he is a chick, that’s what… because you have no conscience, that’s what! I, you see, I love him with my soul, that’s what! Do you believe, Alyosha, that I love you with all my soul?”

“Oh, you shameless woman! She is declaring her love to you, Alexei!”

“And why not? I do love him.”

“And the officer? And the golden little message from Mokroye?”

“That is one thing, and this is another.”

“That’s a woman’s way of looking at it!”

“Don’t make me angry, Rakitka,” Grushenka caught him up hotly. “That is one thing, and this is another. I love Alyosha differently. It’s true, Alyosha, I had a sly idea about you before. But then I’m low, I’m violent; and yet at another moment, Alyosha, I used to look at you as at my conscience. I kept thinking: ‘How a man like that must despise a nasty woman like me now.’ I thought it the day before yesterday too, as I ran here from the young lady’s. I noticed you like that long ago, Alyosha, and Mitya knows; I told him. Now Mitya understands. Would you believe it, sometimes, really, Alyosha, I look at you and feel ashamed, ashamed of my whole self… And how I came to think of you, and since when, I don’t know and don’t remember…”

Fenya came in and set a tray on the table, with an uncorked bottle on it and three glasses already poured.

“The champagne has come!” Rakitin shouted. “You are excited, Agrafena Alexandrovna, and beside yourself. One glass, and you’ll be off dancing. Ugh, they couldn’t even do that properly,” he added, looking at the champagne. “The old woman poured it out in the kitchen, and they have brought the bottle without a cork, and it’s warm. Well, let’s have it as it is.”

He went to the table, took a glass, drank it at a gulp and poured himself another.

“One doesn’t often come across champagne,” he said, licking his lips. “Come on, Alyosha, take a glass, show what you’re made of. What shall we drink to? The gates of paradise? Take a glass, Grusha, and you drink to the gates of paradise too.”

“What gates of paradise?”

She took a glass. Alyosha took his, drank a sip and put the glass back.

“No, better not!” he smiled quietly.

“And you boasted!” cried Rakitin.

“Well, if so, I won’t either,” Grushenka caught up. “I don’t want to anyway. Drink the whole bottle by yourself, Rakitka. If Alyosha drinks, then I’ll drink.”

“Here comes the calf-love!” Rakitin teased. “And she’s sitting on his knee herself! He has his grief, let’s say, but what have you got? He has rebelled against his God; he was ready to gobble sausage…”

“How so?”

“His elder died today, Elder Zosima, the saint.”

“So Elder Zosima has died!” Grushenka exclaimed. “Lord, and I didn’t know it!” She crossed herself devoutly. “Lord, what am I doing, sitting on his knee now!” She started up suddenly as if in fright, slipped off his knee in an instant and moved to the sofa. Alyosha gave her a long, surprised look, and something seemed to light up in his face.

“Rakitin,” he said suddenly, loudly and firmly, “don’t taunt me with having rebelled against my God. I don’t want to bear you any spite, so be kinder yourself. I have lost such a treasure as you have never had, and you cannot judge me now. Look at her instead: did you see how she spared me? I came here to find a wicked soul; I was drawn to it myself, because I was base and wicked; and I have found a true sister, I have found a treasure, a loving soul… She spared me just now… Agrafena Alexandrovna, I am speaking of you. You have restored my soul just now.”

Alyosha’s lips trembled and his breath caught. He stopped.

“As if she had saved you!” Rakitin laughed spitefully. “And she wanted to swallow you up, do you know that?”

“Stop, Rakitka!” Grushenka suddenly jumped up. “Be quiet, both of you. Now I’ll say everything. You, Alyosha, be quiet, because such words of yours make me ashamed, because I am wicked and not good; that’s what I am. And you, Rakitka, be quiet because you are lying. I did have such a vile idea, that I wanted to swallow him up; but now you are lying; now it isn’t that at all… and don’t let me hear another word from you, Rakitka!” Grushenka said all this with extraordinary agitation.

“Look at them both raving!” hissed Rakitin, staring at them both in amazement. “Like madmen; as if I’d landed in a lunatic asylum. They’ve both gone soft; they’ll start crying in a minute!”

“And I will start crying, I will!” said Grushenka. “He called me his sister, and I shall never forget that! Only this, Rakitka: wicked as I am, I did give an onion.”

“What onion? Ugh, the devil, they really have gone mad!”

Rakitin wondered at their rapture and grew angry and offended, though he might have realised that everything had come together for both of them that could shake their souls as happens not often in life. But Rakitin, who could understand very sensitively everything that concerned himself, was very coarse in understanding the feelings and sensations of his neighbours, partly from his youthful inexperience and partly from his great egoism.

“You see, Alyoshechka,” Grushenka suddenly laughed nervously, turning to him, “I boasted to Rakitka that I gave an onion, but I won’t boast to you; I’ll tell it to you with another aim. It’s only a fable, but it’s a good fable; I heard it when I was still a child from my Matryona, who is my cook now. This is how it goes: ‘Once upon a time there was a woman, as wicked as wicked could be, and she died. And not a single good deed was left behind her. The devils seized her and threw her into the lake of fire. And her guardian angel stood and thought: what good deed of hers can I remember to tell God? He remembered and said to God: she once pulled up an onion in her garden and gave it to a beggar woman. And God answered him: take that very onion, then, hold it out to her in the lake, let her catch hold of it and be pulled, and if you pull her out of the lake, let her go to paradise; but if the onion breaks, then the woman must stay where she is now. The angel ran to the woman and held out the onion to her: here, woman, catch hold and be pulled. And he began to pull her carefully and had almost pulled her out, when the other sinners in the lake, seeing her being pulled out, all began catching hold of her, so as to be pulled out with her. But the woman was as wicked as wicked could be, and she began kicking them with her feet: “It’s me that’s being pulled, not you; it’s my onion, not yours.” As soon as she said that, the onion broke. And the woman fell into the lake and is burning to this day. And the angel wept and went away.’ That’s the fable, Alyosha; I learned it by heart, because I am that very woman, as wicked as can be. I boasted to Rakitka that I gave an onion, but to you I’ll say it differently: in my whole life I have given only one onion or so; that is all the good deed there is in me. And don’t praise me after that, Alyosha; don’t think me good. I’m wicked, as wicked as can be, and if you praise me you’ll put me to shame. Oh, I may as well confess everything. Listen, Alyosha: I wanted so much to lure you here, and I pestered Rakitka so, that I promised him twenty-five roubles if he brought you to me. Wait, Rakitka, wait!” She went with quick steps to the table, opened a drawer, took out a purse, and from it a twenty-five-rouble note.

“What nonsense! What nonsense!” exclaimed the disconcerted Rakitin.

“Take what I owe you, Rakitka; I don’t suppose you’ll refuse; you asked for it yourself.” And she flung the note at him.

“As if I would refuse,” Rakitin boomed, evidently embarrassed but covering his shame with a swagger. “It will come in very handy; fools exist for the profit of the clever man.”

“And now be quiet, Rakitka; everything I’m going to say now is not for your ears. Sit in the corner there and be quiet; you don’t love us, so be quiet.”

“And why should I love you?” Rakitin snapped, no longer hiding his spite. He had stuffed the twenty-five-rouble note into his pocket, and he was decidedly ashamed in front of Alyosha. He had reckoned on getting his payment afterwards, so that Alyosha would not even know, and now he was furious from shame. Until this moment he had found it very politic not to contradict Grushenka much, in spite of all her flicks, for it was clear that she had some power over him. But now he too lost his temper:

“People love for some reason; and what have you two done for me?”

“Love for no reason, the way Alyosha loves.”

“And how does he love you, and what has he shown you that you make such a fuss of him?”

Grushenka was standing in the middle of the room; she spoke with heat, and hysterical notes could be heard in her voice.

“Be quiet, Rakitka, you understand nothing about us! And don’t you dare speak to me so familiarly in future; I won’t allow it; where did you get such boldness, I’d like to know! Sit in the corner and be quiet, like my footman. And now, Alyosha, I’ll tell you the whole pure truth, you alone, so that you see what a creature I am! I’m saying it to you, not to Rakitka. I wanted to ruin you, Alyosha; that is the great truth; I had quite made up my mind to it; I wanted it so much that I bribed Rakitka with money to bring you. And what made me want it so? You, Alyosha, knew nothing; you turned away from me; when you passed me you dropped your eyes; and I had looked at you a hundred times before this; I began asking everyone about you. Your face stayed in my heart: ‘He despises me,’ I thought; ‘he won’t even want to look at me.’ And such a feeling took hold of me in the end that I wonder at myself: why am I afraid of such a boy? I’ll swallow him whole and laugh. I was quite furious. Would you believe it: no one here dares to say or think that he could come to Agrafena Alexandrovna for that bad business; I have only the old man; I am bound to him and sold to him, Satan married us; but of the others, no one. But looking at you, I made up my mind: I’ll swallow him. I’ll swallow him and laugh. You see what a wicked dog I am, whom you called your sister! And now this man who wronged me has come, and I’m sitting here waiting for a message. And do you know what this man who wronged me was to me? Five years ago, when Kuzma brought me here, I used to sit hiding from people, so that they shouldn’t see or hear me, thin, silly; I would sit and sob; I didn’t sleep for nights on end; I kept thinking: ‘And where is he now, the man who wronged me? Laughing at me with another woman, I expect; and oh, if only I could see him, meet him some time, I’d pay him back, I’d pay him back!’ At night in the dark I would sob into my pillow and think it all over; I tore my heart on purpose, I fed it with spite: ‘Oh, I’ll pay him back, I’ll pay him back!’ That’s how I would cry out in the dark. And when I suddenly remembered that I could do nothing to him, while he was laughing at me now, or perhaps had forgotten me altogether and didn’t remember, I would throw myself from the bed onto the floor, flood myself with helpless tears and shake and shake till dawn. In the morning I’d get up angrier than a dog, ready to swallow the whole world. Then, what do you think? I began to save up capital; I grew pitiless; I grew stout; I grew wiser, you think, eh? Well, I didn’t; no one sees it or knows it in the whole universe, but when the darkness of night comes down, I lie there sometimes just as I did as a girl five years ago, grinding my teeth and crying all night: ‘Oh, I’ll pay him back, I’ll pay him back,’ I think! Did you hear all that? Well, then, how do you understand me now? A month ago, suddenly, this very letter comes to me: he is coming, he has been left a widower, he wants to see me. It took my breath away, Lord, and suddenly I thought: and if he comes and whistles to me, calls me, I’ll crawl to him like a little beaten dog, guilty! I think this and don’t believe myself: ‘Am I vile or not? Shall I run to him or not?’ And such anger at myself has come over me all this month, worse even than five years ago. Do you see now, Alyosha, how violent I am, how furious? I’ve told you the whole truth! I played with Mitya so as not to run to the other one. Be quiet, Rakitka, it’s not for you to judge me; it wasn’t you I was telling. Before you came I was lying here waiting, thinking, deciding my whole fate, and you will never know what was in my heart. No, Alyosha, tell your young lady not to be angry about the day before yesterday!… And no one in the whole world knows what it is like for me now, and no one can know… Because perhaps I’ll take a knife with me there today; I haven’t decided yet…”

And having uttered this “pitiful” word, Grushenka suddenly broke down; she did not finish, but covered her face with her hands, threw herself on the sofa among the pillows and sobbed like a little child. Alyosha got up and went over to Rakitin.

“Misha,” he said, “don’t be angry. She has hurt you, but don’t be angry. Did you hear her just now? One can’t demand so much of a human soul; one must be more merciful…”

Alyosha said this in an irresistible impulse of his heart. He needed to speak out, and he turned to Rakitin. If Rakitin had not been there he would have begun exclaiming to himself. But Rakitin gave him a mocking look, and Alyosha suddenly stopped.

“That’s your elder they loaded you with earlier, and now you’ve fired your elder off at me, Alyoshenka, little man of God,” Rakitin said with a hateful smile.

“Don’t laugh, Rakitin, don’t smirk, don’t talk of the dead man: he is higher than anyone who has ever lived on earth!” Alyosha cried with tears in his voice. “I didn’t stand up to speak to you as a judge, but as the lowest of the accused myself. What am I before her? I came here to be ruined, and I said, ‘Let it be, let it be!’, and that from my faint-heartedness; and she, after five years of torment, as soon as someone came and spoke a sincere word to her, has forgiven everything, forgotten everything, and is weeping! The man who wronged her has come back, he is calling her, and she forgives him everything and hurries to him in joy, and she won’t take a knife, she won’t! No, I am not like that. I don’t know whether you are like that, Misha, but I am not! I have had this lesson today, just now… She is higher than we are in love… Had you heard from her before what she has told us now? No, you hadn’t; if you had, you would have understood everything long ago… and the other one, who was insulted the day before yesterday, let her forgive her too! And she will forgive, if she learns… and she will learn… This soul is not yet reconciled; one must spare it… there may be a treasure in this soul…”

Alyosha fell silent because his breath failed him. Rakitin, in spite of all his spite, was looking at him in surprise. He had never expected such a tirade from the quiet Alyosha.

“Here’s a lawyer for you! Have you fallen in love with her or what? Agrafena Alexandrovna, our faster really has fallen in love with you; you have conquered!” he shouted with an insolent laugh.

Grushenka raised her head from the pillow and looked at Alyosha with a tender smile that lit up her face, somehow swollen all at once from her recent tears.

“Leave him alone, Alyosha, my cherub; you see what he is like; you’ve found someone to talk to. Mikhail Osipovich,” she turned to Rakitin, “I meant to ask your forgiveness for abusing you, but now I don’t want to again. Alyosha, come to me, sit here,” she beckoned him with a joyful smile, “like that, sit here; tell me” (she took his hand and peered smiling into his face), “tell me: do I love that man or not? The man who wronged me, do I love him or not? Before you came I was lying here in the dark, questioning my heart over and over: do I love him or not? Decide it for me, Alyosha; the time has come; whatever you decide, so it shall be. Shall I forgive him or not?”

“But you have forgiven him already,” Alyosha said, smiling.

“And so I have,” Grushenka said thoughtfully. “What a vile heart! To my vile heart!” She suddenly snatched a glass from the table, drank it at one gulp, raised it and flung it on the floor. The glass broke with a ring. A sort of cruel little line flashed in her smile.

“And yet perhaps I haven’t forgiven him yet,” she said somehow menacingly, dropping her eyes to the ground, as if talking to herself alone. “Perhaps my heart is only getting ready to forgive. I’ll struggle with my heart yet. You see, Alyosha, I have grown terribly fond of my five years’ tears… Perhaps I have only grown fond of my wrong, and not of him at all!”

“Well, I shouldn’t like to be in his skin!” hissed Rakitin.

“And you won’t be, Rakitka; you will never be in his skin. You shall make my shoes, Rakitka; that’s the job I’ll use you for; and a woman like me you will never see… And perhaps he won’t either…”

“He won’t? Then what have you dressed up for?” Rakitin teased venomously.

“Don’t reproach me with my dress, Rakitka; you don’t know my whole heart yet! If I like, I’ll tear my finery off; I’ll tear it off this minute, this very minute,” she cried ringingly. “You don’t know what this finery is for, Rakitka! Perhaps I’ll go out to him and say: ‘Have you ever seen me like this?’ For he left me a thin, consumptive cry-baby of seventeen. And I’ll sit down beside him and charm him and set him on fire: ‘Have you seen what I’m like now?’ I’ll say. ‘Well then, stay as you are, my dear sir: so near and yet so far!’ That’s perhaps what this finery is for, Rakitka,” Grushenka finished with a spiteful little laugh. “I’m violent, Alyosha, furious. I’ll tear off my finery, I’ll disfigure myself, my beauty; I’ll burn my face and cut it with a knife, and go begging. If I like, I won’t go anywhere now, to anyone; if I like, tomorrow I’ll send Kuzma back everything he has given me, and all his money, and go out as a day labourer for the rest of my life!… You think I won’t do it, Rakitka, won’t dare to do it? I will, I will; I can do it this minute; only don’t provoke me… and as for that one, I’ll drive him away, I’ll show him a fig, he won’t see me!”

She cried out the last words hysterically, but again she broke down, covered her face with her hands, threw herself on the pillow and again shook with sobs. Rakitin got up.

“It’s time,” he said. “It’s late; they won’t let us into the monastery.”

Grushenka simply leapt up.

“Can you really want to go, Alyosha?” she exclaimed in sorrowful amazement. “But what are you doing to me now? You have roused me all, torn me apart, and now there is this night again, and I am to be alone again!”

“He’s not going to spend the night with you, is he? Though if he wants to, let him! I’ll go alone!” Rakitin jeered caustically.

“Be quiet, you wicked soul,” Grushenka shouted at him furiously. “You have never said such words to me as he came to say to me.”

“What did he say to you, then?” Rakitin grumbled irritably.

“I don’t know, I don’t know, I don’t know at all what he said to me; it spoke to my heart; he turned my heart over… He was the first, the only one, to pity me, that’s what! Why didn’t you come before, my cherub?” She suddenly fell on her knees before him as if in a frenzy. “All my life I have been waiting for someone like you; I knew someone like that would come and forgive me. I believed that someone would love me too, nasty as I am, not only for my shame!…”

“What have I done for you?” Alyosha answered with a tender smile, bending over her and gently taking her hands. “I gave you an onion, one very little onion, only that, only that!…”

And having said it, he began to cry himself. At that moment there was suddenly a noise in the passage; someone had come into the hall. Grushenka leapt up as if in terrible fright. Fenya ran into the room with noise and shouting.

“Mistress, my dear, mistress, the courier has galloped up!” she cried gaily and out of breath. “A carriage from Mokroye for you, Timofei the driver with a troika; they are changing the horses this minute… A letter, a letter, mistress, here is a letter!”

The letter was in her hand, and all the time she was shouting she waved it in the air. Grushenka snatched the letter from her and held it to the candle. It was only a note, a few lines; she read it in an instant.

“He has called!” she cried, quite pale, her face twisted with a painful smile. “He has whistled! Crawl, little dog!”

But only for one instant did she stand as if irresolute; suddenly the blood rushed to her head and flooded her cheeks with fire.

“I’m going!” she suddenly exclaimed. “My five years! Goodbye! Goodbye, Alyosha, my fate is decided… Go, go, go away from me now, all of you, so that I don’t see you again!… Grushenka has flown off into a new life… Don’t remember me unkindly, you either, Rakitka. Perhaps I am going to my death! Ugh! As if I were drunk!”

She suddenly left them and ran into her bedroom.

“Well, she has no time for us now!” Rakitin grumbled. “Let’s go, or perhaps that woman’s screaming will start again; I’m sick of these tearful screams…”

Alyosha let himself be led out mechanically. In the yard stood the carriage; they were unharnessing the horses, walking about with a lantern, bustling. A fresh troika was being led in through the open gate. But no sooner had Alyosha and Rakitin come down from the porch than the window of Grushenka’s bedroom suddenly opened, and she called after Alyosha in a ringing voice:

“Alyoshechka, give my greetings to your brother Mitenka, and tell him not to remember me, his evil one, unkindly. And tell him too, in my words: ‘Grushenka has gone to a scoundrel, and not to you, a noble man!’ And tell him too that Grushenka loved him for one little hour, only one little hour she loved him, so let him remember that little hour all his life from now on; tell him Grushenka bids him so for all his life!…”

She finished in a voice full of sobs. The window slammed shut.

“Hm, hm!” Rakitin mumbled, laughing. “She has cut brother Mitenka’s throat and tells him to remember it all his life too. What carnivorousness!”

Alyosha made no answer, as if he had not heard; he walked fast beside Rakitin, as if in a terrible hurry; he was as if in a trance, walking mechanically. Something suddenly stung Rakitin, as if someone had touched his fresh wound with a finger. This was not at all what he had expected earlier, when he brought Grushenka and Alyosha together; something quite different had happened from what he had very much wanted.

“He is a Pole, that officer of hers,” he began again, restraining himself, “and he isn’t an officer at all now; he served as a customs official in Siberia, somewhere there on the Chinese border; some puny little Pole, I expect. He lost his job, they say. Now he has heard that Grushenka has got hold of some capital, so he has come back; that’s all the miracle.”

Again Alyosha seemed not to hear. Rakitin could not contain himself:

“Well, have you converted the sinner?” he laughed spitefully at Alyosha. “Turned the harlot to the path of truth? Cast out seven devils, eh? So here they are, our miracles we were waiting for this morning; they have come to pass!”

“Stop it, Rakitin,” Alyosha answered, with suffering in his soul.

“So now you ‘despise’ me for those twenty-five roubles this evening? Sold a true friend, you think. But you are not Christ, and I am not Judas.”

“Oh, Rakitin, I assure you I had forgotten about it,” Alyosha exclaimed. “You reminded me yourself just now…”

But Rakitin was now thoroughly furious.

“The devil take you all, every one of you!” he suddenly yelled. “And why the devil did I get mixed up with you? I don’t want to know you from now on. Go on alone; there’s your road!”

And he turned sharply into another street, leaving Alyosha alone in the darkness. Alyosha went out of the town and across the fields towards the monastery.
