## On Self-Overcoming

“Will to truth”: is that what you call what drives you and sets you on fire, you wisest of men?

Will to make all that exists thinkable: that is what I call your will!

You want first to make all that exists thinkable: for you doubt, with a healthy mistrust, whether it is thinkable already.

But it must fit itself to you and bend! So your will wants it. It must become smooth and subject to the spirit, as its mirror and reflection.

That is your whole will, you wisest of men, a will to power; and even when you speak of good and evil and of valuations. You still want to create the world before which you can kneel: that is your last hope and intoxication.

The unwise, of course, the people: they are like the river on which a boat floats along; and in the boat sit the valuations, solemn and masked.

You set your will and your values on the river of becoming; what the people believe to be good and evil betrays to me an old will to power.

It was you, wisest of men, who put such passengers into this boat and gave them pomp and proud names: you and your ruling will!

Now the river carries your boat further: it must carry it. It matters little if the broken wave foams and angrily contradicts the keel!

It is not the river that is your danger and the end of your good and evil, you wisest of men, but that will itself, the will to power: the unexhausted, begetting will of life.

But so that you understand my word about good and evil, I will tell you my word about life too, and about the nature of all living things.

I followed the living; I walked the greatest and the smallest paths, to know its nature.

With a hundredfold mirror I still caught its look when its mouth was shut, so that its eye might speak to me. And its eye spoke to me.

But wherever I found living things, there I also heard talk of obedience. All living things are things that obey.

And this is the second thing: whoever cannot obey himself is commanded. That is the nature of living things.

But this is the third thing I heard: that commanding is harder than obeying. And not only that the one who commands bears the burden of all who obey, and that this burden easily crushes him:

an experiment and a risk appeared to me in all commanding; and whenever the living thing commands, it risks itself in doing so.

Yes, even when it commands itself: even then it must pay for its commanding. It must become judge and avenger and victim of its own law.

How does this happen? So I asked myself. What persuades the living thing to obey and to command, and even in commanding to practise obedience?

Hear my word now, you wisest of men! Test it seriously, whether I have crept into the heart of life itself, and down into the roots of its heart!

Wherever I found living things, I found will to power; and even in the will of the one who serves I found the will to be master.

That the weaker should serve the stronger, its will persuades it, which wants to be master over what is weaker still: this one pleasure it does not want to do without.

And as the smaller gives itself to the greater, so that it may have pleasure and power over the smallest: so even the greatest gives itself, and for the sake of power stakes its life.

That is the surrender of the greatest: that it is risk and danger, and a throw of the dice for death.

And where there is sacrifice and service and loving looks, there too is the will to be master. By secret paths the weaker creeps into the castle and into the very heart of the more powerful, and steals power there.

And this secret life itself told me. “Look,” it said, “I am that which must always overcome itself.

Of course, you call it will to beget, or drive towards a goal, towards the higher, the farther, the more manifold: but all this is one, and one secret.

I would rather go under than renounce this one thing; and truly, where there is going under and the falling of leaves, look, there life sacrifices itself, for power!

That I must be struggle and becoming and goal and the contradiction of goals: ah, whoever guesses my will surely guesses too on what crooked paths it must go!

Whatever I create, and however much I love it, soon I must be its opponent, and my love’s: so my will wills it.

And you too, seeker of knowledge, are only a path and footprint of my will: truly, my will to power walks on the feet of your will to truth as well!

He certainly did not hit the truth who shot at it with the phrase ‘will to existence’: that will does not exist!

For what does not exist cannot will; but what is in existence, how could it still will to exist!

Only where there is life is there will too: but not will to life, but, so I teach you, will to power!

Much is valued more highly by the living than life itself; but out of the very valuing speaks the will to power!”

So life once taught me; and with this I solve for you, you wisest of men, the riddle of your hearts as well.

Truly, I tell you: good and evil that would be everlasting, that does not exist! It must overcome itself again and again out of itself.

With your values and words of good and evil you use force, you who value; and this is your hidden love, and the shining, trembling and overflowing of your souls.

But a stronger force grows out of your values, and a new overcoming: on it egg and eggshell break.

And whoever must be a creator in good and evil, truly, he must first be a destroyer and break values.

So the highest evil belongs to the highest goodness: but this is the creative kind.

Let us speak of it, you wisest of men, even if it is bad. To be silent is worse; all truths kept silent become poisonous.

And let everything break that can break on our truths! There are many houses still to build!

Thus spoke Zarathustra.

## On the Sublime Ones

The floor of my sea is still: who would guess that it hides playful monsters!

My depth is unshakable: but it sparkles with swimming riddles and laughter.

Today I saw a sublime one, a solemn one, a penitent of the spirit: oh, how my soul laughed at his ugliness!

With his chest puffed out, like those who are drawing in breath: so he stood there, the sublime one, and silent:

hung with ugly truths, his hunting spoils, and rich in torn clothes; many thorns hung on him too, but I saw no rose yet.

He has not yet learned laughter and beauty. Gloomily this hunter came back from the forest of knowledge.

He came home from fighting with wild beasts: but out of his seriousness a wild beast still looks too, an unconquered one!

He still stands there like a tiger about to spring; but I do not like these tense souls; my taste is unfriendly to all these withdrawn ones.

And you tell me, friends, that there is no arguing about taste and tasting? But all life is an argument about taste and tasting!

Taste: that is at once the weight and the scale and the one who weighs; and woe to every living thing that wanted to live without arguing about weight and scale and weigher!

If he grew tired of his sublimity, this sublime one, only then would his beauty begin; and only then will I taste him and find him tasteful.

And only when he turns away from himself will he jump over his own shadow, and, truly, into his own sun.

Too long he sat in the shade; the cheeks of the penitent of the spirit grew pale; he almost starved on his expectations.

There is still contempt in his eye, and disgust hides at his mouth. He is resting now, it is true, but his rest has not yet lain down in the sun.

He should do as the bull does; and his happiness should smell of the earth, and not of contempt for the earth.

I would like to see him as a white bull, snorting and bellowing as he goes before the ploughshare; and his bellowing should still praise everything earthly!

His face is still dark; the shadow of the hand plays on it. The sense of his eye is still in shadow.

His deed itself is still the shadow on him: the hand darkens the one who acts. He has not yet overcome his deed.

I love the bull’s neck on him, it is true: but now I want to see the angel’s eye as well.

He must also unlearn his hero’s will: he should be one who is lifted up, and not merely a sublime one; the air itself should lift him, the will-less one!

He tamed monsters, he solved riddles: but he should also redeem his monsters and riddles; he should turn them into heavenly children.

His knowledge has not yet learned to smile and to be without jealousy; his streaming passion has not yet grown still in beauty.

Truly, his longing should fall silent and sink, not in being full, but in beauty! Grace belongs to the generosity of the great-minded.

With his arm laid over his head: so the hero should rest; so he should overcome even his resting.

But to the hero above all, the beautiful is the hardest thing of all. The beautiful cannot be won by any violent will.

A little more, a little less: that is just what counts for much here, for most of all.

To stand with relaxed muscles and with the will unharnessed: that is the hardest thing for all of you, you sublime ones!

When power becomes gracious and comes down into the visible: I call such coming down beauty.

And from no one do I want beauty so much as from you, you powerful one: let your goodness be your last self-conquest.

I believe you capable of every evil: that is why I want the good from you.

Truly, I have often laughed at the weaklings who think themselves good because they have lame paws!

You should strive after the virtue of the column: it grows ever more beautiful and more delicate, but inwardly harder and more able to bear weight, the higher it rises.

Yes, you sublime one, one day you shall still be beautiful, and hold the mirror up to your own beauty.

Then your soul will shudder with divine desires; and there will be worship even in your vanity!

For this is the secret of the soul: only when the hero has left it does there come to it, in a dream, the over-hero.

Thus spoke Zarathustra.

## On the Land of Culture

I flew too far into the future: a horror came over me.

And when I looked around me, look! time was my only contemporary.

Then I fled backwards, homewards, and ever faster: so I came to you, you people of today, and into the land of culture.

For the first time I brought an eye for you, and a good desire: truly, I came with longing in my heart.

But what happened to me? Afraid as I was, I had to laugh! Never had my eye seen anything so motley!

I laughed and laughed, while my foot still trembled, and my heart as well: “Why, this is the home of all the paint pots!” I said.

Painted with fifty blotches on your faces and limbs: so you sat there, to my astonishment, you people of today!

And with fifty mirrors around you, flattering your play of colours and repeating it!

Truly, you could wear no better mask, you people of today, than your own face! Who could recognise you!

Written all over with the signs of the past, and these signs too painted over with new signs: so you have hidden yourselves well from all interpreters of signs!

And even if one could test the inmost heart: who would still believe that you have one! You seem to be baked out of colours and glued scraps of paper.

All ages and peoples look out motley from your veils; all customs and beliefs speak motley out of your gestures.

Whoever took away your veils and wraps and colours and gestures would have just enough left over to frighten the birds with.

Truly, I myself am the frightened bird who once saw you naked and without colour; and I flew away when the skeleton beckoned to me with love.

I would rather be a day labourer in the underworld, among the shades of the past! Why, even the people of the underworld are plumper and fuller than you!

This, yes this is bitterness to my bowels, that I can bear you neither naked nor clothed, you people of today!

Everything uncanny in the future, and everything that ever made strayed birds shudder, is truly more homely and more familiar than your “reality”.

For so you speak: “We are wholly real, and without faith or superstition.” So you puff out your chests: ah, though you have no chests!

Yes, how could you be able to believe, you motley ones! You who are paintings of everything that was ever believed!

You are walking refutations of belief itself, and the breaking of the limbs of every thought. Unbelievable: that is what I call you, you real ones!

All ages chatter against one another in your spirits; and the dreams and chatter of all ages were more real than your being awake!

You are barren: that is why you lack faith. But whoever had to create always had his prophetic dreams and signs in the stars too, and believed in belief!

You are half-open gates, at which gravediggers wait. And this is your reality: “Everything deserves to perish.”

Ah, how you stand there before me, you barren ones, how lean in the ribs! And many of you have surely noticed this yourselves.

And he said: “Did a god perhaps secretly take something from me while I slept? Truly, enough to make a little woman out of!

The poverty of my ribs is wonderful!” So many a person of today has spoken.

Yes, you make me laugh, you people of today! And especially when you are amazed at yourselves!

And woe to me if I could not laugh at your amazement, and had to drink down everything repulsive out of your bowls!

But as it is, I will take you more lightly, since I have something heavy to carry; and what does it matter to me if beetles and winged worms settle on my bundle!

Truly, it shall not become heavier for me because of that! And it is not from you, you people of today, that my great weariness shall come.

Ah, where shall I climb now with my longing! From all mountains I look out for fatherlands and motherlands.

But I found a home nowhere: I am restless in all cities, and a departure at all gates.

Strange to me, and a mockery, are the people of today, to whom my heart lately drove me; and I am driven out of fatherlands and motherlands.

So now I love only my children’s land, the undiscovered one, in the farthest sea: for that I bid my sails search and search.

In my children I will make up for being the child of my fathers; and to all the future, for this present!

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
