## Chapter III · The Confession of a Passionate Heart, in Verse

Alyosha, having heard his father’s order, which the old man had shouted to him from the carriage as he drove away from the monastery, stayed for some time where he was in great perplexity. Not that he stood like a post; that never happened to him. On the contrary, for all his uneasiness he managed at once to go to the Father Superior’s kitchen and find out what his papa had done upstairs. Then, however, he set off, hoping that on the way to the town he would somehow manage to solve the problem that was troubling him. I will say beforehand: he was not in the least afraid of his father’s shouts and his order to move home “with your pillows and mattress”. He understood only too well that the order to move, given aloud and with such a show of shouting, had been given “in a fit of enthusiasm”, so to speak even for effect, rather like a tradesman of their little town who, carousing not long ago at his own name-day party, and in front of his guests, getting angry because they would not give him any more vodka, suddenly began smashing his own crockery, tearing his own and his wife’s clothes, breaking his furniture and, finally, the windows in the house, and all again for effect; and of course something of the same kind had happened now with his papa. The next day, of course, the carousing tradesman, sobered up, regretted the broken cups and plates. Alyosha knew that the old man too would certainly let him go back to the monastery the next day, perhaps even today. And besides he was completely sure that his father, whomever else he might wish to offend, would not wish to offend him. Alyosha was sure that no one in the whole world would ever wish to offend him; not only would not wish to, but could not. For him this was an axiom, given once and for all, without reasoning, and in this sense he went forward without any hesitation.

But at this moment another fear was stirring in him, a fear of quite another kind, and all the more tormenting because he could not have defined it himself: namely a fear of a woman, and precisely of Katerina Ivanovna, who had so insistently begged him, in the note passed to him that morning by Madame Khokhlakova, to come and see her for some reason. This request, and the necessity of certainly going, had at once planted a tormenting feeling in his heart, and all morning, the further it went, the more and more painfully this feeling had been aching in him, in spite of all the scenes and adventures that had followed in the monastery, and just now at the Father Superior’s, and so on and so on. He was not afraid because he did not know what she would talk to him about and what he would answer her. And it was not women in general that he feared in her: he knew little of women, of course, but still all his life, from his very infancy until the monastery, he had lived with them alone. He was afraid of this woman, of Katerina Ivanovna herself precisely. He had been afraid of her from the very time he first saw her. He had seen her only once or twice, perhaps three times, and had even once happened to exchange a few words with her. He remembered her image as that of a beautiful, proud and imperious girl. But it was not her beauty that tormented him; it was something else. It was precisely this inexplicability of his fear that now increased his fear. This girl’s aims were most noble, he knew it; she was trying to save his brother Dmitry, who was already guilty before her, and trying out of sheer magnanimity alone. And yet, in spite of his recognition of this and of the justice which he could not help rendering to all these beautiful and magnanimous feelings, a chill ran down his spine the nearer he came to her house.

He reckoned that he would not find his brother Ivan Fyodorovich, who was so close to her, at her house: brother Ivan would surely be with their father now. Still more surely he would not find Dmitry there, and he had a presentiment why. So their conversation would take place alone. He would have very much liked to see his brother Dmitry before this fateful conversation, and run in to see him. Without showing him the letter, he could have exchanged a few words with him. But brother Dmitry lived far away and was surely not at home now either. Having stood still for a minute, he finally made up his mind. Crossing himself with a habitual, hurried sign of the cross, and at once smiling at something, he set off firmly towards his terrible lady.

He knew her house. But if he had gone by the High Street, then across the square, and so on, it would have been rather far. Our small town is extraordinarily spread out, and the distances in it are sometimes quite large. Besides, his father was waiting for him, might not have forgotten his order yet, might turn capricious, and so he had to hurry so as to manage both here and there. As a result of all these considerations he decided to shorten the way by going by the back ways, and he knew all these passages in the little town like the five fingers of his hand. By the back ways meant almost without roads, along deserted fences, sometimes even climbing over strangers’ wattle fences, passing through strangers’ yards, where, however, everyone knew him and everyone greeted him. By this way he could come out onto the High Street in half the distance. At one point he even had to pass very close to his father’s house, namely past the garden next to his father’s, which belonged to a small, dilapidated, crooked little house with four windows. The owner of this little house was, as Alyosha knew, a tradeswoman of the town, an old woman without legs, who lived with her daughter, formerly a civilised housemaid in the capital, who until recently had lived in nothing but generals’ households and had now come home a year ago because of the old woman’s illness, and showed off in smart dresses. This old woman and her daughter, however, had fallen into terrible poverty and even went every day next door to Fyodor Pavlovich’s kitchen for soup and bread. Marfa Ignatievna poured them out some willingly. But the daughter, coming for soup, had not sold a single one of her dresses, and one of them even had a very long train. Alyosha had learned of this last circumstance, quite by chance of course, from his friend Rakitin, to whom absolutely everything in their little town was known, and having learned it, he of course forgot it at once. But now, coming level with the neighbour’s garden, he suddenly remembered precisely that train, quickly raised his drooping and thoughtful head, and... suddenly came upon a most unexpected encounter.

Behind the wattle fence in the neighbour’s garden, perched on something, leaning out to his chest, stood his brother Dmitry Fyodorovich, making signs to him with his hands with all his might, calling him and beckoning to him, evidently afraid not only to shout but even to say a word aloud, lest he be heard. Alyosha at once ran up to the fence.

“It’s good that you looked round yourself, or else I nearly shouted to you,” Dmitry Fyodorovich whispered to him joyfully and hurriedly. “Climb in here! Quickly! Ah, how splendid that you’ve come. I was just thinking about you...”

Alyosha was glad himself, and was only puzzled how to get over the fence. But “Mitya” caught his elbow with a mighty hand and helped him jump. Gathering up his cassock, Alyosha leapt over with the agility of a barefoot town boy.

“Well done, come on!” broke from Mitya in a rapturous whisper.

“Where to?” Alyosha whispered too, looking round on all sides and seeing himself in a completely empty garden, in which there was no one but the two of them. The garden was small, but the owner’s little house still stood at least fifty paces from them. “But there’s no one here; why are you whispering?”

“Why am I whispering? Ah, the devil take it,” Dmitry Fyodorovich suddenly shouted at the top of his voice, “why am I whispering? Well, you see for yourself how a confusion of nature can suddenly come about. I am here in secret, keeping watch on a secret. The explanation later; but, knowing that it’s a secret, I suddenly started talking secretly too, and whispering like a fool, when there’s no need. Come on! Over there! Until then, be quiet. I want to kiss you!

> Glory to the Highest in the world,
> Glory to the Highest in me!..

I was just repeating that, sitting here, before you came...”

The garden was about an acre in size, or a little more, but planted with trees only round the edges, along all four fences: apple trees, maple, lime, birch. The middle of the garden was empty, laid down to a meadow, from which several hundredweight of hay were mown in summer. The owner let the garden out from the spring for a few roubles. There were also beds of raspberries, gooseberries and currants, also all along the fences; vegetable beds close to the house, started only recently, however. Dmitry Fyodorovich led his guest to the corner of the garden furthest from the house. There, among thickly growing limes and old bushes of currant and elder, guelder rose and lilac, there suddenly appeared something like the ruins of a very ancient green summer house, blackened and lopsided, with latticed walls, but with a covered roof, under which one could still shelter from the rain. God knows when the summer house had been built, by tradition about fifty years before, by some owner of the little house at that time, Alexander Karlovich von Schmidt, a retired lieutenant colonel. But everything had already rotted; the floor had decayed, all the floorboards were loose, the wood smelled of damp. In the summer house stood a green wooden table, dug into the ground, and round it ran benches, also green, on which one could still sit. Alyosha had noticed his brother’s rapturous state at once, but on entering the summer house he saw half a bottle of brandy and a little glass on the table.

“It’s brandy!” Mitya burst out laughing. “And you are already looking: ‘drinking again’? Don’t believe the phantom.

> Do not believe the empty, lying crowd,
> forget your doubts...

I am not drinking; I am only ‘savouring’, as that swine of yours Rakitin says, who will become a state councillor and will always say ‘savouring’. Sit down. I would like to take you, Alyoshka, and press you to my breast, so as to crush you, because in the whole world... really... re-al-ly... (take it in! take it in!) I love only you!”

He uttered this last line in a kind of almost frenzy.

“Only you, and one more, a ‘low woman’, with whom I have fallen in love, and with that I am done for. But to fall in love does not mean to love. One can fall in love and hate. Remember that! Now, while I am merry, I’m talking! Sit down here at the table, and I’ll sit beside you, and look at you, and talk all the time. You will be silent all the time, and I will talk all the time, because the time has come. But then, you know, I have decided that I really must speak quietly, because here... here... the most unexpected ears may turn up. I’ll explain everything; it has been said: to be continued. Why have I been longing for you, thirsting for you now, all these days, and now? (I have dropped anchor here for five days already.) All these days? Because I will tell everything to you alone, because I must, because you are needed, because tomorrow I fly down from the clouds, because tomorrow life will end and begin. Have you ever felt, have you ever seen in a dream, how one falls from a mountain into a pit? Well, now I am falling, and not in a dream. And I am not afraid, and don’t you be afraid. That is, I am afraid, but it is sweet to me. That is, not sweet, but rapture... Well, the devil, it makes no difference, whatever happens. A strong spirit, a weak spirit, a woman’s spirit, whatever happens! Let us praise nature: look how much sun there is, how clear the sky, all the leaves are green, it is still quite summer, four o’clock in the afternoon, the stillness! Where were you going?”

“I was going to Father, but first I meant to call on Katerina Ivanovna.”

“To her and to Father! Ooh! A coincidence! Why, what else did I call you for, what else did I want you for, hunger and thirst for with all the curves of my soul and even my ribs? Precisely to send you to Father from me, and then to her, to Katerina Ivanovna, and so be done both with her and with Father. To send an angel. I could have sent anyone, but I needed to send an angel. And here you are going to her and to Father yourself.”

“Did you really mean to send me?” broke from Alyosha, with a pained expression on his face.

“Stop, you knew it. And I see that you understood everything at once. But be quiet, be quiet for now. Don’t pity me and don’t cry!”

Dmitry Fyodorovich stood up, fell to thinking and put a finger to his forehead.

“She called you herself, she wrote you a letter or something, that’s why you were going to her; otherwise would you have gone?”

“Here is the note,” Alyosha took it out of his pocket. Mitya quickly ran through it.

“And you came by the back ways! O gods! I thank you for directing him by the back ways, so that he fell into my hands like the golden fish to the old fool of a fisherman in the fairy tale. Listen, Alyosha, listen, brother. Now I mean to tell everything. For one must tell someone at least. I have told an angel in heaven already, but I must tell an angel on earth too. You are an angel on earth. You will listen, you will judge, and you will forgive... And that is just what I need, for someone higher to forgive me. Listen: if two beings suddenly tear themselves away from everything earthly and fly into the extraordinary, or at least one of them does, and before that, flying away or perishing, he comes to the other and says: do this and that for me, something which is never asked of anyone, but which can be asked only on one’s deathbed; then surely the other will not refuse to do it... if he is a friend, if he is a brother?”

“I will do it, but tell me what it is, and tell me quickly,” said Alyosha.

“Quickly... Hm. Don’t hurry, Alyosha: you are hurrying and worrying. There is no need to hurry now. Now the world has come out onto a new street. Eh, Alyosha, what a pity you have never thought your way to rapture! But then, what am I saying to him? You, not thought your way to it! What am I saying, blockhead that I am:

> Be noble, O man!

Whose verse is that?”

Alyosha resolved to wait. He understood that all his business really was, perhaps, here alone now. Mitya fell to thinking for a minute, leaning his elbow on the table and resting his head on his palm. Both were silent.

“Lyosha,” said Mitya, “you alone won’t laugh! I would like to begin... my confession... with Schiller’s hymn to joy. An die Freude! To Joy! But I don’t know German; I know only that it is an die Freude. And don’t think I am babbling drunk either. I am not drunk at all. Brandy is brandy, but I need two bottles to get drunk:

> And ruddy-faced Silenus
> upon his stumbling ass;

and I haven’t drunk a quarter of a bottle, and I am not Silenus. Not Silenus, but silent-strong, because I have taken a decision for ever. Forgive me the pun; you must forgive me much today, not only a pun. Don’t worry, I am not spreading it thin, I am talking business and I’ll come to the business in a moment. I won’t drag it out of my soul like a miser. Wait, how does it go...”

He raised his head, fell to thinking, and suddenly began rapturously:

> Timid, naked and wild, the troglodyte
> hid in the caves of the rocks;
> the nomad wandered over the fields
> and laid the fields waste.
> The hunter, with spear and arrows,
> ran fearsome through the woods...
> Woe to those cast by the waves
> upon inhospitable shores!
> From the heights of Olympus
> Mother Ceres comes down in search
> of her stolen Proserpina:
> the world lies wild before her.
> No shelter and no welcome
> anywhere for the goddess there;
> and nowhere does a temple
> bear witness to the worship of the gods.
> The fruit of the fields and the sweet grapes
> do not shine at the feasts;
> only the remains of bodies smoke
> upon the bloody altars.
> And wherever Ceres turns
> her sorrowful eye there,
> everywhere she sees man
> in deep humiliation!

Sobs suddenly broke from Mitya’s breast. He seized Alyosha’s hand.

“Friend, friend, in humiliation, in humiliation even now. Man has a terrible lot to bear on earth, terribly many misfortunes! Don’t think that I am only a lout in an officer’s rank who drinks brandy and goes in for debauchery. I, brother, think of almost nothing else, of this humiliated man, if only I am not lying. God grant that I am not lying now and not praising myself. I think about this man because I am such a man myself.

> That man may rise in soul
> out of his baseness,
> let him enter for ever into a union
> with his ancient mother, the earth.

But here is the whole point: how am I to enter into a union with the earth for ever? I don’t kiss the earth, I don’t cut open her breast; what, am I to become a peasant or a shepherd boy? I walk on and don’t know: have I fallen into stench and shame, or into light and joy? There is the trouble, for everything in the world is a riddle! And whenever I happened to sink into the very, very deepest shame of debauchery (and that is all that ever happened to me), I always read this poem about Ceres and about man. Did it reform me? Never! Because I am a Karamazov. Because if I fly into the abyss, I fly straight, head down and heels up, and I am even pleased to be falling in such a humiliating position, and I count it beauty for myself. And in the very depth of that shame I suddenly begin a hymn. Let me be accursed, let me be low and vile, but let me too kiss the hem of the garment in which my God is clothed; let me be following the devil at the same time, but I am still your son too, O Lord, and I love you, and I feel the joy without which the world cannot stand and be.

> Eternal joy gives drink
> to the soul of God’s creation;
> by the secret power of ferment
> it sets the cup of life aflame;
> it lured the little grass to the light,
> unfolded chaos into suns,
> and poured them out in spaces
> beyond the stargazer’s reach.
> At the breast of kindly nature
> all that breathes drinks joy;
> all creatures, all peoples
> she draws after her;
> she gave us friends in misfortune,
> the juice of the grape, the Graces’ garlands,
> to insects, sensual lust...
> and the angel stands before God.

But enough of verses! I have shed tears, and you let me weep. Let it be foolishness at which everyone will laugh, but not you. There, your little eyes are shining too. Enough of verses. I want to tell you now about ‘insects’, about those whom God endowed with sensual lust:

> To insects, sensual lust!

I, brother, am that very insect, and it was said of me specially. And all of us Karamazovs are the same, and in you, angel, that insect lives and raises storms in your blood. Storms, because sensual lust is a storm, more than a storm! Beauty is a terrible and dreadful thing! Terrible because it cannot be defined, and it cannot be defined because God has set nothing but riddles. Here the shores meet, here all contradictions live together. I am very uneducated, brother, but I have thought about this a lot. There are terribly many mysteries! Far too many riddles weigh down man on earth. Solve them as you can and come out of the water dry. Beauty! What I can’t bear, besides, is that a man, even one of the highest heart and of lofty mind, begins with the ideal of the Madonna and ends with the ideal of Sodom. What is still more terrible is when a man who already has the ideal of Sodom in his soul does not renounce the ideal of the Madonna either, and his heart burns with it, and truly, truly burns, as in his young, blameless years. No, man is broad, even too broad; I would narrow him. The devil knows what to make of it, that’s what! What seems shame to the mind is all beauty to the heart. Is there beauty in Sodom? Believe me that it is in Sodom that it sits for the vast majority of people; did you know that secret or not? What is terrible is that beauty is not only a terrible but also a mysterious thing. Here the devil is fighting with God, and the battlefield is the hearts of men. But then, everyone talks about where it hurts him. Listen, now to the business itself.”
