## CHAPTER IV

At that moment the door opened quietly, and a girl came into the room, looking round timidly. Everyone turned to her with surprise and curiosity. Raskolnikov did not recognise her at first glance. It was Sofya Semyonovna Marmeladova. He had seen her yesterday for the first time, but at such a moment, in such surroundings and in such a costume, that his memory had kept the image of quite a different person. Now she was a modestly and even poorly dressed girl, still very young, almost like a child, with a modest and decent manner and a clear but somewhat frightened face. She wore a very simple everyday dress, and on her head an old hat of an outdated style; only in her hands, as yesterday, was a parasol. Seeing unexpectedly a room full of people, she was not so much embarrassed as completely lost; she grew timid, like a small child, and even made a movement to go back out.

“Ah... it’s you?..” said Raskolnikov in extreme surprise, and suddenly he was embarrassed himself.

It at once occurred to him that his mother and sister already knew in passing, from Luzhin’s letter, about a certain young person of “notorious” behaviour. Only just now he had protested against Luzhin’s slander and mentioned that he had seen this girl for the first time, and suddenly she walks in herself. He remembered too that he had not protested at all against the expression “of notorious behaviour”. All this slid through his head vaguely and in an instant. But looking more closely, he suddenly saw that this humiliated being was so humiliated already that he suddenly felt sorry for her. And when she made a movement to run away in fear, something in him seemed to turn over.

“I wasn’t expecting you at all,” he hurried, stopping her with his eyes. “Do me the kindness, sit down. You have come from Katerina Ivanovna, I expect. Allow me, not there, sit here...”

When Sonya came in, Razumikhin, who was sitting on one of Raskolnikov’s three chairs, right by the door, half rose to let her in. At first Raskolnikov had pointed her to a place at the corner of the sofa, where Zosimov had been sitting, but remembering that the sofa was too familiar a place, and served him as a bed, he hastened to point her to Razumikhin’s chair.

“And you sit here,” he said to Razumikhin, putting him in the corner where Zosimov had been sitting.

Sonya sat down, almost trembling with fear, and looked timidly at the two ladies. It was evident that she herself did not understand how she could sit down beside them. Realising this, she was so frightened that she suddenly stood up again and in utter confusion turned to Raskolnikov.

“I... I... have only come in for one minute; forgive me for disturbing you,” she began, stammering. “I have come from Katerina Ivanovna, she had no one else to send... And Katerina Ivanovna told me to beg you very much to be at the funeral service tomorrow, in the morning... at the liturgy... at the Mitrofaniev cemetery, and afterwards at our place... at hers... to have something to eat... To do her the honour... She told me to ask you.”

Sonya stumbled and fell silent.

“I will try without fail... without fail,” answered Raskolnikov, half rising too, stammering too, and not finishing his words... “Do me the kindness, sit down,” he said suddenly. “I must talk with you. Please, perhaps you are in a hurry, do me the kindness, give me two minutes...”

And he moved a chair towards her. Sonya sat down again and again glanced timidly, lost, quickly, at the two ladies, and suddenly lowered her eyes.

Raskolnikov’s pale face flushed; something seemed to jerk through him; his eyes lit up.

“Mama,” he said firmly and insistently, “this is Sofya Semyonovna Marmeladova, the daughter of that same unfortunate Mr Marmeladov who was run over by horses yesterday before my eyes, and of whom I have already told you...”

Pulkheria Alexandrovna looked at Sonya and narrowed her eyes slightly. In spite of all her confusion before Rodya’s insistent and challenging look, she could not deny herself this pleasure. Dunechka fixed her eyes seriously and intently on the poor girl’s face and examined her in perplexity. Sonya, hearing herself introduced, raised her eyes again, but was more embarrassed than before.

“I wanted to ask you,” Raskolnikov turned to her quickly, “how did things go for you today? Did they trouble you?.. the police, for instance.”

“No, sir, it all went off... It was too plain, after all, what the death was from; they didn’t trouble us; only the lodgers are angry.”

“Why?”

“Because the body has been lying so long... it’s hot now, the smell... so today, by vespers, they will carry it to the cemetery, to the chapel, until tomorrow. Katerina Ivanovna didn’t want it at first, but now she sees herself that it can’t be helped...”

“Today, then?”

“She asks you to do us the honour of being at the funeral service in the church tomorrow, and then to come to her place, to the memorial meal.”

“She is giving a memorial meal?”

“Yes, sir, a little meal; she told me to thank you very much for helping us yesterday... without you we would have had nothing at all to bury him with.” And her lips and chin suddenly began to quiver, but she controlled herself and held it back, quickly lowering her eyes to the ground again.

While they talked, Raskolnikov was examining her closely. It was a thin, quite thin and pale little face, rather irregular, somehow sharp, with a sharp little nose and chin. She could not even be called pretty, but her blue eyes were so clear, and when they came alive, the expression of her face became so kind and so simple-hearted that one was drawn to her in spite of oneself. In her face, and in her whole figure, there was besides one special characteristic feature: in spite of her eighteen years she seemed almost still a little girl, much younger than her years, almost quite a child, and this sometimes showed itself even comically in some of her movements.

“But can Katerina Ivanovna really manage with such small means, and even intends to give a meal?..” asked Raskolnikov, insistently keeping up the conversation.

“The coffin will be a plain one, sir... and everything will be plain, so it won’t cost much... Katerina Ivanovna and I worked it all out earlier, so there will be something left for the memorial... and Katerina Ivanovna very much wants it to be so. You see, it can’t be helped, sir... it’s a comfort to her... she is like that, you know...”

“I understand, I understand... of course... Why are you looking round my room like that? Mama says too that it looks like a coffin.”

“You gave us everything yesterday!” Sonechka said suddenly in reply, in a kind of strong, rapid whisper, and suddenly looked down hard again. Her lips and chin began to quiver again. She had been struck for some time by Raskolnikov’s poor surroundings, and now these words burst out of her of themselves. A silence followed. Dunechka’s eyes somehow grew brighter, and Pulkheria Alexandrovna even looked at Sonya kindly.

“Rodya,” she said, standing up, “of course we shall dine together. Come, Dunechka... And you, Rodya, should go out for a little walk, and then rest, lie down, and then come to us soon... We have tired you out, I am afraid...”

“Yes, yes, I’ll come,” he answered, standing up and hurrying... “I have some business, though...”

“Surely you are not going to dine separately?” cried Razumikhin, looking at Raskolnikov in surprise. “What’s the matter with you?”

“Yes, yes, I’ll come, of course, of course... And you stay a minute. You don’t need him just now, do you, Mama? Or am I taking him from you?”

“Oh no, no! And you, Dmitry Prokofich, will you come to dinner, will you be so kind?”

“Please do come,” Dunya asked.

Razumikhin bowed and beamed all over. For a moment everyone was suddenly and strangely embarrassed.

“Goodbye, Rodya, that is, until we meet; I don’t like saying ‘goodbye’. Goodbye, Nastasya... ah, I’ve said ‘goodbye’ again!..”

Pulkheria Alexandrovna meant to bow to Sonechka too, but somehow did not manage it, and hurried out of the room.

But Avdotya Romanovna seemed to have been waiting her turn, and, passing Sonya as she followed her mother, she made her an attentive, courteous and full bow. Sonechka was confused and bowed in a hurried, frightened way; something even like pain was reflected in her face, as if Avdotya Romanovna’s courtesy and attention were burdensome and tormenting to her.

“Dunya, goodbye, then!” Raskolnikov called out, already in the passage. “Give me your hand!”

“But I did give it to you, have you forgotten?” answered Dunya, turning to him tenderly and awkwardly.

“Well, what of it, give it again!”

And he pressed her little fingers hard. Dunechka smiled at him, blushed, quickly pulled her hand away and went after her mother, also, for some reason, completely happy.

“Well, that’s splendid!” he said to Sonya, coming back into his room and looking at her brightly. “God rest the dead, but the living must go on living! Isn’t that so? Isn’t it? It is, isn’t it?”

Sonya looked with something like surprise at his suddenly brightened face; for a few moments he gazed at her silently and intently: the whole story her late father had told about her swept suddenly through his memory at that moment...

“Lord, Dunechka!” Pulkheria Alexandrovna began at once, as soon as they were out in the street. “Here I am now actually glad we have left: it’s somehow easier. Well, did I think yesterday, in the train, that I would be glad even of this!”

“I tell you again, Mama, he is still very ill. Can’t you see? Perhaps he upset himself suffering for our sake. One must be indulgent, and much, much can be forgiven.”

“But you weren’t indulgent yourself!” Pulkheria Alexandrovna interrupted at once, hotly and jealously. “You know, Dunya, I was looking at you both: you are his perfect portrait, and not so much in face as in soul: you are both melancholics, both gloomy and hot-tempered, both proud and both generous... Surely he can’t be an egoist, Dunechka? eh?.. And when I think of what is going to happen at our place this evening, my whole heart sinks!”

“Don’t worry, Mama; what must be will be.”

“Dunechka! But just think what a position we are in now! Well, what if Pyotr Petrovich breaks it off?” poor Pulkheria Alexandrovna suddenly blurted out incautiously.

“Then what will he be worth after that!” Dunechka answered sharply and contemptuously.

“We did well to leave just now,” Pulkheria Alexandrovna hurried on, interrupting. “He was hurrying off somewhere on business; let him take a walk, at least breathe some air... it’s dreadfully stuffy in his room... but where is one to breathe air here? Here even the streets are like rooms without windows. Lord, what a city!.. Wait, step aside, they’ll crush us, they’re carrying something! Why, it was a piano they carried past, really... how they push... I am very much afraid of that girl too...”

“What girl, Mama?”

“Why, that one, Sofya Semyonovna, who was there just now...”

“Afraid of what?”

“I have a presentiment, Dunya. Well, believe it or not, the moment she came in, I thought at once that this is where the main thing lies...”

“Nothing lies there at all!” Dunya cried in vexation. “What you are like with your presentiments, Mama! He has known her only since yesterday, and just now, when she came in, he didn’t recognise her.”

“Well, you will see!.. She troubles me; you’ll see, you’ll see! And how frightened I was: she looks at me and looks, such eyes, I could hardly stay on my chair, do you remember when he began introducing her? And it seems strange to me: Pyotr Petrovich writes about her like that, and he introduces her to us, and to you too! So she must be dear to him!”

“What does it matter what he writes! People talked about us too, and wrote, have you forgotten? And I am sure that she is... a fine person, and that all this is nonsense!”

“God grant it!”

“And Pyotr Petrovich is a worthless gossip,” Dunechka suddenly snapped.

Pulkheria Alexandrovna simply shrank. The conversation broke off.

“Here’s what, here’s the business I have with you...” said Raskolnikov, drawing Razumikhin aside to the window...

“Then I will tell Katerina Ivanovna that you will come...” Sonya hurried, bowing in order to leave.

“In a moment, Sofya Semyonovna; we have no secrets, you are not in the way... I would like to say two more words to you... Here’s what,” he turned suddenly to Razumikhin, without finishing, as if breaking off. “You know that... what’s his name!.. Porfiry Petrovich?”

“I should think so! He’s a relation. Why?” the other added with a kind of burst of curiosity.

“He is the one handling that case now... well, the one about the murder... that you were talking about yesterday... isn’t he?”

“Yes... well?” Razumikhin suddenly goggled.

“He was questioning the people who had pledges there, and I have pledges there too, just rubbish, but still, my sister’s little ring that she gave me as a keepsake when I left for here, and my father’s silver watch. It’s all worth five or six roubles, but it’s dear to me, a keepsake. So what am I to do now? I don’t want the things to be lost, especially the watch. I was trembling earlier in case Mother asked to see it, when they began talking about Dunechka’s watch. It is the only thing left after my father. She will fall ill if it is lost! Women! So how am I to do it, teach me! I know I ought to report it at the police station. But wouldn’t it be better to go to Porfiry himself, eh? What do you think? I would like to get the business done as soon as possible. You’ll see, Mama will ask before dinner!”

“Not the police station on any account, Porfiry without fail!” cried Razumikhin in a kind of extraordinary excitement. “Well, how glad I am! Why wait, let’s go now, it’s two steps, we’ll be sure to find him in!”

“Very well... let’s go...”

“And he will be very, very, very, very glad to meet you! I have talked to him a lot about you at various times... And I talked about you yesterday. Let’s go!.. So you knew the old woman? That’s it!.. It’s all turned out mag-ni-fi-cently!.. Ah, yes... Sofya Ivanovna...”

“Sofya Semyonovna,” Raskolnikov corrected him. “Sofya Semyonovna, this is my friend Razumikhin, and he is a good man...”

“If you have to go now...” Sonya began, without even looking at Razumikhin, and becoming still more embarrassed because of it.

“And let us go!” Raskolnikov decided. “I will come to see you today, Sofya Semyonovna; only tell me where you live.”

He was not exactly confused, but seemed to be in a hurry and to avoid her eyes. Sonya gave her address, and blushed as she did so. They all went out together.

“Don’t you lock your door?” asked Razumikhin, coming down the stairs after them.

“Never!.. Though I have been meaning to buy a lock for two years now,” he added carelessly. “Happy people, aren’t they, who have nothing to lock up?” he turned to Sonya, laughing.

In the street they stopped in the gateway.

“You go to the right, Sofya Semyonovna? By the way: how did you find me?” he asked, as if wanting to say something quite different to her. He kept wanting to look into her quiet, clear eyes, and somehow it never quite came off...

“Why, you told Polechka your address yesterday.”

“Polya? Ah, yes... Polechka! That’s... the little one... she is your sister? So I gave her my address?”

“But have you forgotten?”

“No... I remember...”

“And I had heard about you from my late father, at that time... Only I didn’t know your surname then, and he didn’t know it himself... And now I came... and since I learned your surname yesterday... I asked today: where does Mr Raskolnikov live?.. And I didn’t know that you too live as a lodger... Goodbye, sir... I’ll tell Katerina Ivanovna...”

She was terribly glad that she had got away at last; she walked off with her eyes down, hurrying, so as to get out of their sight as quickly as she could, to get through those twenty steps to the turning on the right into the street as quickly as possible and be alone at last, and there, walking, hurrying, looking at no one, noticing nothing, to think, to remember, to weigh every word that had been said, every circumstance. Never, never had she felt anything like it. A whole new world had come down into her soul, unknown and dim. She suddenly remembered that Raskolnikov himself meant to come to see her today, perhaps in the morning, perhaps now!

“Only not today, please, not today!” she muttered with a sinking heart, as if begging someone, like a frightened child. “Lord! To me... to that room... he will see... oh Lord!”

And of course she could not notice at that moment a certain gentleman unknown to her who was diligently watching her and following on her heels. He had followed her from the moment she came out of the gate. At the moment when all three of them, Razumikhin, Raskolnikov and she, had stopped on the pavement to exchange a few words, this passer-by, going round them, had suddenly given a kind of start, catching by chance, in passing, Sonya’s words: “and I asked: where does Mr Raskolnikov live?” He looked quickly but attentively at all three, especially at Raskolnikov, to whom Sonya was speaking; then he looked at the house and noted it. All this was done in an instant, in passing, and the passer-by, trying not even to show it, walked on, slackening his pace as if waiting. He was waiting for Sonya; he saw that they were saying goodbye and that Sonya would now go home somewhere.

“So where is home? I have seen that face somewhere,” he thought, recalling Sonya’s face... “I must find out.”

When he reached the turning he crossed to the opposite side of the street, turned round, and saw that Sonya was already coming after him along the same way, noticing nothing. When she reached the turning, she too turned into this same street. He followed, not taking his eyes off her from the opposite pavement; after about fifty paces he crossed back to the side Sonya was walking on, caught up with her, and walked behind her, keeping five paces away.

He was a man of about fifty, rather above middle height, portly, with broad, steep shoulders, which gave him a somewhat stooping look. He was dressed smartly and comfortably, and looked like a dignified gentleman. In his hands was a handsome cane, which he tapped on the pavement at every step, and his hands were in fresh gloves. His broad face with its high cheekbones was rather pleasant, and his complexion was fresh, not a Petersburg one. His hair, still very thick, was quite fair, with perhaps just a touch of grey, and his broad, thick beard, falling like a spade, was even lighter than the hair on his head. His eyes were blue and looked coldly, intently and thoughtfully; his lips were scarlet. Altogether he was an excellently preserved man who seemed much younger than his years.

When Sonya came out onto the canal, the two of them found themselves alone on the pavement. Watching her, he had managed to notice her absorption and absent-mindedness. On reaching her house, Sonya turned in at the gate, and he after her, seeming somewhat surprised. Entering the courtyard, she turned right, into the corner where the staircase to her lodging was. “Ba!” muttered the unknown gentleman, and began to climb the stairs after her. Only then did Sonya notice him. She went up to the third floor, turned into the gallery and rang at number nine, on whose door was written in chalk: “Kapernaumov, Tailor.” “Ba!” the stranger said again, surprised at the strange coincidence, and rang next door, at number eight. The two doors were about six paces from each other.

“You lodge at Kapernaumov’s!” he said, looking at Sonya and laughing. “He altered a waistcoat for me yesterday. And I am here, next door to you, at Madame Resslich’s, Gertrude Karlovna’s. How it falls out!”

Sonya looked at him attentively.

“Neighbours,” he went on, somehow especially merrily. “I have only been in town three days, you know. Well, until we meet again.”

Sonya did not answer; the door was opened, and she slipped inside. For some reason she felt ashamed, and as if she had grown timid...

On the way to Porfiry’s, Razumikhin was in a particularly excited state.

“This is splendid, brother,” he repeated several times, “and I’m glad! I’m glad!”

“But what are you glad about?” Raskolnikov thought to himself.

“I didn’t know that you too pawned things with the old woman. And... and... was it long ago? That is, was it long ago that you were at her place?”

“What a naive fool he is!”

“When?..” Raskolnikov stopped, trying to remember. “Why, I was at her place about three days before her death, I think. Though I am not going to redeem the things now,” he took up, with a kind of hurried and peculiar concern about the things, “because again I have only one silver rouble... because of that cursed delirium yesterday!..”

He pronounced the word delirium especially impressively.

“Well, yes, yes, yes,” Razumikhin agreed hurriedly, about what was unclear. “So that is why it... struck you somewhat then... and you know, even in your delirium you kept mentioning some rings and chains!.. Well, yes, yes... That’s clear, it’s all clear now.”

“There you are! How that idea has spread among them! Here is a man who would go to be crucified for me, and yet he is very glad that it has been cleared up why I mentioned rings in my delirium! How firmly it has taken hold of them all!..”

“And shall we find him in?” he asked aloud.

“We shall, we shall,” Razumikhin hurried on. “He’s a splendid fellow, brother, you’ll see! A bit clumsy, that is, he is a man of the world too, but I mean clumsy in another respect. A clever fellow, clever, very far from stupid even, only he has a peculiar turn of mind... Mistrustful, a sceptic, a cynic... he likes to deceive, that is, not deceive, but to make a fool of people... And the old material method... But he knows his business, he knows it... Last year he cleared up one murder case in which almost all the traces had been lost! He very, very, very much wants to meet you!”

“But why on earth so very much?”

“That is, not exactly... you see, lately, since you fell ill, I have had to mention you often and a lot... Well, he listened... and when he learned that you were in law and couldn’t finish the course, because of circumstances, he said: ‘What a pity!’ And I concluded... that is, all this together, not only that; yesterday Zamyotov... You see, Rodya, I was babbling something to you yesterday when I was drunk, on the way home... so I am afraid, brother, that you may have exaggerated it, you see...”

“What? That they think I’m mad? Well, perhaps it’s true.”

He gave a strained smile.

“Yes... yes... that is, pah, no!.. Well, everything I said (and about the other thing too) was all nonsense and drunken talk.”

“But why are you apologising! How sick I am of all this!” cried Raskolnikov with exaggerated irritability. He was, however, partly pretending.

“I know, I know, I understand. Be sure that I understand. It’s shameful even to speak of it...”

“If it’s shameful, then don’t speak of it!”

Both fell silent. Razumikhin was more than delighted, and Raskolnikov felt it with disgust. He was disturbed too by what Razumikhin had just been saying about Porfiry.

“With this one too I shall have to sing a sob song,” he thought, turning pale and with his heart thumping, “and sing it more naturally. The most natural thing would be to sing nothing at all. To sing nothing on purpose! No, on purpose would be unnatural again... Well, we’ll see how it turns out... we’ll see... now... is it good or bad that I am going? The moth flies to the candle by itself. My heart is pounding, that is what is bad!..”

“In this grey house,” said Razumikhin.

“The most important thing is: does Porfiry know or not that I was in that witch’s flat yesterday... and asked about the blood? I must find that out in an instant, from the first step, as soon as I go in, find it out from his face; oth-er-wise... even if I perish, I shall find out!”

“And you know what?” he turned suddenly to Razumikhin with a sly smile. “I noticed today, brother, that you have been in an extraordinary agitation since the morning. Is it true?”

“What agitation? I’m not in any agitation at all,” Razumikhin winced.

“No, brother, really, it shows. You were sitting on your chair earlier as you never sit, somehow on the very edge, and you kept twitching. You jumped up for no reason at all. Now angry, and then suddenly your mug turns into the sweetest sugar candy, for some reason. You even blushed; especially when you were invited to dinner, you blushed terribly.”

“Not at all; you’re lying!.. What are you talking about?”

“But why are you wriggling like a schoolboy! Pah, the devil, he’s blushing again!”

“What a swine you are, though!”

“But why are you so embarrassed? Romeo! Wait, I’ll tell this here and there today, ha-ha-ha! Won’t I make Mama laugh... and someone else too...”

“Listen, listen, listen, this is serious, this is... What will happen after this, the devil!” Razumikhin was completely thrown, going cold with horror. “What will you tell them? I, brother... Pah, what a swine you are!”

“A real spring rose! And how it suits you, if you only knew; a Romeo nearly seven feet tall! And how you washed yourself today, you even cleaned your nails, eh? When did that ever happen? And by God, you’ve put pomade on your hair! Bend down!”

“Swine!!!”

Raskolnikov laughed so much that it seemed he could no longer restrain himself, and so, laughing, they entered Porfiry Petrovich’s flat. That was just what Raskolnikov wanted: from inside the rooms it could be heard that they had come in laughing and were still guffawing in the hall.

“Not a word here, or I’ll... smash you!” Razumikhin whispered in fury, seizing Raskolnikov by the shoulder.
