## CHAPTER V

Raskolnikov was already going into the rooms. He went in looking as though he were holding himself back with all his strength from bursting out laughing somehow. Behind him, with a completely upside-down and ferocious face, red as a peony, lanky and awkward, came the ashamed Razumikhin. His face and his whole figure were really comical at that moment and justified Raskolnikov’s laughter. Raskolnikov, not yet introduced, bowed to the host, who was standing in the middle of the room looking at them questioningly, held out his hand and shook his, still with a visible extreme effort to suppress his merriment and to get out at least two or three words to introduce himself. But hardly had he managed to put on a serious look and mutter something, when suddenly, as if involuntarily, he glanced at Razumikhin again, and now he could not hold out: the suppressed laughter burst out all the more irresistibly for having been held back so hard until then. The extraordinary ferocity with which Razumikhin received this “heartfelt” laughter gave the whole scene an air of the most sincere merriment and, above all, naturalness. Razumikhin, as if on purpose, helped matters further.

“Pah, the devil!” he roared, waving his arm, and struck it right against a small round table on which stood a finished glass of tea. Everything went flying and ringing.

“But why break the chairs, gentlemen? It’s a loss to the Treasury!” Porfiry Petrovich shouted merrily.

The scene looked like this: Raskolnikov was finishing his laugh, forgetting his hand in his host’s hand, but, knowing the measure, was waiting for the moment to end it as quickly and naturally as possible. Razumikhin, completely embarrassed by the fall of the table and the broken glass, looked gloomily at the fragments, spat, and turned sharply to the window, where he stood with his back to the company, with a terribly frowning face, looking out of the window and seeing nothing. Porfiry Petrovich was laughing and wanted to laugh, but it was evident that he needed explanations. In the corner, on a chair, sat Zamyotov, who had half risen when the guests came in and was standing in expectation, his mouth stretched in a smile, but looking at the whole scene with perplexity and even as if with distrust, and at Raskolnikov even with a kind of embarrassment. Zamyotov’s unexpected presence struck Raskolnikov unpleasantly.

“That’s something else to think over!” he thought.

“Excuse me, please,” he began, with studied embarrassment, “Raskolnikov...”

“Not at all, very pleased, sir, and you came in so pleasantly... Well, doesn’t he even want to say good-day?” Porfiry Petrovich nodded at Razumikhin.

“By God, I don’t know why he’s so furious with me. I only told him on the way that he looked like Romeo, and... and proved it, and nothing more happened, I think.”

“Swine!” Razumikhin responded, without turning round.

“Then he must have had very serious reasons to get so angry over one little word,” Porfiry laughed.

“Well, you! examining magistrate!.. Well, the devil take the lot of you!” snapped Razumikhin, and suddenly, laughing himself, with a brightened face, as if nothing had happened, he went up to Porfiry Petrovich.

“Enough! We’re all fools; to business: here is a friend, Rodion Romanych Raskolnikov; in the first place, he has heard about you and wanted to meet you, and in the second, he has a little matter of business with you. Ba! Zamyotov! How do you come to be here? Do you two know each other? Since when?”

“What now!” Raskolnikov thought anxiously.

Zamyotov seemed embarrassed, but not very.

“We met at your place yesterday,” he said casually.

“Then God has saved me trouble: last week he begged me terribly to introduce him to you somehow, Porfiry, and you have sniffed each other out without me... Where is your tobacco?”

Porfiry Petrovich was dressed for home, in a dressing gown, very clean linen and trodden-down slippers. He was a man of about thirty-five, below middle height, stout and even with a little paunch, clean-shaven, with no moustache or side-whiskers, his hair cut close on a large round head that was somehow especially bulging and rounded at the back. His plump, round, slightly snub-nosed face was of a sickly, dark yellow colour, but rather lively and even mocking. It would even have been good-natured, had it not been for the expression of his eyes, which had a kind of liquid, watery gleam and were covered by almost white lashes that kept blinking, as if winking at someone. The look of those eyes was strangely out of harmony with his whole figure, which even had something womanish about it, and gave it something much more serious than one might have expected at first sight.

As soon as Porfiry Petrovich heard that his guest had a “little matter” for him, he at once asked him to sit on the sofa, sat down himself at the other end, and stared at his guest in immediate expectation of his stating his business, with that intense and all too serious attention which oppresses and embarrasses one from the very first, especially with a stranger, and especially if what you are stating is, in your own opinion, far out of proportion to such extraordinarily important attention being paid to you. But Raskolnikov explained his business clearly and precisely in short, coherent words, and was so pleased with himself that he even managed to take a fairly good look at Porfiry. Porfiry Petrovich did not take his eyes off him once the whole time either. Razumikhin, settling opposite at the same table, followed the statement of the business hotly and impatiently, moving his eyes every minute from one to the other and back again, which was already going a little beyond the measure.

“Fool!” Raskolnikov swore to himself.

“You should give notice to the police,” Porfiry answered with the most businesslike air, “stating, sir, that, having learned of such-and-such an incident, that is, of this murder, you request in your turn that the investigator in charge of the case be informed that such-and-such things belong to you and that you wish to redeem them... or something of that kind... but they will write it for you.”

“That is just the point, that at the present moment,” Raskolnikov did his best to look as embarrassed as possible, “I am not quite in funds... and can’t manage even such a trifle... I would like, you see, only to declare now that these things are mine, but that when I have the money...”

“That makes no difference, sir,” answered Porfiry Petrovich, receiving the explanation about finances coldly. “But then you can also write to me directly, if you like, to the same effect: that, having learned of such-and-such, and declaring such-and-such things of mine, I request...”

“On ordinary paper, then?” Raskolnikov hastened to interrupt, again interested in the financial side of the matter.

“Oh, on the most ordinary, sir!” And suddenly Porfiry Petrovich looked at him in a somehow openly mocking way, narrowing his eyes and as if winking at him. Perhaps, however, it only seemed so to Raskolnikov, because it lasted only an instant. At any rate there was something of the kind. Raskolnikov would have sworn that he had winked at him, the devil knows why.

“He knows!” flashed through him like lightning.

“Forgive me for troubling you with such trifles,” he went on, somewhat thrown, “my things are worth only five roubles, but they are especially dear to me as a memory of those from whom they came, and I confess that when I heard, I was very frightened...”

“That’s why you started up so yesterday when I let slip to Zosimov that Porfiry was questioning the people with pledges!” Razumikhin put in, with obvious intent.

This was already unbearable. Raskolnikov could not stand it, and his black eyes, lit up with anger, flashed at him viciously. At once he recollected himself.

“You seem to be making fun of me, brother?” he turned to him with skilfully manufactured irritation. “I agree that perhaps, in your eyes, I care too much about such rubbish; but you cannot think me either selfish or greedy for that, and in my eyes these two trifling little things may not be rubbish at all. I told you just now that this silver watch, which isn’t worth a farthing, is the only thing left after my father. Laugh at me, but my mother has come to see me,” he turned suddenly to Porfiry, “and if she knew,” he turned quickly back to Razumikhin, taking special care to make his voice tremble, “that this watch was lost, I swear she would be in despair! Women!”

“But not at all! I didn’t mean it that way at all! Quite the contrary!” shouted the distressed Razumikhin.

“Was that good? Natural? Did I overdo it?” Raskolnikov trembled inwardly. “Why did I say ‘women’?”

“So your mother has come to see you?” Porfiry Petrovich inquired, for some reason.

“Yes.”

“When was that, sir?”

“Last night.”

Porfiry was silent, as if considering.

“Your things could not have been lost in any case,” he went on calmly and coldly. “I have been expecting you here for a long time, you know.”

And as if nothing had happened he began carefully placing an ashtray for Razumikhin, who was mercilessly dropping cigarette ash on the carpet. Raskolnikov started, but Porfiry seemed not even to be looking, still taken up with Razumikhin’s cigarette.

“Wha-at? Expecting him! Then did you know that he pawned things there too?” cried Razumikhin.

Porfiry Petrovich turned straight to Raskolnikov:

“Both your things, the ring and the watch, were at her place wrapped in one piece of paper, and on the paper your name was clearly written in pencil, as was the date of the month when she received them from you...”

“How observant you are!..” Raskolnikov began with an awkward smile, trying especially hard to look him straight in the eyes; but he could not hold back and suddenly added: “I remarked on it just now because there were probably a great many people with pledges... so that it would be hard for you to remember them all... But you, on the contrary, remember them all so distinctly, and... and...”

“Stupid! Weak! Why did I add that!”

“But almost all the people with pledges are known now, so that you are the only one who has not been pleased to call,” answered Porfiry, with a barely noticeable shade of mockery.

“I was not quite well.”

“I heard that too, sir. I heard even that you were very much upset by something. You still look rather pale, don’t you?”

“Not pale at all... on the contrary, perfectly well!” Raskolnikov snapped rudely and viciously, suddenly changing his tone. Anger was boiling up in him and he could not suppress it. “And in anger I shall give myself away!” flashed through him again. “But why are they tormenting me!..”

“Not quite well!” Razumikhin took it up. “What a thing to come out with! Up to yesterday he was raving almost unconscious... Well, would you believe it, Porfiry, he could barely stand, and as soon as we, Zosimov and I, turned our backs yesterday, he dressed and slipped away on the quiet and roamed about somewhere till nearly midnight, and that in a state of complete delirium, I tell you; can you imagine it! A most remarkable case!”

“And really in complete delirium? You don’t say!” Porfiry shook his head with a kind of womanish gesture.

“Eh, nonsense! Don’t believe it! But then, you don’t believe it anyway!” broke from Raskolnikov, far too angrily. But Porfiry Petrovich seemed not to have heard these strange words.

“But how could you have gone out if you weren’t delirious?” Razumikhin suddenly grew heated. “Why did you go out? What for?.. And why on the sly? Well, did you have any common sense in you then? Now that the danger is all past, I tell you straight!”

“I got very sick of them yesterday,” Raskolnikov turned suddenly to Porfiry with an insolently challenging smile, “and I ran away from them to rent a flat where they wouldn’t find me, and I took a pile of money with me. Mr Zamyotov over there saw the money. Well, Mr Zamyotov, was I sensible yesterday or delirious? Settle the argument, will you?”

He could, it seemed, have strangled Zamyotov at that moment. He disliked his look and his silence far too much.

“In my opinion you spoke very sensibly, sir, and even cunningly, sir; only you were much too irritable,” Zamyotov declared drily.

“And today Nikodim Fomich told me,” put in Porfiry Petrovich, “that he met you yesterday, very late, in the lodging of an official who had been run over by horses...”

“Well, take that official!” Razumikhin took it up. “Well, weren’t you mad at the official’s? You gave your last money to the widow for the funeral! Well, if you wanted to help, give fifteen, give twenty, but at least keep three roubles for yourself, and instead he forked out all twenty-five just like that!”

“But perhaps I found a treasure somewhere and you don’t know? So yesterday I was generous... Mr Zamyotov over there knows that I found a treasure!.. Forgive us, please,” he turned to Porfiry with quivering lips, “for troubling you for half an hour with such trivial chatter. You’re sick of us, aren’t you?”

“Not at all, sir; on the contrary, on the co-o-ontrary! If you knew how you interest me! It is curious both to look and to listen... and I confess I am so glad that you have been pleased to call at last...”

“But give us some tea at least! My throat is parched!” cried Razumikhin.

“An excellent idea! Perhaps everyone will keep you company. But wouldn’t you like... something more substantial, before the tea?”

“Get along with you!”

Porfiry Petrovich went out to order tea.

Thoughts whirled like a whirlwind in Raskolnikov’s head. He was terribly irritated.

“The main thing is that they don’t even hide it, and don’t want to stand on ceremony! And on what occasion, if you don’t know me at all, did you talk about me with Nikodim Fomich? So they don’t even want to hide that they are tracking me like a pack of dogs! They spit in my face just as openly as that!” He was trembling with fury. “Well, strike straight out, don’t play with me like a cat with a mouse. That is impolite, Porfiry Petrovich, I may not allow it yet, sir!.. I’ll get up and blurt out the whole truth in all your faces; and you’ll see how I despise you!..” He drew breath with difficulty. “And what if it only seems so to me? What if it is a mirage, and I am mistaken in everything, getting angry from inexperience, not keeping up my vile part? Perhaps it is all without intent? All their words are ordinary, but there is something in them... All this can always be said, but there is something. Why did he say straight out ‘at her place’? Why did Zamyotov add that I spoke cunningly? Why are they speaking in such a tone? Yes... the tone... Razumikhin was sitting right here, why doesn’t anything seem so to him? Nothing ever seems anything to that innocent blockhead! The fever again!.. Did Porfiry wink at me earlier or not? Nonsense, surely; why would he wink? Do they want to irritate my nerves, or are they teasing me? Either it’s all a mirage, or they know!.. Even Zamyotov is insolent... Is Zamyotov insolent? Zamyotov has thought things over during the night. I had a presentiment that he would think things over! He is at home here, and yet it’s his first time. Porfiry doesn’t treat him as a guest, sits with his back to him. They’ve sniffed each other out! Certainly they have sniffed each other out because of me! Certainly they were talking about me before we came!.. Do they know about the flat? If only it would come quickly!.. When I said I ran away yesterday to rent a flat, he let it pass, didn’t take it up... And that was clever of me, slipping in the flat: it will be useful later!.. In delirium, he says!.. Ha-ha-ha! He knows about the whole of yesterday evening! He didn’t know about my mother’s arrival!.. And the witch even wrote down the date in pencil!.. You’re lying, I won’t give in! These are not facts yet, this is only a mirage! No, give me facts! And the flat is not a fact, it’s delirium; I know what to tell them... Do they know about the flat? I won’t leave without finding out! Why did I come? But the fact that I am angry now, that perhaps is a fact! Pah, how irritable I am! But perhaps that’s good; the part of a sick man... He is feeling me out. He will try to trip me up. Why did I come?”

All this swept through his head like lightning.

Porfiry Petrovich came back in an instant. He had suddenly somehow grown merry.

“My head, brother, since your party yesterday... And I am all unscrewed somehow,” he began in quite a different tone, laughing, to Razumikhin.

“Well, was it interesting? I left you yesterday at the most interesting point, didn’t I? Who won?”

“Why, no one, of course. We got onto the eternal questions, soared in the clouds.”

“Imagine, Rodya, what we got onto yesterday: is there such a thing as crime or not? I told you they talked themselves into lies like devils!”

“What’s surprising about that? An ordinary social question,” Raskolnikov answered absently.

“The question wasn’t formulated quite like that,” Porfiry remarked.

“Not quite like that, it’s true,” Razumikhin agreed at once, hurrying and growing heated as usual. “You see, Rodion: listen and give your opinion. I want you to. I was jumping out of my skin with them yesterday and waiting for you; I told them about you too, that you would come... It began with the view of the socialists. The view is well known: crime is a protest against the abnormality of the social order, and that’s all, and nothing more, and no other causes are admitted, nothing!..”

“There you’ve lied!” cried Porfiry Petrovich. He was visibly growing livelier and kept laughing, looking at Razumikhin, which fired him up still more.

“N-nothing is admitted!” Razumikhin interrupted hotly. “I’m not lying!.. I’ll show you their books: with them everything is because ‘the environment has eaten him up’, and nothing more! Their favourite phrase! From which it follows directly that if society is organised normally, all crimes will vanish at once, since there will be nothing to protest against, and everyone will become righteous in an instant. Human nature is not taken into account, nature is banished, nature is not supposed to exist! With them it is not humanity, having developed by a living, historical path to the end, that will at last turn of itself into a normal society; on the contrary, a social system, coming out of some mathematical head, will at once organise the whole of humanity and make it righteous and sinless in an instant, before any living process, without any historical and living path! That is why they instinctively dislike history so much: ‘nothing in it but ugliness and stupidity’, and everything is explained by stupidity alone! That is why they so dislike the living process of life: no need for a living soul! A living soul will demand life, a living soul won’t obey mechanics, a living soul is suspicious, a living soul is reactionary! But this other thing, even if it smells a little of carrion, can be made out of rubber, and at least it is not alive, at least it has no will, at least it is servile, it won’t rebel! And the result is that they have reduced everything to the laying of bricks and the arrangement of corridors and rooms in a phalanstery! The phalanstery is ready, all right, but your nature is not yet ready for the phalanstery; it wants life, it hasn’t yet completed the process of life, it’s too early for the graveyard! You can’t leap over nature with logic alone! Logic will foresee three cases, but there are a million of them! Cut off the whole million and reduce everything to the one question of comfort! The easiest solution of the problem! Temptingly clear, and no need to think! The main thing is no need to think! The whole mystery of life fits on two printed sheets!”

“There, he’s burst loose, he’s drumming away! We must hold him by the arms,” Porfiry laughed. “Imagine,” he turned to Raskolnikov, “it was just like this yesterday evening, in one room, six voices at once, and he had plied them all with punch beforehand: can you picture it? No, brother, you’re wrong: the ‘environment’ means a great deal in crime; I can confirm that for you.”

“I know myself that it means a lot, but tell me this: a man of forty dishonours a girl of ten; was it the environment that made him do it?”

“Well, strictly speaking, perhaps it was the environment,” Porfiry remarked with surprising gravity. “A crime against a little girl can very, very well be explained by the ‘environment’.”

Razumikhin nearly flew into a rage.

“Well, do you want me to prove to you right now,” he roared, “that your eyelashes are white solely because the bell tower of Ivan the Great is two hundred and fifty feet high, and prove it clearly, precisely, progressively and even with a liberal tinge? I’ll take it on! Well, do you want to bet!”

“Accepted! Let us hear, please, how he proves it!”

“But he’s pretending all the time, the devil!” cried Razumikhin, jumping up and waving his hand. “Well, is it worth talking to you! He does it all on purpose, you don’t know him yet, Rodion! And yesterday he took their side only to make fools of everyone. And what he said yesterday, Lord! And how glad they were of him!.. He can keep this up for a fortnight at a time. Last year he assured us, for some reason, that he was going to become a monk: he stuck to it for two months! Recently he took it into his head to assure us he was getting married, that everything was ready for the wedding. He even had new clothes made. We had already begun to congratulate him. There was no bride, nothing at all: it was all a mirage!”

“Now there you’ve lied! I had the clothes made before. It was because of the new clothes that it occurred to me to take you all in.”

“Are you really such a pretender?” Raskolnikov asked carelessly.

“And you thought I wasn’t? Wait, I’ll take you in too, ha-ha-ha! No, you see, sir, I’ll tell you the whole truth. Apropos of all these questions, crimes, environment, little girls, I have just remembered (though it always interested me) a little article of yours: ‘On Crime’... or whatever it was, I’ve forgotten the title, I don’t remember. Two months ago I had the pleasure of reading it in Periodical Speech.”

“My article? In Periodical Speech?” Raskolnikov asked in surprise. “I did indeed write an article, six months ago, when I left the university, apropos of a certain book, but I took it then to the newspaper Weekly Speech, not to Periodical.”

“But it ended up in Periodical.”

“But Weekly Speech ceased to exist, that’s why they didn’t print it then...”

“That’s true, sir; but on ceasing to exist, Weekly Speech merged with Periodical Speech, and so your little article appeared two months ago in Periodical Speech. Didn’t you know?”

Raskolnikov really had known nothing.

“Goodness, but you can ask them for money for the article! What a character you have, though! You live so solitary that you don’t know of things that concern you directly. That is a fact, sir.”

“Bravo, Rodka! I didn’t know either!” cried Razumikhin. “I’ll run into the reading room this very day and ask for the number! Two months ago? What date? Never mind, I’ll find it! There’s a thing! And he wouldn’t say!”

“And how did you find out that the article was mine? It was signed with an initial.”

“By chance, and only the other day. Through the editor; I know him... I was very much interested.”

“I examined, as I remember, the psychological state of the criminal throughout the whole course of the crime.”

“Yes, sir, and you insist that the act of carrying out a crime is always accompanied by illness. Very, very original, but... it was not, properly speaking, that part of your little article that interested me, but a certain idea let slip at the end of the article, which, unfortunately, you put forward only by hint, unclearly... In a word, if you remember, there is a certain hint that there exist in the world certain persons, as it were, who can... that is, not exactly can, but have every right to commit all sorts of outrages and crimes, and that the law, as it were, is not written for them.”

Raskolnikov smiled at this strained and deliberate distortion of his idea.

“What? What’s that? A right to crime? But surely not because ‘the environment has eaten them up’?” Razumikhin inquired, even with a kind of alarm.

“No, no, not quite because of that,” answered Porfiry. “The whole point is that in his article all people are somehow divided into the ‘ordinary’ and the ‘extraordinary’. The ordinary must live in obedience and have no right to transgress the law, because, you see, they are ordinary. And the extraordinary have the right to commit any crime and to transgress the law in every way, precisely because they are extraordinary. That is how you have it, I believe, unless I am mistaken?”

“But how can that be? It can’t possibly be like that!” Razumikhin muttered in bewilderment.

Raskolnikov smiled again. He understood at once what it was about and what they wanted to push him into; he remembered his article. He decided to accept the challenge.

“That is not quite how I have it,” he began, simply and modestly. “I confess, however, that you have stated it almost correctly, even, if you like, perfectly correctly...” (It was as if he found it pleasant to agree that it was perfectly correct.) “The only difference is that I do not at all insist that extraordinary people must and are bound always to commit all sorts of outrages, as you say. It seems to me even that such an article would not have been passed for print. I simply hinted that an ‘extraordinary’ man has the right... that is, not an official right, but that he himself has the right to permit his conscience to step over... certain obstacles, and only in the case where the fulfilment of his idea (sometimes saving, perhaps, for the whole of humanity) requires it. You are pleased to say that my article is unclear; I am ready to make it clear to you, as far as I can. I am perhaps not mistaken in supposing that that is just what you would like; very well, sir. In my opinion, if the discoveries of Kepler and Newton, through some combination of circumstances, could in no way become known to people except by sacrificing the lives of one, ten, a hundred and so on people who were hindering that discovery or standing in its way as an obstacle, then Newton would have the right, and would even be obliged... to remove those ten or a hundred people, in order to make his discoveries known to all humanity. It does not at all follow from this, however, that Newton had the right to kill anyone he pleased, all and sundry, or to steal every day at the market. Further, I recall, I develop in my article the idea that all... well, for example, the lawgivers and founders of humanity, beginning with the most ancient, continuing with the Lycurguses, Solons, Mahomets, Napoleons and so on, were all without exception criminals, by the very fact that in giving a new law they thereby broke the ancient one, held sacred by society and handed down from their fathers, and of course they did not stop short even at blood, if only blood (sometimes quite innocent and valiantly shed for the ancient law) could help them. It is even remarkable that the greater part of these benefactors and founders of humanity were particularly terrible shedders of blood. In a word, I conclude that all men, not only the great but even those who stand a little out of the rut, that is, who are even a little capable of saying something new, must by their very nature be criminals: more or less, of course. Otherwise it is hard for them to get out of the rut, and to stay in the rut they of course cannot agree, again by their very nature, and in my opinion they are even obliged not to agree. In a word, you see that so far there is nothing especially new here. This has been printed and read a thousand times. As for my division of people into ordinary and extraordinary, I agree that it is somewhat arbitrary, but then I don’t insist on exact figures. I believe only in my main idea. It consists precisely in this: that people, by a law of nature, are divided in general into two categories: a lower one (the ordinary), that is, so to speak, material, serving solely for the reproduction of their own kind, and people properly so called, that is, those who have the gift or talent of speaking a new word in their milieu. The subdivisions here are of course endless, but the distinguishing features of both categories are fairly sharp: the first category, that is, the material, generally speaking, are people conservative by nature, orderly; they live in obedience and like to be obedient. In my opinion they are even obliged to be obedient, because that is their purpose, and there is absolutely nothing humiliating for them in it. The second category all transgress the law; they are destroyers, or inclined to be, according to their abilities. The crimes of these people are of course relative and of many kinds; for the most part they demand, in very various forms, the destruction of the present in the name of something better. But if such a man needs, for the sake of his idea, to step over even a corpse, over blood, then in my opinion he may, within himself, in his conscience, give himself permission to step over blood, depending, however, on the idea and its scale: note that. Only in that sense do I speak in my article of their right to crime. (You will recall that we began with a legal question.) However, there is not much to worry about: the mass almost never recognises this right of theirs; it executes them and hangs them (more or less), and in so doing, quite justly, fulfils its conservative purpose, with this proviso, however, that in following generations this same mass puts the executed on a pedestal and worships them (more or less). The first category is always the master of the present; the second category is the master of the future. The first preserve the world and increase it in number; the second move the world and lead it towards its goal. Both have exactly the same right to exist. In a word, with me all have equal rights, and vive la guerre éternelle, long live eternal war, until the New Jerusalem, of course!”

“So you do believe in the New Jerusalem, after all?”

“I believe,” Raskolnikov answered firmly; as he said this, and all through his long tirade, he had been looking at the ground, having chosen a spot on the carpet.

“And-and-and do you believe in God? Forgive me for being so curious.”

“I believe,” repeated Raskolnikov, raising his eyes to Porfiry.

“And-and do you believe in the raising of Lazarus?”

“I-I believe. Why do you want to know all this?”

“You believe literally?”

“Literally.”

“I see, sir... I was just curious. Forgive me, sir. But allow me, I return to what we were saying: they are not always executed, after all; some, on the contrary...”

“Triumph in their lifetime? Oh yes, some achieve it in their lifetime too, and then...”

“They begin executing others themselves?”

“If necessary, and, you know, for the most part. In general your remark is witty.”

“Thank you, sir. But tell me this: how is one to distinguish these extraordinary people from the ordinary ones? Are there signs at birth, or something? I mean that one would need a little more precision here, so to speak, more outward definiteness: forgive the natural anxiety of a practical and well-meaning man, but couldn’t one introduce a special uniform, for instance, wear something, brands of some sort, perhaps?.. Because, you’ll agree, if some confusion arises and someone from one category imagines that he belongs to the other category, and begins to ‘remove all obstacles’, as you so happily put it, why then...”

“Oh, that happens very often! That remark of yours is even wittier than the last one...”

“Thank you, sir...”

“Not at all, sir; but take into consideration that a mistake is possible only on the side of the first category, that is, the ‘ordinary’ people (as I called them, perhaps very unfortunately). In spite of their inborn inclination to obedience, through a certain playfulness of nature that is not denied even to a cow, a great many of them like to imagine themselves advanced people, ‘destroyers’, and to push into the ‘new word’, and this quite sincerely, sir. At the same time they very often fail to notice the really new people, and even despise them as backward and grovelling thinkers. But in my opinion there cannot be any significant danger here, and you really need not worry, because they never go very far. For their enthusiasm one might sometimes, of course, have them flogged, to remind them of their place, but no more than that; there is no need even of an executioner: they will flog themselves, because they are very well behaved; some do each other this service, and others do it to themselves with their own hands... And they impose various public penances on themselves into the bargain; it turns out beautiful and edifying; in a word, you need not worry... There is such a law.”

“Well, at least from that side you have reassured me somewhat; but here again is a trouble, sir: tell me, please, are there many such people who have the right to cut the throats of others, these ‘extraordinary’ ones? I am ready to bow down, of course, but you’ll agree, it is frightening, sir, if there are going to be very many of them, eh?”

“Oh, don’t worry about that either,” Raskolnikov went on in the same tone. “In general, people with a new idea, even those just a little capable of saying anything at all new, are born extraordinarily few, even strangely few. Only one thing is clear: that the order in which people are born, all these categories and subdivisions, must be very accurately and precisely determined by some law of nature. This law is of course unknown at present, but I believe that it exists and may later become known. The vast mass of people, the material, exists in the world only so that at last, through some effort, by some process still mysterious, by means of some crossing of races and breeds, it may strain itself and at last bring into the world, well, perhaps one in a thousand, a man who is at least somewhat independent. One with a still broader independence is born, perhaps, one in ten thousand (I speak roughly, for illustration). With a still broader one, one in a hundred thousand. Men of genius, one in millions; and the great geniuses, the consummators of humanity, perhaps after many thousands of millions of people have passed over the earth. In a word, I have not looked into the retort in which all this takes place. But a definite law certainly exists and must exist; there can be no chance here.”

“What, are you both joking, or what?” Razumikhin cried at last. “Are you pulling each other’s legs or not? They sit there making fun of each other! Are you serious, Rodya?”

Raskolnikov silently raised his pale and almost sad face to him and made no answer. And beside this quiet, sad face, Porfiry’s undisguised, insistent, irritable and impolite sarcasm seemed strange to Razumikhin.

“Well, brother, if this is really serious, then... You are right, of course, in saying that it is not new and is like everything we have read and heard a thousand times; but what is really original in all this, and really belongs to you alone, to my horror, is that after all you permit blood in good conscience, and, forgive me, even with such fanaticism... So that, then, is the main idea of your article. Why, this permission of blood in good conscience is... it is, in my opinion, more terrible than an official permission to shed blood, a legal one...”

“Perfectly true, it is more terrible, sir,” Porfiry responded.

“No, you got carried away somehow! There’s a mistake here. I’ll read it... You got carried away! You can’t think that... I’ll read it.”

“All that is not in the article; there are only hints there,” said Raskolnikov.

“Just so, sir, just so,” Porfiry could not sit still, “it has become almost clear to me now how you are pleased to look at crime, sir, but... forgive my persistence (I am troubling you so much, I am ashamed myself!), you see, sir: you reassured me very much just now, sir, about the mistaken cases of confusing the two categories, but... I am still troubled by various practical cases! Well, what if some man or other, or some youth, imagines that he is a Lycurgus or a Mahomet... a future one, of course... and sets about removing all the obstacles to it... There is a long campaign ahead, he says, and a campaign needs money... so he starts getting it for himself for the campaign... you know?”

Zamyotov suddenly snorted from his corner. Raskolnikov did not even raise his eyes to him.

“I must agree,” he answered calmly, “that such cases must indeed occur. The silly and the vain especially swallow that bait; young people in particular.”

“There, you see, sir. Well, and what then, sir?”

“Why, nothing then,” Raskolnikov smiled. “I am not to blame for that. So it is and so it always will be. He here” (he nodded at Razumikhin) “was saying just now that I permit blood. So what? Society is only too well provided with exile, prisons, examining magistrates, penal servitude: why worry? Go and find your thief!..”

“Well, and if we do find him?”

“Then serve him right.”

“You are logical, to be sure. Well, sir, and what about his conscience?”

“But what business is that of yours?”

“Why, just so, out of humaneness, sir.”

“Whoever has one, let him suffer, if he recognises his mistake. That is his punishment, apart from penal servitude.”

“Well, and the real geniuses,” asked Razumikhin, frowning, “those who are given the right to cut throats: are they not to suffer at all, then, even for the blood they have shed?”

“Why the word ‘to be’ here? There is neither permission nor prohibition in it. Let him suffer, if he pities the victim... Suffering and pain are always obligatory for a broad consciousness and a deep heart. The truly great men, it seems to me, must feel a great sadness in the world,” he added suddenly, thoughtfully, not even in the tone of the conversation.

He raised his eyes, looked thoughtfully at everyone, smiled and took his cap. He was too calm compared with how he had come in earlier, and he felt it. Everyone stood up.

“Well, sir, scold me or not, be angry or not, but I can’t help it,” Porfiry Petrovich concluded again. “Allow me one more little question (I am troubling you so much, sir!), I wanted to slip in just one small little idea, solely so as not to forget it, sir...”

“Very well, tell me your little idea,” Raskolnikov stood before him, serious and pale, waiting.

“Why, sir... really, I don’t know how best to put it... it’s a very playful little idea... a psychological one, sir... Why, sir, when you were composing your little article, it surely can’t be, he-he! that you did not consider yourself, well, just a tiny drop at least, also an ‘extraordinary’ man, speaking a new word, in your sense, that is, sir... Isn’t that so, sir?”

“Very possibly,” Raskolnikov answered contemptuously.

Razumikhin made a movement.

“And if so, sir, would you really bring yourself, well, in view of some worldly failures and straits, or for the furtherance somehow of all humanity, to step over the obstacle?.. Well, for instance, to kill and rob?..”

And somehow he suddenly winked at him again with his left eye and laughed soundlessly, exactly as before.

“If I had stepped over, I would certainly not tell you,” Raskolnikov answered with challenging, haughty contempt.

“No, sir, I am only interested, properly speaking, in understanding your article, purely from the literary point of view, sir...”

“Pah, how obvious and insolent it is!” Raskolnikov thought with disgust.

“Allow me to point out to you,” he answered drily, “that I do not consider myself a Mahomet or a Napoleon... or any person of that kind, and consequently, not being one of them, I cannot give you a satisfactory explanation of how I would act.”

“Come now, who in Russia nowadays doesn’t consider himself a Napoleon?” Porfiry suddenly said with terrible familiarity. Even in the intonation of his voice there was this time something especially plain.

“Wasn’t it some future Napoleon who did in our Alyona Ivanovna with an axe last week?” Zamyotov suddenly blurted out from the corner.

Raskolnikov was silent, looking steadily and firmly at Porfiry. Razumikhin frowned darkly. It was as though something had begun to seem so to him even before. He looked round angrily. A minute of gloomy silence passed. Raskolnikov turned to leave.

“Leaving already!” said Porfiry kindly, holding out his hand extremely amiably. “Very, very glad to have met you. And as for your request, have no doubt about it. Just write as I told you. Or best of all, come and see me there yourself... one of these days... tomorrow, if you like. I shall be there at about eleven o’clock, for certain. We’ll arrange everything... we’ll talk... As one of the last people to have been there, you might perhaps be able to tell us something...” he added with the most good-natured air.

“You want to question me officially, with all the formalities?” Raskolnikov asked sharply.

“Why should I, sir? For the time being that is not required at all. You have misunderstood. I, you see, don’t let a chance slip, and... and I have already talked with all the people who had pledges... I have taken statements from some... and you, as the last... Why, by the way!” he cried, suddenly delighted at something. “By the way, I’ve remembered, what am I thinking of!..” He turned to Razumikhin. “Why, you wore my ears out about that Nikolashka then... well, I know it myself, I know it myself,” he turned to Raskolnikov, “that the lad is clean, but what can one do, we had to trouble Mitka too... here is the point, sir, the whole essence of it, sir: passing up the stairs then... allow me: you were there between seven and eight, weren’t you, sir?”

“Between seven and eight,” answered Raskolnikov, feeling unpleasantly in the same second that he need not have said it.

“Then, passing up the stairs between seven and eight, sir, didn’t you at least see, on the second floor, in the open flat, remember? two workmen, or at least one of them? They were painting there, didn’t you notice? It is very, very important for them!..”

“Painters? No, I didn’t see them...” Raskolnikov answered slowly, as if rummaging in his memory, at the same instant straining his whole being and freezing with the torment of guessing as quickly as possible where exactly the trap lay, and not overlooking anything. “No, I didn’t see them, and I don’t think I noticed such a flat, open... but on the fourth floor” (he had now fully mastered the trap and was triumphant) “I do remember that an official was moving out of his flat... opposite Alyona Ivanovna’s... I remember... I remember that clearly... soldiers were carrying out a sofa of some kind and pressed me against the wall... but painters, no, I don’t remember that there were painters... and I don’t think there was an open flat anywhere. No; there wasn’t...”

“But what are you doing!” Razumikhin suddenly shouted, as if coming to his senses and realising. “Why, the painters were daubing on the very day of the murder, and he was there three days before! What are you asking?”

“Pah! I’ve muddled it!” Porfiry slapped himself on the forehead. “The devil take it, this case is driving me out of my wits!” he turned to Raskolnikov, as if even apologising. “It would be so important for us to know whether anyone saw them between seven and eight in the flat that I imagined just now that you too could tell us... I’ve muddled it completely!”

“Then you should be more careful,” Razumikhin remarked sullenly.

These last words were spoken already in the hall. Porfiry Petrovich saw them to the very door extremely amiably. They both came out into the street gloomy and frowning, and for several steps neither said a word. Raskolnikov drew a deep breath...
