## Book Three

1. We should not only reckon that each day our life is being used up and a smaller part of it is left, but also this: that if a man lives longer, it is not at all certain that his mind will still be equal to understanding things, and to the study that leads to knowledge of things divine and human. For if he begins to lose his wits, breathing and eating and imagining and wanting, and all the rest of that kind, will not fail him; but the power to make use of himself, to fill in exactly the measures of his duty, to sort out what appears to him, to judge whether it is now time to take his leave of life, and everything else that needs a well-trained reason, goes out first. So we must hurry, not only because we come nearer to death every day, but also because our grasp of things and our power to follow them may come to an end before we do.

2. We should also notice things like this: that even what happens as a side-effect of nature’s work has its own charm and attraction. When bread is baked, for example, some parts of it split open; and these cracks, which in a way go against the baker’s intention, are somehow becoming, and in their own way make us want to eat.

Figs, again, burst open when they are ripest; and in olives ready to fall, the very nearness to rotting adds a beauty of its own to the fruit. And ears of corn bending down, and the lion’s wrinkled brow, and the foam dripping from a wild boar’s mouth, and many other things, if you looked at them by themselves, are far from beautiful; but because they follow from nature’s work, they add to its beauty and give delight.

So that a man who has feeling and a deeper understanding of what happens in the whole will find that almost nothing, even among the things that come as consequences, fails to fit in with a kind of pleasure. Such a man will look at the real gaping jaws of wild beasts with no less pleasure than at the copies that painters and sculptors show us; he will be able to see a kind of ripeness and bloom in an old woman or an old man, and the loveliness of children, with his own clear eyes; and many such things will strike him, which would not convince everyone, but only the man who has become truly at home with nature and her works.

3. Hippocrates cured many diseases, and then fell ill himself and died. The Chaldean astrologers foretold the deaths of many, and then fate caught up with them too. Alexander, Pompey and Julius Caesar, after so often wiping whole cities off the map and cutting down many tens of thousands of horse and foot in battle, also departed from life one day. Heraclitus, after all his theories about the world ending in fire, died with his insides full of water, smeared with cow dung. Lice killed Democritus, and another kind of vermin killed Socrates. What does this mean? You went on board, you made the voyage, you have come into harbour: get out. If it is into another life, there is nothing empty of gods there either. If into nothing at all, you will stop putting up with pains and pleasures, and stop being a slave to a vessel so much worse than what serves it: for the one is mind and divine spirit, and the other earth and gore.

4. Do not waste what is left of your life in thinking about other people, when it is not for some common good. For you lose the chance of some other work: that is, when you think about what so-and-so is doing, and why, and what he is saying, and what he is thinking, and what he is plotting, and all the things that make you wander away from watching over your own ruling mind.

You must, then, keep out of the chain of your thoughts everything random and pointless, and above all everything nosy and malicious; and you must get used to thinking only the kind of thoughts that, if someone suddenly asked you, “What are you thinking about now?”, you could answer at once and frankly, “This, and this”; so that it would be clear straight away that everything in you is simple and kind, and belongs to a creature made for living with others, one that does not care for pleasures or indulgent fancies at all, nor for rivalry, envy, suspicion or anything else you would blush to admit you had in mind.

For such a man, who no longer puts off being among the best, is a kind of priest and servant of the gods, making use of the god who is set up within him, which keeps the man unstained by pleasures, unwounded by any pain, untouched by any insult, unfeeling towards any wickedness; a fighter in the greatest of fights, not to be thrown by any passion; dyed deep with justice; welcoming with his whole soul everything that happens and is given to him; and not often, and not without great need and for the common good, thinking about what someone else is saying or doing or thinking. For he works only at what is his own, and keeps always in mind what is woven for him out of the whole; and the one he makes good, and the other he is sure is good. For the fate given to each person is carried along with him and carries him along.

He remembers too that everything rational is akin, and that to care for all people is in keeping with human nature; but that one should not hold on to the opinion of everyone, but only of those who live in agreement with nature. As for those who do not live so, he keeps always in mind what kind of people they are, at home and away from home, by night and by day, and what kind of people they mix with. So he takes no account even of praise from such people, who are not even pleased with themselves.

5. Do nothing unwillingly, nor selfishly, nor without examining it, nor pulled in two directions. Do not dress up your thoughts in fine words. Do not talk too much, or busy yourself with too much. Let the god within you be the guardian of a living being who is manly, mature, a citizen, a Roman, a ruler, who has taken up his post like a man waiting for the signal to leave life, ready to go, needing no oath and no man as a witness. And let there be in you cheerfulness, and no need of help from outside, and no need of the peace that others give. You must stand upright, not be held upright.

6. If you find anything in human life better than justice, truth, self-control, courage, and, in a word, than your mind being content with itself, where it makes you act according to right reason, and content with fate in what is given you without your choice; if, I say, you see anything better than this, turn to it with your whole soul, and enjoy the best thing you have found.

But if nothing appears better than the very spirit set up within you, which has brought its own impulses under its control, which examines its thoughts, which, as Socrates used to say, has drawn itself away from the feelings of the senses, which has placed itself under the gods and cares for human beings; if you find everything else smaller and cheaper than this, give room to nothing else, for once you lean towards it and turn aside, you will no longer be able, without distraction, to put first that good which is your own.

For it is not right that anything of a different kind should stand against the good of reason and of citizenship: such as the praise of the many, or power, or wealth, or the enjoyment of pleasures. All these, even if for a little while they seem to fit in, suddenly take over and carry you away. But you, I say, simply and freely choose the better, and hold on to it. “But the better is what is useful.” If it is useful to you as a rational being, keep to it; but if it is useful to you as an animal, say so, and keep your judgement without conceit; only make sure that you examine it safely.

7. Never value as useful to yourself anything that will force you one day to break your word, to give up your self-respect, to hate anyone, to suspect, to curse, to pretend, to desire anything that needs walls and curtains. For the man who has chosen first his own mind and spirit and the worship of its goodness makes no tragic scene, does not groan, will need neither solitude nor a crowd. Above all, he will live neither chasing after anything nor running away; and whether his soul is to be held in his body for a longer or a shorter time does not concern him in the least. For even if he had to depart right now, he will go as readily as he would set about any other task that can be done with decency and order; taking care through his whole life of this one thing only, that his mind is never turned in a direction that does not belong to a thinking and social being.

8. In the mind of a man who has been disciplined and thoroughly cleansed you will find nothing festering, nothing foul, no hidden sore. Fate does not catch his life unfinished, as one might say of an actor who leaves the stage before finishing and playing out the play. And there is nothing in him servile, nothing affected, nothing clinging, nothing cut off, nothing answerable to others, nothing hiding.

9. Respect your power of forming judgements. Everything depends on it, so that no judgement may ever arise in your ruling mind that is out of keeping with nature and with the make-up of a rational being. And this promises freedom from hasty judgement, friendship with human beings, and following the gods.

10. So throw away everything else and hold on to these few things only; and remember also that each of us lives only this present moment, this instant. All the rest is either already lived, or unknown. Small, then, is the life each one lives; small the corner of the earth where he lives; and small too is the longest fame after death, and even that passed on through a line of little men who will very soon die, and who do not know even themselves, let alone someone who died long ago.

11. To the rules already given, add one more: always make a definition or sketch of whatever presents itself to you, so as to see what kind of thing it is in its own substance, stripped bare, as a whole and in all its parts; and to tell yourself its proper name, and the names of the things from which it was put together and into which it will be broken up.

For nothing is so able to make the mind great as to be able to examine methodically and truly each thing that comes before you in life, and always to look at things in such a way as to consider at the same time what kind of world this is, what use this thing has in it, what value it has for the whole, and what for a human being, who is a citizen of the highest city, of which all other cities are like households; what it is, and what it is made of, and how long it is by nature likely to last, this thing that now makes an impression on me, and what virtue I need for dealing with it: gentleness, courage, truthfulness, good faith, simplicity, self-sufficiency, and the rest.

So you must say of each thing: this has come from God; this from the way things are woven together, the threads of fate spun in with each other, from such a meeting and chance; and this from a fellow member of my tribe, my kinsman and companion, who does not know, however, what is in keeping with his nature. But I do know. And so I treat him according to the natural law of fellowship, kindly and justly; while at the same time, in things that are indifferent, I aim at what each is worth.

12. If you do the work in front of you following right reason, seriously, vigorously, kindly, and without any side-business, but keep your own spirit pure and standing, as if you had to give it back at once; if you hold to this, expecting nothing and avoiding nothing, but content with acting now according to nature, and with heroic truth in what you say and utter, you will live well. And there is no one who can prevent this.

13. Just as doctors always keep their instruments and knives ready for sudden cases, so you keep your principles ready for understanding things divine and human, and for doing everything, even the smallest, as one who remembers how the two are bound together. For you will not do anything human well without referring it to the divine, nor the other way round.

14. Stop wandering about. You are not going to read your little notebooks, nor the deeds of the ancient Romans and Greeks, nor the extracts from books that you were saving up for your old age. Hurry, then, to the end; throw away empty hopes, and come to your own help, if you care for yourself at all, while you still can.

15. People do not know how many meanings there are in stealing, sowing, buying, being at peace, seeing what must be done; which is not done with the eyes, but with another kind of sight.

16. Body, soul, mind. To the body belong sensations, to the soul impulses, to the mind principles. To receive impressions like a stamp in wax belongs to cattle too. To be jerked about like a puppet by impulses belongs to wild beasts too, and to effeminate men, and to Phalaris and Nero. To have the mind as a guide to what seem to be duties belongs also to those who do not believe in the gods, and those who betray their country, and those who do what they do once they have shut their doors.

If, then, everything else is shared with the kinds of people I have named, what is left that is special to the good man is this: to love and welcome what happens to him and is spun for him by fate; not to soil the spirit set up within his breast, nor trouble it with a crowd of fancies, but to keep it calm, following God in good order, saying nothing contrary to the truth and doing nothing contrary to justice. And if all men refuse to believe that he lives simply, modestly and cheerfully, he is not angry with any of them, nor does he turn aside from the road that leads to the end of life, which he must reach pure, at peace, ready to go, fitted to his fate without being forced.
