## Chapter IV

Darya Alexandrovna, in a loose jacket, with the plaits of her once thick and beautiful hair, now thin, pinned up at the back of her head, with a sunken, thin face and large frightened eyes that stood out because her face was so thin, was standing among things scattered about the room, in front of an open chiffonier, from which she was taking something out. Hearing her husband’s steps, she stopped, looking at the door and trying in vain to give her face a stern and contemptuous expression. She felt that she was afraid of him and afraid of the coming interview. She had just been trying to do what she had tried to do ten times already in these three days: to sort out her own and the children’s things, which she would take to her mother’s, and again she could not bring herself to it; but now too, as on the earlier occasions, she told herself that things could not stay like this, that she must do something, punish him, shame him, revenge herself on him for at least a small part of the pain he had caused her. She still said that she would leave him, but she felt that it was impossible; it was impossible because she could not break the habit of regarding him as her husband and of loving him. Besides, she felt that if here, in her own house, she could hardly manage to look after her five children, they would be still worse off wherever she went with them all. As it was, in these three days the youngest had fallen ill because he had been given bad broth, and the others had gone almost without dinner the day before. She felt that it was impossible to leave; but, deceiving herself, she went on sorting things and pretending she would go.

Seeing her husband, she lowered her hands into the drawer of the chiffonier as though looking for something, and looked round at him only when he had come right up to her. But her face, to which she had wanted to give a stern and resolute expression, expressed bewilderment and suffering.

“Dolly!” he said in a low, timid voice. He drew his head down into his shoulders and wanted to look pitiful and submissive, but all the same he was radiant with freshness and health.

With a quick glance she looked over his figure, radiant with freshness and health, from head to foot. “Yes, he is happy and contented!” she thought. “And I?… And that disgusting good nature, for which everyone loves him and praises him so; I hate that good nature of his,” she thought. Her mouth tightened; a muscle of her cheek began to twitch on the right side of her pale, nervous face.

“What do you want?” she said in a quick, deep voice that was not her own.

“Dolly!” he repeated, with a tremor in his voice. “Anna is coming today.”

“Well, what is that to me? I cannot receive her!” she cried.

“But you must, after all, Dolly…”

“Go away, go away, go away!” she cried without looking at him, as though the cry were wrung from her by physical pain.

Stepan Arkadyich could be calm when he thought about his wife; he could hope that everything would shape itself, as Matvei put it, and could calmly read the paper and drink his coffee; but when he saw her tormented, suffering face and heard that tone of voice, submissive and despairing, his breath caught, something rose in his throat, and his eyes shone with tears.

“My God, what have I done! Dolly! For God’s sake!… After all…” He could not go on; a sob caught in his throat.

She slammed the chiffonier shut and looked at him.

“Dolly, what can I say?… Only one thing: forgive, forgive… Remember, can’t nine years of life make up for a minute, a minute…”

She stood with her eyes lowered and listened, waiting for what he would say, as though begging him to persuade her somehow that it was not so.

“A minute… a minute of being carried away…” he brought out, and wanted to go on; but at that word her lips tightened again, as if from physical pain, and again the muscle of her cheek began to jump on the right side of her face.

“Go away, get out of here!” she shouted still more shrilly. “And don’t talk to me about your infatuations, about your vileness!”

She wanted to leave, but she swayed and took hold of the back of a chair to steady herself. His face broadened, his lips swelled, his eyes filled with tears.

“Dolly!” he said, now sobbing. “For God’s sake, think of the children; they are not to blame. I am to blame; punish me, tell me how to atone for my guilt. Whatever I can do, I am ready for anything! I am to blame; there are no words to say how much I am to blame! But, Dolly, forgive me!”

She sat down. He heard her heavy, loud breathing, and he felt inexpressibly sorry for her. Several times she wanted to begin speaking, but could not. He waited.

“You remember the children when you want to play with them, but I remember them and know that they are ruined now,” she said: evidently one of the phrases she had repeated to herself more than once in these three days.

She had said “thou” to him, and he looked at her gratefully and moved to take her hand, but she drew away from him with disgust.

“I remember the children, and for that reason I would do anything in the world to save them; but I don’t know myself how I am to save them: by taking them away from their father, or by leaving them with a depraved father, yes, a depraved father… Well, tell me, after what… has happened, is it possible for us to live together? Is it possible? Tell me, is it possible?” she repeated, raising her voice. “After my husband, the father of my children, starts a love affair with his own children’s governess…”

“But what… what is to be done?” he said in a pitiful voice, not knowing himself what he was saying, and lowering his head more and more.

“You are vile to me, disgusting!” she shouted, growing more and more heated. “Your tears are water! You never loved me; there is neither heart nor honour in you! You are loathsome to me, disgusting, a stranger, yes, a stranger!” With pain and fury she pronounced that word, so terrible to her: stranger.

He looked at her, and the fury that showed in her face frightened and astonished him. He did not understand that his pity for her was what exasperated her. She saw in him regret for her, but not love. “No, she hates me. She will not forgive,” he thought.

“This is terrible! Terrible!” he said.

At that moment a child cried in the next room, probably after a fall; Darya Alexandrovna listened, and her face suddenly softened.

For a few seconds she was evidently coming to herself, as though not knowing where she was or what she had to do; then, getting up quickly, she moved towards the door.

“She does love my child, after all,” he thought, noticing the change in her face at the child’s cry, “my child; so how can she hate me?”

“Dolly, one word more,” he said, following her.

“If you follow me, I shall call the servants, the children! Let everyone know that you are a scoundrel! I am leaving today, and you can live here with your mistress!”

And she went out, slamming the door.

Stepan Arkadyich sighed, wiped his face and walked out of the room with quiet steps. “Matvei says it will shape itself; but how? I can’t even see the possibility. Oh, oh, how horrible! And how trivially she shouted,” he said to himself, remembering her shout and the words “scoundrel” and “mistress”. “And perhaps the maids heard! Horribly trivial, horribly.” Stepan Arkadyich stood alone for a few seconds, wiped his eyes, sighed, and, straightening his chest, went out of the room.

It was Friday, and in the dining-room the German clockmaker was winding the clocks. Stepan Arkadyich remembered his joke about this punctual, bald clockmaker, that the German “had been wound up for life himself, in order to wind clocks”, and he smiled. Stepan Arkadyich loved a good joke. “And perhaps it will shape itself! A good little word, that: shape itself,” he thought. “I must tell that one.”

“Matvei!” he called. “So arrange everything with Marya in the sitting-room for Anna Arkadyevna,” he said to Matvei when he appeared.

“Very good, sir.”

Stepan Arkadyich put on his fur coat and went out onto the porch.

“Will you not be dining at home?” said Matvei, seeing him off.

“That depends. Here, take this for the housekeeping,” he said, handing him ten roubles from his pocket-book. “Will that be enough?”

“Enough or not enough, we shall have to manage, it seems,” said Matvei, slamming the carriage door and stepping back onto the porch.

Meanwhile Darya Alexandrovna, having calmed the child and understood from the sound of the carriage that he had gone, went back to the bedroom. It was her only refuge from the household cares that crowded round her as soon as she went out. Even now, in the short time she had been in the nursery, the English governess and Matryona Filimonovna had managed to put several questions to her that would not wait and that she alone could answer: what were the children to wear for their walk? Should they be given milk? Should not someone be sent for another cook?

“Oh, leave me alone, leave me alone!” she said, and, going back to the bedroom, sat down again in the same place where she had talked with her husband, clasping her thin hands, with the rings slipping down off her bony fingers, and began going over the whole conversation in her memory. “He has gone! But how has he ended it with her?” she thought. “Can it be that he sees her? Why didn’t I ask him? No, no, we cannot come together again. Even if we stay in the same house, we are strangers. Strangers for ever!” she repeated again, with a special meaning, that word so terrible to her. “And how I loved him, my God, how I loved him!… How I loved him! And even now, don’t I love him? Don’t I love him more than before? The terrible thing, above all, is that…” she began, but did not finish her thought, because Matryona Filimonovna put her head in at the door.

“Do let us send for my brother,” she said. “He can get the dinner ready at least; otherwise, like yesterday, the children won’t have eaten till six o’clock.”

“Well, all right, I will come out in a minute and see to it. And has someone been sent for fresh milk?”

And Darya Alexandrovna plunged into the cares of the day and for a time drowned her grief in them.

## Chapter V

Stepan Arkadyich had done well at school thanks to his good abilities, but he had been lazy and mischievous, and so had come out near the bottom; yet in spite of his always dissipated life, his modest rank and his not very advanced years, he held the honourable and well-paid post of head of one of the government offices in Moscow. He had obtained this post through his sister Anna’s husband, Alexei Alexandrovich Karenin, who held one of the most important positions in the ministry to which the office belonged; but if Karenin had not appointed his brother-in-law to this post, then through a hundred other people, brothers, sisters, relations, cousins, uncles, aunts, Stiva Oblonsky would have obtained this post or another like it, with a salary of some six thousand, which he needed, since his affairs, in spite of his wife’s considerable fortune, were in disorder.

Half of Moscow and Petersburg were Stepan Arkadyich’s relations and friends. He was born among the people who were, and who became, the powerful of this world. A third of the statesmen, the old ones, had been friends of his father and had known him in his little shirt; another third were on “thou” terms with him; and the last third were good acquaintances. Consequently the distributors of earthly blessings, in the form of posts, leases, concessions and the like, were all his friends and could not pass over one of their own; and Oblonsky did not need to try particularly hard to get a profitable post; he needed only not to refuse, not to envy, not to quarrel, not to take offence, which, from the good nature that was his, he never did. It would have seemed ridiculous to him if he had been told that he would not get a post with the salary he needed, all the more since he did not ask for anything extraordinary; he wanted only what men of his own age were getting, and he could carry out an office of that kind no worse than anyone else.

Stepan Arkadyich was liked by everyone who knew him, not only for his kind, cheerful temper and unquestionable honesty; there was something in him, in his handsome, bright appearance, his shining eyes, black brows and hair, the whiteness and rosiness of his face, that acted physically, in a friendly and cheerful way, on the people who met him. “Aha! Stiva! Oblonsky! Here he is!” people said almost always, with a joyful smile, when they met him. And if it sometimes happened that after a conversation with him it turned out that nothing particularly joyful had happened, the next day, or the day after, everyone was just as glad again to meet him.

Now in his third year as head of one of the government offices in Moscow, Stepan Arkadyich had won, besides affection, the respect of his colleagues, his subordinates, his superiors and everyone who had dealings with him. The chief qualities that earned Stepan Arkadyich this general respect in the service were: first, his extraordinary indulgence towards people, founded in him on the awareness of his own failings; second, his complete liberalism, not the kind he read about in the newspapers but the kind that was in his blood, by which he treated all people perfectly equally and alike, whatever their fortune or rank; and third, and most important, his complete indifference to the business he was engaged in, as a result of which he never got carried away and never made mistakes.

Arriving at his place of work, Stepan Arkadyich, accompanied by the respectful porter, walked with his portfolio into his little private room, put on his uniform and went into the office. The clerks and officials all stood up, bowing cheerfully and respectfully. Stepan Arkadyich hurried, as always, to his place, shook hands with the members of the board and sat down. He joked and chatted exactly as much as was proper, and began the business of the day. No one knew better than Stepan Arkadyich how to find that boundary of freedom, simplicity and formality that is needed to make business pleasant. The secretary, cheerfully and respectfully, like everyone in Stepan Arkadyich’s office, came up with the papers and said, in that familiar and liberal tone which Stepan Arkadyich had introduced:

“We have managed to get the information from the Penza provincial board after all. Here it is, if you please…”

“Got it at last?” said Stepan Arkadyich, marking the paper with his finger. “Well, gentlemen…” And the session began.

“If only they knew,” he thought, bending his head with a look of importance as he listened to the report, “what a guilty little boy their president was half an hour ago!” And his eyes laughed while the report was read. Business was to go on without a break until two o’clock, and at two there would be an interval and lunch.

It was not yet two when the large glass doors of the office suddenly opened and someone came in. All the members, from under the portrait and from behind the emblem of justice, glad of a distraction, looked round at the door; but the attendant standing by the door at once drove the newcomer out and closed the glass door behind him.

When the case had been read through, Stepan Arkadyich got up and stretched, and, paying his tribute to the liberalism of the times, took out a cigarette right there in the office and went into his private room. Two of his colleagues, the old official Nikitin and the gentleman of the bedchamber Grinevich, went out with him.

“We shall have time to finish after lunch,” said Stepan Arkadyich.

“Plenty of time!” said Nikitin.

“He must be a fine rogue, that Fomin,” said Grinevich of one of the persons involved in the case they were examining.

Stepan Arkadyich frowned at Grinevich’s words, letting it be felt by this that it was improper to form a judgement too soon, and gave him no answer.

“Who was that who came in?” he asked the attendant.

“Some man, your excellency, pushed his way in without asking, the moment I turned my back. He was asking for you. I told him: when the members come out, then…”

“Where is he?”

“He’s gone out into the hall, I expect; he was walking about here all the time. That’s him,” said the attendant, pointing to a strongly built, broad-shouldered man with a curly beard who, without taking off his sheepskin cap, was running quickly and lightly up the worn steps of the stone staircase. One of the officials coming down, a thin man with a portfolio, stopped and looked disapprovingly at the legs of the man running up, and then glanced questioningly at Oblonsky.

Stepan Arkadyich was standing at the top of the stairs. His good-naturedly beaming face above the embroidered collar of his uniform beamed still more when he recognized the man running up.

“So it is! Levin, at last!” he said with a friendly, mocking smile, looking at Levin as he came up. “How is it you didn’t scorn to look for me in this den?” said Stepan Arkadyich, and, not content with a handshake, he kissed his friend. “Have you been here long?”

“I have only just arrived, and I wanted very much to see you,” answered Levin, looking round shyly and at the same time angrily and uneasily.

“Well, let’s go into my room,” said Stepan Arkadyich, who knew his friend’s proud and resentful shyness; and, seizing him by the arm, he drew him along after him, as though steering him past dangers.

Stepan Arkadyich was on “thou” terms with almost all his acquaintances: with old men of sixty, with boys of twenty, with actors, with ministers, with merchants and with adjutant-generals, so that a great many of those who were on “thou” terms with him stood at the two extreme ends of the social ladder and would have been very much surprised to learn that through Oblonsky they had something in common. He was on “thou” terms with everyone he drank champagne with, and he drank champagne with everyone; and so, when in the presence of his subordinates he met any of his shameful “thous”, as he jokingly called many of his friends, he knew, with the tact that was his, how to lessen the unpleasantness of the impression for his subordinates. Levin was not a shameful “thou”, but Oblonsky with his tact felt that Levin thought he might not wish to show his intimacy with him in front of his subordinates, and so he hurried to take him into his room.

Levin was almost the same age as Oblonsky, and was on “thou” terms with him not through champagne alone. Levin had been his comrade and friend in early youth. They loved each other in spite of the difference in their characters and tastes, as friends love each other who have come together in early youth. But in spite of that, as often happens between people who have chosen different kinds of work, each of them, though in argument he justified the other’s work, in his heart despised it. To each it seemed that the life his friend was leading was only a phantom. Oblonsky could not keep back a slight mocking smile at the sight of Levin. How many times he had seen him come to Moscow from the country, where he was doing something, though what exactly Stepan Arkadyich could never properly understand, nor was he interested; Levin always came to Moscow excited, hurried, a little ill at ease and irritated by his own unease, and usually with a completely new, unexpected view of things. Stepan Arkadyich laughed at this and loved it. In just the same way Levin in his heart despised his friend’s town way of life and his service, which he considered nonsense, and laughed at them. But the difference was that Oblonsky, doing what everyone does, laughed self-confidently and good-naturedly, while Levin laughed without self-confidence, and sometimes angrily.

“We have been expecting you for a long time,” said Stepan Arkadyich, going into his room and letting go of Levin’s arm, as if to show that here the dangers were over. “I am very, very glad to see you,” he went on. “Well, how are you? What’s new? When did you arrive?”

Levin was silent, looking at the unfamiliar faces of Oblonsky’s two colleagues, and in particular at the hand of the elegant Grinevich, with such white slender fingers, such long yellow nails curving at the ends, and such enormous shining cuff-links on his shirt, that these hands evidently absorbed all his attention and left him no freedom of thought. Oblonsky noticed this at once and smiled.

“Ah yes, allow me to introduce you,” he said. “My colleagues: Filipp Ivanych Nikitin, Mikhail Stanislavich Grinevich,” and, turning to Levin: “A zemstvo man, a new zemstvo man, a gymnast who lifts two hundred pounds with one hand, a cattle-breeder and a hunter, and my friend, Konstantin Dmitrich Levin, the brother of Sergei Ivanych Koznyshev.”

“Delighted,” said the little old man.

“I have the honour of knowing your brother, Sergei Ivanych,” said Grinevich, holding out his slender hand with its long nails.

Levin frowned, shook the hand coldly and at once turned to Oblonsky. Although he had a great respect for his half-brother, the writer, known to all Russia, he could not bear it when people addressed him not as Konstantin Levin but as the brother of the famous Koznyshev.

“No, I am not a zemstvo man any more. I have quarrelled with everyone and don’t go to the meetings any more,” he said, turning to Oblonsky.

“That was quick!” said Oblonsky with a smile. “But how? Why?”

“It’s a long story. I’ll tell you some time,” said Levin, but at once began telling it. “Well, to put it briefly, I became convinced that there is no zemstvo activity and cannot be any,” he began, as though someone had just insulted him. “On one side it’s a toy, they play at parliament, and I am neither young enough nor old enough to amuse myself with toys; and on the other side” (he stammered) “it is a means for the district coterie to make a little money. Before, it was the wardships and the courts, and now it’s the zemstvo… not in the form of bribes, but in the form of unearned salaries,” he said, as hotly as if someone present had been disputing his opinion.

“Aha! I see you are in a new phase again, a conservative one,” said Stepan Arkadyich. “But we’ll talk of that later.”

“Yes, later. But I needed to see you,” said Levin, staring with hatred at Grinevich’s hand.

Stepan Arkadyich smiled almost imperceptibly.

“How is it you said you would never again wear European clothes?” he said, looking over his new suit, evidently from a French tailor. “Just so! I see: a new phase.”

Levin suddenly blushed, but not as grown men blush, slightly, without noticing it themselves, but as boys blush, feeling that they are ridiculous in their shyness, and as a result ashamed and blushing still more, almost to tears. And it was so strange to see this intelligent, manly face in such a childish state that Oblonsky stopped looking at him.

“Well, where shall we meet? I very, very much need to talk to you,” said Levin.

Oblonsky seemed to think.

“I’ll tell you what: let’s go to Gurin’s for lunch and talk there. I am free till three.”

“No,” answered Levin, after thinking, “I still have to go somewhere else.”

“Well, all right, then let’s dine together.”

“Dine? But there’s nothing special, you know, only a couple of words to say, a question to ask, and we can have a talk afterwards.”

“Then say your couple of words now, and we’ll have the talk over dinner.”

“The couple of words are these,” said Levin; “though it’s nothing special.”

His face suddenly took on an angry expression, which came from the effort to overcome his shyness.

“What are the Shcherbatskys doing? Is everything as it was?” he said.

Stepan Arkadyich, who had long known that Levin was in love with his sister-in-law Kitty, smiled almost imperceptibly, and his eyes shone merrily.

“You said a couple of words, but I can’t answer in a couple of words, because… Excuse me for a minute…”

The secretary came in, with familiar respectfulness and with that modest awareness, common to all secretaries, of his superiority over his chief in knowledge of the business; he came up to Oblonsky with some papers and began, under the guise of a question, to explain some difficulty. Stepan Arkadyich, without hearing him out, laid his hand kindly on the secretary’s sleeve.

“No, you just do it the way I said,” he said, softening the remark with a smile; and, explaining briefly how he understood the matter, he pushed the papers away and said: “Do it that way, please. Please, that way, Zakhar Nikitich.”

The embarrassed secretary withdrew. Levin, who during the consultation with the secretary had quite recovered from his embarrassment, was standing with both elbows leaning on a chair, and on his face was a look of mocking attention.

“I don’t understand it, I don’t understand it,” he said.

“What don’t you understand?” said Oblonsky, smiling just as cheerfully and taking out a cigarette. He was expecting some odd sally from Levin.

“I don’t understand what you are doing,” said Levin, shrugging his shoulders. “How can you do this seriously?”

“Why not?”

“Why, because there is nothing to do.”

“You think so, but we are snowed under with work.”

“Paper work. Well, yes, you have a gift for that,” added Levin.

“You mean you think I am lacking in something?”

“Perhaps I do,” said Levin. “But all the same I admire your grandeur and am proud that my friend is such a great man. But you haven’t answered my question,” he added, with a desperate effort looking Oblonsky straight in the eyes.

“Well, all right, all right. Wait a little, and you will come to this too. It’s all very well while you have three thousand desyatinas in the Karazinsky district, and such muscles, and the freshness of a girl of twelve; but you will come over to us too. Yes, as for what you asked: there is no change, but it’s a pity you have stayed away so long.”

“Why?” asked Levin in alarm.

“Oh, nothing,” answered Oblonsky. “We’ll talk. But what, exactly, have you come for?”

“Oh, we’ll talk about that later too,” said Levin, blushing again to his ears.

“Well, all right. I understand,” said Stepan Arkadyich. “You see, I would ask you to my house, but my wife is not quite well. But here’s an idea: if you want to see them, they are sure to be at the Zoological Gardens today between four and five. Kitty goes skating. You drive there, and I’ll come by, and we’ll go and dine somewhere together.”

“Splendid. Well, goodbye.”

“Mind now, I know you, you’ll forget, or suddenly go off to the country!” Stepan Arkadyich called out, laughing.

“No, I shan’t.”

And, remembering that he had forgotten to take leave of Oblonsky’s colleagues only when he was already in the doorway, Levin went out of the room.

“That must be a very energetic gentleman,” said Grinevich when Levin had gone.

“Yes, my dear fellow,” said Stepan Arkadyich, shaking his head, “there’s a lucky man! Three thousand desyatinas in the Karazinsky district, everything ahead of him, and so much freshness! Not like the rest of us.”

“What have you to complain of, Stepan Arkadyich?”

“Oh, it’s bad, things are poor,” said Stepan Arkadyich, with a heavy sigh.
