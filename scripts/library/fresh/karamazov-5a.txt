## Book Three: The Sensualists · Chapter I · In the Servants’ Quarters

Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov’s house stood far from the very centre of the town, but not quite on the outskirts either. It was rather dilapidated, but had a pleasant appearance: one storey, with an attic room, painted grey, with a red iron roof. It could still stand a very long time, however; it was roomy and cosy. There were many little storerooms in it, many hiding places and unexpected little staircases. There were rats in it, but Fyodor Pavlovich was not altogether angry with them: “All the same it’s not so boring in the evenings when you are left alone.” And he really did have the habit of sending the servants off to the lodge for the night and locking himself up alone in the house all night. This lodge stood in the yard; it was large and solid; Fyodor Pavlovich had decided that the kitchen should be there too, though there was a kitchen in the house: he did not like the smell of cooking, and the food was carried across the yard winter and summer. In general the house had been built for a large family: five times as many masters and servants could have been housed in it. But at the time of our story only Fyodor Pavlovich and Ivan Fyodorovich lived in the house, and in the servants’ lodge only three servants: old Grigory, old Marfa, his wife, and the servant Smerdyakov, still a young man. I must say a little more in detail about these three persons in service. About old Grigory Vasilievich Kutuzov, however, we have already said enough. He was a firm and unswerving man, going stubbornly and in a straight line towards his point, if only that point, for some reason (often amazingly illogical), stood before him as an unalterable truth. Generally speaking, he was honest and incorruptible. His wife, Marfa Ignatievna, although she had bowed without question to her husband’s will all her life, pestered him terribly, for example, immediately after the emancipation of the serfs, to leave Fyodor Pavlovich and go to Moscow and start some little business there (they had some little money put by); but Grigory decided then and there, once and for all, that the woman was talking nonsense, “because every woman is dishonest”, but that they ought not to leave their former master, whatever he might be himself, “because that is now their duty”.

“Do you understand what duty is?” he said, turning to Marfa Ignatievna.

“I understand about duty, Grigory Vasilievich, but what duty we have to stay here, that I don’t understand at all,” Marfa Ignatievna answered firmly.

“Then don’t understand, but that’s how it will be. Be quiet from now on.”

And so it came out: they did not leave, and Fyodor Pavlovich fixed them a wage, a small one, and paid the wage. Grigory knew besides that he had an indisputable influence over his master. He felt it, and it was true: the cunning and stubborn buffoon Fyodor Pavlovich, very firm of character “in some things in life”, as he himself put it, was, to his own surprise, even rather weak of character in some other “things in life”. And he knew himself in which, knew and feared much. In some things in life one had to keep one’s ears pricked, and it was hard without a faithful man, and Grigory was the most faithful of men. It even happened that Fyodor Pavlovich might many times in the course of his career have been beaten, and beaten painfully, and it was always Grigory who rescued him, though each time he read him a lecture afterwards. But beatings alone would not have frightened Fyodor Pavlovich: there were higher cases, and even very subtle and complex ones, when Fyodor Pavlovich himself would perhaps not have been able to define that extraordinary need for a faithful and close person which he would sometimes suddenly, instantly and incomprehensibly, begin to feel in himself. These were almost morbid cases: most depraved, and in his sensuality often cruel, like a wicked insect, Fyodor Pavlovich would suddenly feel in himself at times, in drunken moments, a spiritual fear and a moral shock, which echoed in his soul almost, so to speak, physically. “At those times my soul seems to be fluttering in my throat,” he used to say sometimes. It was just at such moments that he liked to have near him, close by, perhaps not in the same room but in the lodge, a man like that, devoted, firm, not at all like himself, not depraved, who, though he saw all this dissipation going on and knew all the secrets, would still allow it all out of devotion, would not oppose it, and above all would not reproach him or threaten him with anything, either in this world or in the next; and in case of need would even defend him; from whom? From someone unknown, but terrible and dangerous. The point was precisely that there should certainly be another man, an old and friendly one, so that in a sick moment he could call him, only in order to look into his face, perhaps to exchange a word, even a quite irrelevant one; and if the man was all right and not angry, then the heart was somehow easier, and if he was angry, well, then it was sadder. It happened (though extremely rarely) that Fyodor Pavlovich even went at night to the lodge to wake Grigory, so that he would come to him for a minute. Grigory would come, and Fyodor Pavlovich would talk about the most complete trifles and soon let him go, sometimes even with a little mockery and a little joke, and he himself, with a spit, would go to bed and sleep the sleep of the righteous. Something of this kind happened to Fyodor Pavlovich after Alyosha’s arrival too. Alyosha had “pierced his heart” by the fact that he “lived, saw everything and condemned nothing”. More than that, he brought with him something unheard of: a complete absence of contempt for him, the old man; on the contrary, a constant gentleness and a perfectly natural, straightforward affection for him, who had deserved it so little. All this was a complete surprise for the old rake and man without a family, quite unexpected for him, who had until then loved only “filth”. After Alyosha left he admitted to himself that he had understood something which until then he had not wanted to understand.

I have already mentioned at the beginning of my story how Grigory hated Adelaida Ivanovna, Fyodor Pavlovich’s first wife and the mother of his first son, Dmitry Fyodorovich, and how, on the contrary, he defended his second wife, the shrieker Sofya Ivanovna, against his master himself and against anyone who took it into his head to say a bad or frivolous word about her. In him sympathy for this unhappy woman had turned into something sacred, so that even twenty years later he would not have borne, from whomever it came, even a bad hint about her, and would at once have retorted to the offender. In appearance Grigory was a cold and grave man, not talkative, letting out weighty, unfrivolous words. In the same way it would have been impossible to make out at first glance whether he loved his uncomplaining, obedient wife or not, and yet he really did love her, and she of course understood it. This Marfa Ignatievna was a woman not only far from stupid, but perhaps cleverer than her spouse, at least more sensible than he in everyday matters, and yet she submitted to him without a murmur and without answering back, from the very beginning of their marriage, and indisputably respected him for his spiritual superiority. It is remarkable that all their lives they talked very little to each other, except about the most necessary and current things. The grave and majestic Grigory always thought over all his affairs and cares alone, so that Marfa Ignatievna had long since understood once and for all that he did not need her advice at all. She felt that her husband valued her silence and acknowledged her intelligence for it. He had never beaten her, except just once, and then only lightly. In the first year of Adelaida Ivanovna’s marriage to Fyodor Pavlovich, once in the country, the village girls and women, still serfs then, had been gathered in the manor yard to sing and dance. They began “In the meadows”, and suddenly Marfa Ignatievna, still a young woman then, sprang out in front of the chorus and did the “Russian dance” in a special manner, not in the village way, like the women, but as she had danced when she was a house serf girl at the rich Miusovs’ in their private manor theatre, where the actors were taught to dance by a dancing master brought from Moscow. Grigory saw how his wife had danced, and at home in their cottage, an hour later, he taught her a lesson, pulling her hair a little. But with that the beatings ended once and for all and were never repeated even once in all their lives, and Marfa Ignatievna swore off dancing from then on.

God had not given them children; there had been one baby, and he had died. But Grigory evidently loved children, and did not even hide it, that is, was not ashamed to show it. When Adelaida Ivanovna ran away he took Dmitry Fyodorovich into his arms, a boy of three, and fussed over him for almost a year, combed him with a comb himself, even washed him in a tub himself. Afterwards he took trouble over Ivan Fyodorovich and Alyosha too, for which he got a slap in the face; but I have already told all about that. As for his own baby, it gladdened him only with hope while Marfa Ignatievna was still pregnant. When it was born, it struck his heart with grief and horror. The point is that this boy was born with six fingers. Seeing this, Grigory was so crushed that he not only kept silent right up to the very day of the christening, but went out into the garden on purpose to be silent there. It was spring, and for all three days he dug beds in the kitchen garden. On the third day the baby was to be christened; by this time Grigory had worked something out. Entering the cottage, where the clergy had gathered and the guests had come and, finally, Fyodor Pavlovich himself, who had come in person as godfather, he suddenly announced that the child “ought not to be christened at all”; he announced it not loudly, did not enlarge on it in words, barely squeezed out word by word, and only looked dully and intently at the priest as he did so.

“Why so?” the priest inquired with merry surprise.

“Because it... is a dragon...” muttered Grigory.

“What do you mean, a dragon, what dragon?”

Grigory was silent for some time.

“A confusion of nature has occurred...” he muttered, though very unclearly, yet very firmly, and evidently not wishing to say any more.

They laughed, and of course the poor little baby was christened. Grigory prayed zealously at the font, but did not change his opinion of the newborn. However, he did not hinder anything; only for all the two weeks that the sickly boy lived he hardly looked at him, did not even want to notice him, and for the most part went out of the cottage. But when two weeks later the boy died of thrush, he himself laid him in the little coffin, looked at him with deep anguish, and when they filled in the shallow little grave, he knelt down and bowed to the ground before the little grave. After that, for many years, he never once mentioned his child, and Marfa Ignatievna never once recalled her child in his presence, and when she happened to talk to anyone about her “little one”, she talked in a whisper, even if Grigory Vasilievich was not there. According to Marfa Ignatievna’s observation, from that very grave he began to occupy himself chiefly with “the divine”; he read the Lives of the Saints, mostly silently and alone, each time putting on his big round silver spectacles. He rarely read aloud, except perhaps in Lent. He loved the Book of Job; he had got hold somewhere of a copy of the sayings and sermons of “our God-bearing father Isaac the Syrian”, read it stubbornly and for many years, understood almost absolutely nothing in it, but perhaps for that very reason valued and loved this book most of all. Quite recently he had begun to listen to and look into the Khlyst sect, an opportunity for which had turned up in the neighbourhood; he was evidently shaken, but did not see fit to go over to the new faith. His reading “in the divine”, of course, gave his physiognomy still greater gravity.

Perhaps he was inclined to mysticism. And then, as if on purpose, the event of the birth of his six-fingered baby and its death coincided exactly with another very strange, unexpected and original event, which left on his soul, as he himself once put it afterwards, “a seal”. It so happened that on the very day they buried the six-fingered little one, Marfa Ignatievna, waking in the night, heard what sounded like the crying of a newborn child. She was frightened and woke her husband. He listened and remarked that it was more likely someone moaning, “a woman, it seems”. He got up and dressed; it was a fairly warm May night. Coming out onto the porch, he heard clearly that the moans were coming from the garden. But the garden was locked for the night with a padlock from the yard side, and there was no getting into it except by this entrance, because a strong and high fence ran round the whole garden. Returning home, Grigory lit a lantern, took the garden key and, paying no attention to the hysterical horror of his wife, who still insisted that she heard a child crying and that it must be her boy crying and calling her, silently went into the garden. There he clearly made out that the moans were coming from their little bathhouse, which stood in the garden, not far from the gate, and that it really was a woman moaning. Opening the bathhouse, he saw a sight before which he stood stupefied: a holy fool of the town, who wandered the streets and was known to the whole town, nicknamed Lizaveta Smerdyashchaya, Stinking Lizaveta, had got into their bathhouse and had just given birth to a baby. The baby lay beside her, and she was dying beside it. She said nothing, if only because she did not know how to speak. But all this ought to be explained separately.

## Chapter II · Stinking Lizaveta

There was one special circumstance here which deeply shook Grigory, finally confirming in him a certain unpleasant and loathsome earlier suspicion. This Lizaveta Smerdyashchaya was a girl of very small stature, “four feet and a little”, as many of the pious old women of our little town touchingly remembered after her death. Her twenty-year-old face, healthy, broad and ruddy, was completely that of an idiot; the look of her eyes was fixed and unpleasant, though meek. All her life, summer and winter, she went barefoot and in nothing but a hempen shift. Her almost black hair, extremely thick and curly like a ram’s, sat on her head like some kind of enormous cap. Besides, it was always soiled with earth and mud, with little leaves, splinters and shavings stuck in it, because she always slept on the ground and in the mud. Her father was a homeless, ruined and sickly tradesman, Ilya, who drank heavily and had lived for many years as a sort of labourer with some well-to-do employers, also tradespeople of ours. Lizaveta’s mother had died long ago. The eternally sickly and spiteful Ilya used to beat Lizaveta inhumanly when she came home. But she came home rarely, because she lived as a dependant all over the town, as a holy fool, a person of God. Both Ilya’s employers and Ilya himself, and even many of the compassionate townspeople, merchants and merchants’ wives chiefly, tried more than once to dress Lizaveta more decently than in a shift alone, and towards winter they always put a sheepskin coat on her and boots on her feet; but she would usually let them put everything on her without a word, then go off and somewhere, chiefly on the porch of the cathedral church, would certainly take off everything given her, whether a kerchief, a skirt, the sheepskin, the boots; she left everything on the spot and went away barefoot and in her shift as before. It once happened that a new governor of our province, making a tour of inspection through our little town, was much offended in his best feelings on seeing Lizaveta, and though he understood that she was a “holy fool”, as was reported to him, he nevertheless pointed out that a young girl wandering about in nothing but a shift offended decency, and so this must not happen in future. But the governor went away, and Lizaveta was left as she was. At last her father died, and by that very fact she became still dearer to all the pious people in the town, as an orphan. Indeed, everyone seemed even to love her; even the boys did not tease or hurt her, and our boys, especially at school, are a mischievous lot. She went into strangers’ houses, and no one drove her out; on the contrary, everyone would be kind to her and give her a half-kopeck. They would give her a half-kopeck, she would take it and at once carry it off and drop it into some church or prison collection box. If they gave her a bagel or a loaf at the market, she would certainly go and give the bagel or loaf to the first child she met, or else stop one of our richest ladies and give it to her; and the ladies even accepted it with joy. She herself lived on nothing but black bread and water. She would go into a rich shop, sit down; expensive goods lay there, and money too; the owners never watched her; they knew that even if thousands of roubles were laid out before her and forgotten, she would not take a kopeck of them. She rarely went into church; she slept either on church porches or, having climbed over someone’s wattle fence (we still have many wattle fences instead of board fences even today), in someone’s kitchen garden. She came home, that is, to the house of the employers with whom her late father had lived, about once a week, and in winter she came every day, but only for the night, and slept either in the passage or in the cowshed. People marvelled that she could stand such a life, but she was used to it; though she was small, she was of extraordinarily strong build. Some of our gentry maintained that she did all this only out of pride, but somehow that did not fit: she could not even say a word, and only rarely moved her tongue and mumbled; what pride was there in that? And so it happened that once (this was a long time ago), on a bright and warm September night, at full moon, very late by our standards, a tipsy band of our gentry out on a spree, five or six fine fellows, were returning home from the club “by the back ways”. On both sides of the lane ran a wattle fence, behind which stretched the kitchen gardens of the adjoining houses; and the lane came out onto a footbridge across our long, stinking puddle, which among us it is the custom to call a river sometimes. By the fence, in the nettles and burdock, our company caught sight of Lizaveta asleep. The gentlemen, who had had a drop, stopped over her with guffaws and began to make jokes with every possible lack of restraint. One young gentleman suddenly had a completely eccentric question come into his head on an impossible subject: “Could anyone at all, they say, regard such a beast as a woman, now for instance,” and so on. They all decided with proud disgust that one could not. But Fyodor Pavlovich happened to be in this little crowd, and he instantly jumped forward and decided that one could regard her as a woman, even very much so, and that there was even something piquant of a special kind in it, and so on and so on. True, at that time he was even far too deliberately pushing himself forward among us in his part of buffoon; he liked to jump forward and amuse the gentry, apparently as an equal of course, but in fact as a complete lout before them. This was precisely the very time when he had received the news from Petersburg of the death of his first wife, Adelaida Ivanovna, and when, with crape on his hat, he was drinking and behaving so outrageously that some people in the town, even among the most dissolute, were sickened at the sight of him. The band, of course, roared with laughter at this unexpected opinion; one of the band even began egging Fyodor Pavlovich on, but the rest began spitting even more, though still with excessive merriment, and at last they all went off on their way. Afterwards Fyodor Pavlovich swore that he too had gone away then with all the rest; perhaps that is exactly how it was; no one knows for certain and no one ever did; but five or six months later the whole town began talking with sincere and extraordinary indignation about Lizaveta’s going about pregnant; they asked and inquired: whose sin was it, who was the offender? And it was just then that a strange rumour suddenly spread through the whole town that the offender was this very Fyodor Pavlovich. Where did this rumour come from? Of that band of revelling gentlemen only one participant remained in the town by that time, and he an elderly and respectable state councillor, with a family and grown-up daughters, who would certainly not have spread anything even if there had been something; the other participants, some five men, had left by then. But the rumour pointed straight at Fyodor Pavlovich and went on pointing. Of course he did not even much protest: he would not even have bothered to answer some petty merchants or tradesmen. He was proud then, and did not talk except in his own company of officials and nobles, whom he amused so much. It was at this time that Grigory stood up energetically and with all his might for his master, and not only defended him against all these slanders, but entered into quarrels and arguments on his behalf and convinced many. “She herself, the low creature, is to blame,” he said positively, and the offender was none other than “Karp with the screw” (that was the name of a terrible convict well known to the town at that time, who had escaped by then from the provincial prison and was living secretly in our town). This guess seemed plausible; people remembered Karp; they remembered precisely that on those very nights, towards autumn, he had been roaming the town and had robbed three people. But this whole incident and all this talk not only did not turn general sympathy away from the poor holy fool, but everyone began to guard and protect her even more. A merchant’s widow, Kondratieva, a well-to-do woman, even arranged to take Lizaveta into her house at the end of April, intending not to let her out until her confinement. They watched her without sleeping, but it so happened that in spite of all the watchfulness, on the very last day, in the evening, Lizaveta suddenly slipped away secretly from Kondratieva’s and turned up in Fyodor Pavlovich’s garden. How in her condition she climbed over the high and strong garden fence remained a sort of riddle. Some insisted that she had been “carried over”, others that “something had carried her over”. Most probably it all happened in a very ingenious but natural way, and Lizaveta, who knew how to climb wattle fences into strangers’ kitchen gardens to sleep in them, somehow got up onto Fyodor Pavlovich’s fence too, and jumped down from it into the garden, though to her own harm, in spite of her condition. Grigory rushed to Marfa Ignatievna and sent her to help Lizaveta, and he himself ran for an old midwife, a tradeswoman, who happened to live not far off. They saved the baby, but by dawn Lizaveta was dead. Grigory took the baby, brought it into the house, sat his wife down and laid it on her knees, right at her breast: “A child of God, an orphan, is everyone’s kin, and ours all the more. Our little dead one has sent us this one, and this one was born of the devil’s son and a righteous woman. Feed him, and weep no more.” And so Marfa Ignatievna brought up the child. He was christened and named Pavel, and as for his patronymic, everyone, of their own accord, without being told, began calling him Fyodorovich. Fyodor Pavlovich did not object to anything and even found it all amusing, though he went on with all his might denying everything. The town liked his having taken in the foundling. Later Fyodor Pavlovich invented a surname for the foundling too: he called him Smerdyakov, after his mother’s nickname, Lizaveta Smerdyashchaya. And this Smerdyakov became Fyodor Pavlovich’s second servant, and at the beginning of our story he was living in the lodge with old Grigory and old Marfa. He was employed as a cook. I really ought to add something about him specially, but I am ashamed to distract my reader’s attention so long to such ordinary servants, and so I pass on to my story, hoping that Smerdyakov will somehow come in of himself in the further course of the tale.
