## Book Eleven, Chapter III · A Little Demon

Going in to Lise, he found her half lying in her old chair, the one in which she had been wheeled about when she could not yet walk. She did not move to meet him, but her keen, sharp eyes fastened on him. Her eyes were somewhat feverish, her face a pale yellow. Alyosha was amazed at how she had changed in three days; she had even grown thin. She did not hold out her hand to him. He touched her thin, long little fingers himself, which lay motionless on her dress, then sat down opposite her in silence.

“I know you are in a hurry to get to the prison,” Lise said sharply, “and mama has kept you two hours; she has just told you about me and about Yulia.”

“How do you know?” asked Alyosha.

“I was eavesdropping. Why are you staring at me? I want to eavesdrop, so I eavesdrop; there is nothing wrong in that. I don’t ask forgiveness.”

“Are you upset about something?”

“On the contrary, I’m very glad. I was just thinking it over again, for the thirtieth time: how good it is that I refused you and won’t be your wife. You are no good as a husband: I’d marry you, and suddenly give you a note to take to the man I’d fallen in love with after you, and you would take it and be sure to deliver it, and bring back an answer too. And you’ll be forty, and you’ll still be carrying notes like that for me.”

She suddenly laughed.

“There is something spiteful in you and at the same time something simple-hearted,” Alyosha smiled at her.

“The simple-hearted thing is that I’m not ashamed before you. Not only am I not ashamed, I don’t want to be ashamed, precisely before you, precisely of you. Alyosha, why don’t I respect you? I love you very much, but I don’t respect you. If I respected you, I wouldn’t talk without shame, would I?”

“No.”

“And do you believe that I’m not ashamed before you?”

“No, I don’t.”

Lise laughed nervously again; she was talking fast, rapidly.

“I sent your brother Dmitri Fyodorovich some sweets in prison. Alyosha, do you know how sweet you are? I shall love you terribly for having let me not love you so quickly.”

“Why did you send for me today, Lise?”

“I wanted to tell you a wish of mine. I want someone to torment me, marry me and then torment me, deceive me, go away and leave me. I don’t want to be happy!”

“You have come to love disorder?”

“Ah, I want disorder. I keep wanting to set fire to the house. I imagine how I’ll go up and set fire to it quietly; it must be quietly. They’ll be putting it out, and it will be burning. And I’ll know and say nothing. Ah, what nonsense! And how dull it is!”

She waved her little hand in disgust.

“You live in luxury,” Alyosha said quietly.

“Is it better to be poor, then?”

“It is better.”

“That is what your dead monk told you. It isn’t true. Let me be rich and everyone else poor; I’ll eat sweets and drink cream and give none to anyone. Ah, don’t speak, don’t say anything,” she waved her hand, though Alyosha had not opened his mouth. “You’ve told me all that before; I know it all by heart. It’s dull. If I am poor I’ll kill someone; and if I’m rich, perhaps I’ll kill someone too; why sit about! Do you know, I want to reap, to reap rye. I’ll marry you, and you become a peasant, a real peasant; we’ll have a little foal; would you like that? Do you know Kalganov?”

“I do.”

“He keeps walking about dreaming. He says: why live for real? It’s better to dream. One can dream the merriest things, but living is dull. And yet he’ll be married soon; he has already declared his love to me too. Can you spin a top?”

“I can.”

“Well, he’s like a top: wind him up and set him spinning and whip him, whip him, whip him with a little whip. I’ll marry him and keep him spinning all my life. Aren’t you ashamed to sit with me?”

“No.”

“You are terribly angry that I’m not talking about holy things. I don’t want to be holy. What will they do in the next world for the very greatest sin? You must know exactly.”

“God will judge,” said Alyosha, looking at her intently.

“That’s just what I want. I’d come, and they’d judge me, and I’d suddenly laugh in all their faces. I terribly want to set fire to the house, Alyosha, our house; you still don’t believe me?”

“Why not? There are even children, about twelve years old, who very much want to set fire to something, and they do. It is a sort of illness.”

“That’s not true, not true; there may be children like that, but that isn’t what I mean.”

“You take evil for good: it is a passing crisis; perhaps your former illness is to blame for it.”

“So you do despise me! I simply don’t want to do good; I want to do evil, and there is no illness in it at all.”

“Why do evil?”

“So that nothing should be left anywhere. Ah, how good it would be if nothing were left! Do you know, Alyosha, I sometimes think of doing a terrible lot of evil and everything nasty, and I’ll go on doing it quietly for a long time, and suddenly everyone will find out. Everyone will crowd round me and point their fingers at me, and I’ll look at them all. That is very pleasant. Why is it so pleasant, Alyosha?”

“It just is. A need to crush something good, or, as you said, to set fire to something. That happens too.”

“I didn’t just say it; I shall do it.”

“I believe you.”

“Ah, how I love you for saying ‘I believe you’. And you aren’t lying at all, at all. But perhaps you think I’m saying all this on purpose, to tease you?”

“No, I don’t think so… though perhaps there is a little of that need too.”

“There is a little. I shall never lie to you,” she said, with a sort of little fire flashing in her eyes.

What struck Alyosha most of all was her seriousness: there was not a shadow of laughter or playfulness in her face now, though before, gaiety and playfulness had never left her even in her most “serious” moments.

“There are moments when people love crime,” Alyosha said thoughtfully.

“Yes, yes! You have said my thought; they love it, everyone loves it, and always, not just at ‘moments’. You know, it’s as if everyone once agreed to lie about it, and everyone has lied ever since. Everyone says they hate what is bad, and in themselves they all love it.”

“Are you still reading bad books?”

“I am. Mama reads them and hides them under her pillow, and I steal them.”

“Aren’t you ashamed to destroy yourself?”

“I want to destroy myself. There is a boy here who lay under the rails while the train went over him. Lucky boy! Listen, your brother is being tried now for killing his father, and everyone loves it that he killed his father.”

“Loves it that he killed his father?”

“They love it, everyone loves it! Everyone says it’s terrible, but in themselves they love it terribly. I am the first to love it.”

“There is some truth in what you say about everyone,” Alyosha said quietly.

“Oh, what thoughts you have!” Lise squealed in rapture. “And from a monk! You wouldn’t believe how I respect you, Alyosha, for never lying. Ah, I’ll tell you a funny dream of mine. I sometimes dream of devils. It is night, and I am in my room with a candle, and suddenly there are devils everywhere, in all the corners and under the table, and they open the doors, and there is a crowd of them behind the doors, and they want to come in and seize me. And they come nearer, they are seizing me already. And suddenly I cross myself, and they all draw back; they are afraid; only they don’t go away altogether, but stand at the doors and in the corners, waiting. And suddenly I terribly want to begin abusing God aloud, and so I begin abusing him, and they all come crowding at me again, they are so glad, and they are seizing me again, and suddenly I cross myself again, and they all draw back. It is terribly jolly; it takes my breath away.”

“I have had that very dream too,” Alyosha said suddenly.

“Really?” Lise cried out in surprise. “Listen, Alyosha, don’t laugh; this is terribly important: can two different people really have the same dream?”

“They can, I suppose.”

“Alyosha, I tell you, it is terribly important,” Lise went on in an excessive surprise. “It isn’t the dream that is important, but that you could have had the very same dream as I. You never lie to me; don’t lie now either: is it true? You aren’t laughing?”

“It is true.”

Lise was terribly struck by something and was silent for half a minute.

“Alyosha, come and see me, come and see me more often,” she said suddenly in an imploring voice.

“I shall always, all my life, come and see you,” Alyosha answered firmly.

“I tell it only to you,” Lise began again. “I tell it only to myself, and to you as well. To you alone in the whole world. And I tell it to you more willingly than to myself. And I am not ashamed before you at all. Alyosha, why am I not ashamed before you at all, not at all? Alyosha, is it true that the Jews steal children at Passover and kill them?”

“I don’t know.”

“I have a book here; I read about some trial somewhere, and that a Jew first cut off all the fingers on both hands of a little boy of four, and then crucified him on the wall, nailed him up with nails and crucified him; and then at the trial he said that the boy died quickly, in four hours. Quickly, indeed! He said the boy moaned, kept moaning, and he stood there admiring him. That is good!”

“Good?”

“Good. I sometimes imagine that I crucified him myself. He hangs there moaning, and I sit down opposite him and eat pineapple compote. I am very fond of pineapple compote. Are you?”

Alyosha was silent and looked at her. Her pale yellow face suddenly became distorted; her eyes were burning.

“You know, when I read about that Jew, I shook in tears all night. I imagined how the little child cried and moaned (four-year-old boys do understand, after all), and all the while this thought about the compote wouldn’t leave me. In the morning I sent a letter to a certain person, asking him to come and see me without fail. He came, and I suddenly told him about the boy and the compote, told him everything, everything, and said that ‘it was good’. He suddenly laughed and said that it really was good. Then he got up and went away. He only stayed five minutes. Did he despise me, did he despise me? Tell me, tell me, Alyosha, did he despise me or not?” She sat up straight on the couch, her eyes flashing.

“Tell me,” Alyosha said in agitation, “did you send for him yourself, this person?”

“I did.”

“You sent him a letter?”

“A letter.”

“Simply to ask about this, about the child?”

“No, not about that at all, not at all. But as soon as he came in, I asked him about that at once. He answered, laughed, got up and went away.”

“That person treated you honestly,” Alyosha said quietly.

“And did he despise me? Did he laugh at me?”

“No, because perhaps he believes in the pineapple compote himself. He is very ill now too, Lise.”

“Yes, he believes in it!” Lise’s eyes flashed.

“He doesn’t despise anyone,” Alyosha went on. “He only doesn’t believe anyone. And if he doesn’t believe, then of course he despises too.”

“So me too? Me?”

“You too.”

“That’s good,” Lise somehow ground out. “When he went out and laughed, I felt that it is good to be despised. And the boy with his fingers cut off is good, and being despised is good…”

And she laughed somehow spitefully and feverishly in Alyosha’s face.

“You know, Alyosha, you know, I should like… Alyosha, save me!” She suddenly jumped up from the couch, rushed to him and clasped him tightly in her arms. “Save me,” she almost moaned. “Would I tell anyone in the world what I have told you? And I was telling the truth, the truth, the truth! I shall kill myself, because everything is loathsome to me! I don’t want to live, because everything is loathsome to me! Everything is loathsome to me, everything is loathsome! Alyosha, why don’t you love me at all, not at all?” she finished in a frenzy.

“But I do love you!” Alyosha answered warmly.

“And will you weep for me? Will you?”

“I will.”

“Not because I wouldn’t be your wife, but just weep for me, just weep?”

“I will.”

“Thank you! I only need your tears. And let all the rest punish me and crush me under their feet, all, all, without exception! Because I don’t love anyone. Do you hear, no-o-one! On the contrary, I hate them! Go, Alyosha; it’s time for you to go to your brother!” She suddenly tore herself away from him.

“But how can I leave you like this?” Alyosha said, almost in alarm.

“Go to your brother; they’ll lock the prison; go, here is your hat! Kiss Mitya for me; go, go!”

And she almost pushed Alyosha out of the door by force. He was looking at her in sorrowful perplexity, when suddenly he felt a letter in his right hand, a little letter, folded tight and sealed. He glanced at it and instantly read the address: To Ivan Fyodorovich Karamazov. He looked quickly at Lise. Her face had become almost menacing.

“Give it to him, be sure to give it to him!” she ordered in a frenzy, trembling all over. “Today, at once! Or I’ll poison myself! That’s why I sent for you!”

And she quickly slammed the door. The latch clicked. Alyosha put the letter in his pocket and went straight to the stairs, without going in to Madame Khokhlakova, forgetting about her even. And Lise, as soon as Alyosha had gone, at once unfastened the latch, opened the door a tiny crack, put her finger in the crack and, slamming the door, crushed it with all her might. About ten seconds later, freeing her hand, she walked quietly, slowly, to her chair, sat down, holding herself quite straight, and began to look intently at her blackened little finger and at the blood that had been squeezed out from under the nail. Her lips were trembling, and she whispered quickly, quickly to herself:

“Vile, vile, vile, vile!”
