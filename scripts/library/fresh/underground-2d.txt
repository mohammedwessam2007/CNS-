## II

But a phase of my petty debauchery would come to an end, and I would feel terribly sick. Remorse would set in; I drove it away: it made me too sick. Little by little, however, I got used to this too. I got used to everything; that is, not exactly got used to it, but somehow voluntarily agreed to put up with it. But I had a way out that reconciled everything: escaping into “the beautiful and sublime”, in dreams, of course. I dreamed terribly; I dreamed for three months on end, huddled in my corner, and believe me, at those moments I was nothing like the gentleman who, in the panic of his chicken heart, had sewn a German beaver onto the collar of his overcoat. I suddenly became a hero. I would not even have let my six-foot lieutenant in to call on me then. I could not even picture him then. What my dreams were, and how I could be content with them, is hard to say now; but then I was content with them. In fact, I am still partly content with them now. Dreams came to me especially sweet and strong after a bout of debauchery; they came with remorse and tears, with curses and raptures. There were moments of such positive intoxication, such happiness, that I did not feel even the slightest mockery inside me, by God. There was faith, hope, love. That’s just it: I believed blindly then that by some miracle, by some outside circumstance, all this would suddenly open out and widen; that suddenly there would appear a horizon of suitable activity, beneficial, beautiful and, above all, completely ready-made (what exactly, I never knew; but the main thing was, completely ready-made), and then I would suddenly step out into God’s world, almost on a white horse and crowned with laurels. A secondary role I could not even conceive of, and that is precisely why in reality I very calmly occupied the last one. Either a hero or dirt: there was no middle. That is what ruined me, because in the dirt I consoled myself that at other times I was a hero, and the hero covered up the dirt: for an ordinary man, I told myself, it is shameful to get dirty, but a hero is too lofty to get completely dirty, and therefore one may get dirty. It is remarkable that these surges of “the beautiful and sublime” came to me even during my debauchery, and precisely when I was already at the very bottom; they came in separate little flashes, as if reminding me of themselves, but they did not destroy the debauchery by their appearance; on the contrary, they seemed to liven it up by contrast, and came in exactly the amount needed for a good sauce. The sauce here consisted of contradiction and suffering, of agonising inner analysis, and all these torments, big and small, gave a kind of piquancy, even a meaning, to my debauchery; in a word, they did the job of a good sauce perfectly. There was even a certain depth in all this. And could I have agreed to a simple, vulgar, direct, clerk’s kind of debauchery and taken all that dirt upon myself? What could there have been in it to tempt me and lure me out into the street at night? No, sir, I had a noble loophole for everything...

But how much love, Lord, how much love I used to live through in these dreams of mine, in these “escapes into the beautiful and sublime”: a fantastic love, perhaps, one that was never actually applied to anything human, but there was so much of it, this love, that afterwards, in reality, I did not even feel the need to apply it: that would have been a superfluous luxury. Everything, however, always ended most happily with a lazy and intoxicating passage into art, that is, into beautiful forms of existence, completely ready-made, heavily stolen from poets and novelists and adapted to every possible use and demand. I, for example, triumph over everyone; everyone, of course, is in the dust and forced to acknowledge all my perfections of their own free will, and I forgive them all. I fall in love, being a famous poet and a gentleman of the court; I receive countless millions and at once give them away to the human race, and right there I confess before the whole people my disgraces, which of course are not simply disgraces but contain a very great deal of “the beautiful and sublime”, something Byronic, like Manfred. Everyone weeps and kisses me (what blockheads they would be otherwise), and I go off barefoot and hungry to preach new ideas, and I rout the reactionaries at Austerlitz. Then a march is played, an amnesty is granted, the Pope agrees to leave Rome for Brazil; then there is a ball for all Italy at the Villa Borghese on the shores of Lake Como, since Lake Como has been moved to Rome specially for the occasion; then a scene in the bushes, and so on and so on; as if you didn’t know! You will say it is vulgar and mean to bring all this out to market now, after so many raptures and tears, which I myself have confessed. Why mean, sir? Do you really think I am ashamed of all this, and that it was all more stupid than anything at all in your lives, gentlemen? And besides, believe me, some of it was not at all badly put together... Not everything happened on Lake Como. But then, you are right; it really is vulgar and mean. And meanest of all is that I have now begun to justify myself to you. And meaner still is that I am making this remark now. But enough, or I shall never finish: each thing will be meaner than the last...

I was never able to dream for more than three months at a stretch, and I would begin to feel an irresistible need to plunge into society. To plunge into society meant, for me, going to visit my head of section, Anton Antonych Setochkin. He was the only steady acquaintance I have had in my whole life, and I am even surprised at this myself now. But even to him I went only when such a phase had arrived, and my dreams had reached such happiness, that I absolutely had to embrace people and all mankind at once; and for that I needed to have at least one person at hand, one who actually existed. One had to call on Anton Antonych on Tuesdays, however (his day), and so I always had to adjust my need to embrace all mankind to fit a Tuesday. This Anton Antonych lived near Five Corners, on the fourth floor, in four little rooms, low-ceilinged and each smaller than the last, with the most economical and yellowish look. He had two daughters and their aunt, who poured the tea. The daughters were thirteen and fourteen, both snub-nosed, and I was terribly embarrassed by them, because they kept whispering to each other and giggling. The master of the house usually sat in his study, on a leather sofa, at a table, with some grey-haired guest, an official from our department or even from another one. I never saw more than two or three guests there, and always the same ones. They talked about the excise, about the bidding in the Senate, about salaries, about promotions, about His Excellency, about the art of pleasing, and so on and so on. I had the patience to sit beside these people like a fool for four hours at a time and listen to them, not daring and not knowing how to talk to them about anything. I grew dull; several times I would break into a sweat; a kind of paralysis hovered over me; but it was good and useful. When I got home, I would put off my wish to embrace all mankind for a while.

I did have, however, what seemed like one more acquaintance, Simonov, a former schoolfellow of mine. I had plenty of schoolfellows in Petersburg, perhaps, but I had nothing to do with them and even stopped greeting them in the street. Perhaps I even transferred to another department so as not to be with them and to cut myself off at a stroke from my whole hateful childhood. A curse on that school, on those terrible years of penal servitude! In a word, I broke with my schoolfellows as soon as I got out into the world. There remained two or three people whom I still greeted when we met. Among them was Simonov, who had not distinguished himself in any way at school, was even-tempered and quiet; but I had noticed in him a certain independence of character and even honesty. I don’t even think he was all that limited. I once had some rather bright moments with him, but they did not last long and were somehow suddenly veiled in fog. He was evidently weighed down by these memories and seemed always afraid that I would fall into my old tone. I suspected that he found me very distasteful, but I still went to see him, not being sure of it.

And so one Thursday, unable to bear my loneliness, and knowing that on Thursdays Anton Antonych’s door was closed, I remembered Simonov. As I climbed up to his fourth floor I was thinking precisely that this gentleman found me a burden and that I was going there for nothing. But since such thoughts always ended, as if on purpose, by egging me on still more to climb into an awkward situation, I went in. It was almost a year since I had last seen Simonov.
