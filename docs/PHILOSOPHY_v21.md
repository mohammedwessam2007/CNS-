# The course philosophy: before, now, and next (17 Nov 2026)

## Before (v18, the course used for the 16 Nov MCQ): 3 / 10

The rules the course followed without saying them:

1. **The bank is the exam.** Teach the facts the past papers asked; measure on more past-paper items.
2. **More features means more learning.** Visuals, games, 3D, reading sessions.
3. **Explain right after every answer.** Fast feedback, same or twin item back later.
4. **Give a grade.** Turn a number into "very good".

What it got right: spacing, retrieval on past papers, visual-first, the app deciding the next step.
Why it failed: it optimised a proxy (the bank) and then believed the proxy. The forecast said "very good"; the exam gave about half.

## Now (v21): 7 / 10

1. **The exam is the target, not the bank.** The bank number is labelled as a bank number; the real exam's gap is shown; the post-mortem sorts misses into in-the-bank / new angle / lecture-only.
2. **You think first, the AI second.** Attempt before hint, hint before answer, interpret the finding before the AI does. The AI does the jobs that cost time without teaching (sorting, scheduling, grading, transcribing); it never does the thinking the exam asks.
3. **Every mistake is an asset.** A miss becomes a card that returns re-worded on a spaced schedule; a sure-but-wrong answer is the most valuable moment.
4. **Honesty over comfort.** Ranges, not grades; "not in your notes" said aloud; three examiners and the spread, not one mark.
5. **Zero decisions, a clear stop.** ▶ Today chooses; a short day counts; "done for today" is part of the plan. Work less, live more.
6. **Marks per minute.** The high-yield part first; mnemonics only for arbitrary lists; drawing for structures; cases for clinical MCQs.
7. **Ground in your lectures.** Your slides and notes are the first source.

Why not higher: points 1 and 7 are only half real until the slides arrive and readiness is measured on questions you have never seen, answered with the AI off.

## Next: what would make it 9 / 10 (brainstorm, ranked by expected marks per hour of building)

1. **Lecture vs bank diff.** When the slides arrive: list every slide fact no past paper ever asked. That is exactly where the 16 Nov marks went; it becomes its own short daily stream.
2. **Readiness you cannot fool.** New questions from the slides, checked by the second model, answered with the AI off, never shown twice; readiness is that score as a range.
3. **Examiner modelling.** From the past papers, each department's habits: favourite topics, "all except", clinical stems, the traps they reuse. Practice is weighted to what *these* examiners ask.
4. **Certainty-based marking.** Every answer carries "how sure", scored so that a confident wrong answer costs more than a guess (a method used in UK medical schools). Trains calibration, which is what failed on 16 Nov.
5. **Error types, not just errors.** Each miss labelled: didn't know / knew but misread the stem / confused look-alikes / changed a right answer. Each type has its own fix (reading drill, Tell them apart, trust your first answer).
6. **Forgetting forecast.** For each exam date: which facts will have faded by then if nothing changes, and the cheapest reviews that save them.
7. **Exam-day protocol.** Rehearsed in mocks: minutes per question, skip and return, underline "except", when to change an answer.
8. **One-page sheets you make yourself.** Per topic, from a blank page, graded by the three examiners; the final week is spent on your own sheets, not new material.
9. **Oral viva.** The professor examines like the practical/oral: one structure, one function, one lesion, follow-up "why?".
10. **Protected life.** A hard stop time, a day off a week, sleep before the exam not cramming: memory consolidates while you rest.

Not worth building (weak evidence or poor marks per hour): fixed Pomodoro, voice tutor, more 3D, streak pressure, gamification beyond a small progress bar.
